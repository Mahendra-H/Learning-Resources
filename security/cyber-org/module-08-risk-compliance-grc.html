Opening Story

The auditor's question nobody could answer.

Prove It.
FIELD ACCOUNT · FORMER CISO

The regulatory examiner had been in our building for three days.

She was from the financial services regulator. Her job was to assess whether our security program met regulatory requirements.

On day two she asked one question that I have never forgotten.

"Show me evidence that every employee with access to customer financial data has had that access reviewed and certified by their manager in the past 90 days."

I had a security team of 80 people. We had millions of dollars of security technology. We had a mature SOC, a Red Team, a Threat Intelligence function.

And I could not answer her question.

Not because access reviews were not happening. But because we had no systematic process, no documentation, no evidence trail, and no way to prove what we believed to be true.

⚠ We received a formal regulatory finding.

The remediation program we built afterward — a full IGA access certification program — was the direct result of not being able to answer one question from one auditor.

That finding cost us more in remediation and regulatory attention than any security technology we purchased that year.

GRC exists so you can always answer the auditor's question.

The most expensive security gap is not a technical vulnerability.
It is the gap between what you believe your security program does
and what you can actually prove it does.

CISO
FORMER CISO · ON GRC

Most engineers I work with see GRC as an administrative overhead — the team that produces policies nobody reads and asks for evidence packages before audits.

Let me reframe that completely.

GRC is the team that decides what the security program MUST do.

Not what it should do. Not what it aims to do. What it legally and regulatorily must do — or what the consequences of not doing it will be.

When a compliance framework says "implement privileged access management with session recording," the GRC team translates that into a control requirement that the IAM team must meet.

The GRC team is why the IAM program gets funded. Because non-compliance has a known, quantifiable cost. And that cost is always higher than the IAM program.

CISO
FORMER CISO · ON THE RISK REGISTER

Look at row one. IAM-001. Privileged account compromise, score 9.2 out of 10.

This is not a coincidence. In nearly every enterprise risk register I have reviewed, privileged account compromise sits at or near the top. The probability is real, the impact is catastrophic — ransomware deployment, data exfiltration, regulatory breach notification — and the attack surface is wide.

This is why the IAM team gets funded. The risk register is read by the CISO, the Board, and the Audit Committee. When IAM-001 has a score of 9.2 and the status is MITIGATING, the next question is always: what is the mitigation? The answer is the PAM program. The IGA certification program. The privileged account inventory.

The risk register is the business case for every identity control you design and operate. Know it. Own the row.

CISO
FORMER CISO · ON RISK APPETITE

Risk appetite is the CEO and Board's answer to: "How much risk are we willing to accept?"

It is not a technical question. It is a business question.

A startup might accept HIGH risk appetite — moving fast matters more than perfect security. A financial institution regulated by multiple regulators might have a LOW risk appetite — any breach could trigger regulatory action, stock movement, and customer attrition simultaneously.

The CISO's job is to understand the organization's risk appetite and design a security program that operates within it.

The IAM team's job is to make sure the identity controls match that appetite. PAM controls for a financial institution look very different from PAM controls for a Series A startup. Same technology. Completely different scope, coverage, and rigor — because the risk appetite is different.

CISO
FORMER CISO · ON IAM EVIDENCE

Let me show you something specific about how compliance frameworks connect to IAM.

Every major framework requires IAM evidence. Not recommends. Requires.

SOC 2: access control testing requires evidence that access certifications happened on schedule. PCI-DSS: quarterly user access reviews are mandatory — evidence must show who reviewed what, when, and what actions were taken. HIPAA: audit logs of all access to protected health information must be maintained and available. ISO 27001: access management is a required control domain — certification requires evidence of implementation.

When an auditor arrives, they ask for: user access lists, role assignments, certification records, privileged account inventories, PAM session logs, provisioning and deprovisioning evidence.

Every one of these is an IAM artifact.

The IGA and PAM platforms the IAM Architect designs and operates produce this evidence automatically. Without a mature IAM program, you cannot pass a serious compliance audit. Without a compliance requirement, many organizations would not fund a serious IAM program.

GRC and IAM are codependent.

INSIGHT FOR IAM ARCHITECTS

For IAM Architects, compliance is not a constraint. It is a business case.

When you need to justify a $2M IGA program, the compliance argument is often the most powerful: Without it, here is the regulatory finding we received. Here is what it cost. Here is what it will cost again.

Section 06

Third Party Risk Management.

Your vendors are your attack surface.

CISO
FORMER CISO · A BREACH THAT STARTED WITH AN HVAC VENDOR

In 2013, one of the largest retail data breaches in history did not begin with a sophisticated attacker targeting the company directly.

It began with an HVAC vendor. A small company hired to manage heating and cooling systems, with network credentials to a business portal. Those credentials were stolen. The attacker used them to enter the corporate network. From there, they moved laterally to the payment systems and exfiltrated tens of millions of payment card records.

The security controls at the perimeter were not the failure. The identity of the vendor — inadequately secured, over-privileged, and unmonitored — was the failure.

Third party risk is identity risk. Every vendor with network access has an identity in your environment. That identity is an attack vector.

FORMER CISO · VENDOR ACCESS AND IAM

Every vendor with network access has an identity in your environment. That identity must be governed with the same rigor as your own employees — or more.

Third-party vendor accounts are among the most over-privileged in any enterprise. They are granted access for a project. The project ends. The access remains. Nobody deprovisions it because nobody owns it.

Third-party PAM is one of the fastest-growing areas of IAM program development. Purpose-built solutions for vendor privileged access — just-in-time access, zero standing privilege for vendors, session recording for all third-party administrative sessions.

An IAM Architect who designs third-party identity governance programs is addressing one of the highest-risk identity vectors in the enterprise. And one of the most underfunded.

Section 07

Data Security.

The foundation that makes access control possible.

CISO
FORMER CISO · ON DATA CLASSIFICATION

Data security is foundational to everything that follows it.

You cannot implement least privilege without knowing what data is sensitive. If you do not know that a particular database contains PII subject to GDPR, you cannot design appropriate access controls for it. If you do not know that a file share contains regulated financial data, you cannot set appropriate certification frequencies for the people who access it.

Data classification is the input. IAM is the control mechanism. GRC audit is the proof that the mechanism worked.

When a regulator asks "who has access to your most sensitive customer data?", the answer requires all three functions operating together.

Section 08

GRC makes IAM mandatory.
IAM makes GRC provable.

Four dependency relationships that define the enterprise security ecosystem.

RISK MANAGEMENT
→
Risk registers almost always list privileged account compromise as a top-tier risk. IAM programs directly reduce the probability and impact of this risk. Risk Management provides the business case. IAM provides the mitigation. The risk score on IAM-001 is the budget conversation.
COMPLIANCE
→
Every major compliance framework creates mandatory IAM control requirements. Access certifications. Privileged access management. Unique user IDs. Audit logging. IAM teams produce the evidence that compliance programs need to demonstrate controls are operating effectively.
THIRD PARTY RISK
→
Vendor access governance is an IAM capability. TPRM programs identify which vendors need identity controls. IAM programs implement them. GRC audits verify they are working. The three functions form a closed loop around one of the enterprise's highest-risk attack vectors.
AUDIT
→
Internal and external auditors test IAM controls directly. Access review completion rates, provisioning accuracy, PAM coverage metrics — all IAM data consumed directly by audit teams. The IAM program that cannot produce this data receives findings. The one that can produces evidence that the entire security program is working.
