1.1

🧩 AI vs ML vs Deep Learning vs Generative AI

What is it?

Let's start with the word that started it all: "AI." It's become almost meaningless through overuse — vendors slap it on everything from a spam filter to a chatbot to a smart thermostat. But underneath the marketing fog, there's a real, useful structure: AI, Machine Learning (ML), Deep Learning (DL), and Generative AI (GenAI) aren't four competing technologies. They're four nested categories, like a set of Russian nesting dolls — each one is a specific approach to achieving the one surrounding it. Artificial Intelligence is the broadest goal: build systems that do things we'd normally say require human-like intelligence. Machine Learning is one major approach to that goal: instead of hand-coding every rule, you let the system learn patterns from data. Deep Learning is a specific, extremely successful family of ML techniques based on layered neural networks. Generative AI is what happens when you point deep learning at the task of creating new content — text, code, images, audio — rather than just classifying or scoring things. The LLMs this entire series is about (Claude, GPT, Gemini, Llama, and friends) live in that innermost ring.

🪆 Each ring is a subset of the ring around it — like nesting dolls

Why does it exist?

This nesting reflects roughly seventy years of history, and knowing the shape of that history helps a lot when someone references "AI winters" or asks why this latest wave feels different. In the 1950s through 1980s, "AI" mostly meant rule-based systems: engineers and domain experts hand-wrote enormous trees of "if this, then that" logic. You've almost certainly worked with the modern descendants of this approach — legacy policy engines, complex access-control rule sets, and decision tables that nobody fully understands anymore because they've accumulated for fifteen years. These systems can be powerful, but they're brittle: every new case requires a human to notice it, understand it, and write a new rule.

Machine learning, which took off through the 1990s and 2000s, flipped the model: instead of writing the rules, you collect labeled examples ("this login pattern was fraud," "this one wasn't") and let an algorithm find the patterns itself. This is the machinery behind decades of fraud detection, spam filtering, and recommendation systems — the "AI" most enterprises have quietly run for years without calling it that.

Deep learning, which became dominant from around 2012 onward, is a particular ML technique — neural networks with many stacked layers — that turned out to scale unusually well: the more data and compute you threw at it, the better it kept getting, in a way that older ML techniques mostly didn't. This scaling property is the entire reason the current AI boom looks the way it does — bigger models, bigger data centers, bigger investments, because "bigger keeps working" turned out to be roughly true for over a decade.

Finally, generative AI — the newest and most attention-grabbing ring — emerged when researchers pointed deep learning at generation rather than classification. Older deep learning told you "this image contains a cat" (a label). Generative AI produces "here is a new image of a cat" or "here is a paragraph explaining how cats differ from dogs" (new content). The Transformer architecture (2017) was the breakthrough that made generative text models practical at scale — and from 2022 onward (ChatGPT's launch), generative AI went from "interesting research" to "the thing every enterprise has a task force about" almost overnight. 🚀

🔵 Analogy — Your IAM Stack, As a Set of Nesting Dolls
Picture explaining "Identity & Access Management" to someone unfamiliar with it. You might say: "Security" is the broad goal — keeping the wrong people out and the right people productive. "IAM" is one major approach within security — managing things through identities rather than, say, purely network-based controls. "Federated identity" is a specific IAM technique — using tokens and trust relationships rather than, say, shared credentials stored everywhere. And "SSO with SAML/OIDC, backed by Entra ID" is the concrete, generative-feeling thing your end users actually experience — click a button, get logged into twelve apps. Each layer is a specific implementation choice within the layer above it. When a VP says "we need an AI strategy," it's exactly as underspecified as "we need a security strategy" — and your instinct to immediately ask "which layer, which approach, for which problem?" is precisely the right instinct here too. 🪆
🟣 Analogy — "AI" Is to These Terms What "Cloud" Was to AWS/SaaS/Containers
Rewind to roughly 2014. Someone says "we're moving to the cloud." That sentence could mean: lifting-and-shifting VMs to EC2, adopting a SaaS replacement for an on-prem tool, containerizing an app with Kubernetes, or going fully serverless — four genuinely different projects with different costs, timelines, and skills required, all described by one fashionable word. "AI" today has exactly that problem, times ten. Someone saying "let's add AI to the product" could mean adding a recommendation algorithm (classic ML, weeks of work), adding an image classifier (deep learning, moderate complexity), or adding a conversational assistant (generative AI, an entirely different category of architecture, cost, and risk). The fix is the same one that eventually worked for "cloud": insist on specificity. "Which ring of the diagram, for which problem?" 🎯

When should I care?

Constantly — but especially in three recurring situations. First, vendor conversations: when a vendor's pitch deck says "powered by AI," that phrase alone tells you nothing about risk, explainability, or cost; you need to find out which ring they're actually in. Second, internal prioritization: "AI initiatives" across a company often span all four rings simultaneously, and bundling them into one budget line or one governance process (more on this in Playbook 4) tends to produce either over-control of low-risk classic ML or under-control of high-risk generative AI. Third, your own mental filing system: as you read Playbooks 2-5, almost everything will be about the innermost ring (Generative AI / LLMs) — but you'll regularly need to explain to colleagues why a 10-year-old fraud model and a brand-new chatbot, both labeled "AI" by your company's inventory, need completely different conversations.

🏢 ENTERPRISE USE CASE The Vendor Risk Review With Three "AI Systems"

Your security team is reviewing three systems flagged in a vendor risk assessment, all labeled "AI-powered":

System 1 is a ten-year-old anomaly-detection engine watching VPN login patterns — under the hood, it's a decision-tree ensemble (classic ML, not even deep learning). It's fully deterministic given its inputs, its false-positive rate has been measured for years, and a security analyst can explain exactly why any given alert fired by tracing the decision path.

System 2 is an image-classification model that reads scanned government IDs during employee onboarding to verify document authenticity — deep learning, but not generative. It outputs a confidence score, not new content. Its risk profile is about accuracy and bias (does it work equally well across different document types and demographics?) — a measurable, bounded question.

System 3 is brand new: an LLM-based assistant that drafts access-request justifications for employees to submit to managers. This is generative AI — it can produce different text for the same input on different runs, it can be influenced by how a request is phrased (prompt injection risk, Playbook 4), and its "failure mode" isn't a wrong classification but a plausible-sounding fabrication (hallucination, Module 1.7).

The takeaway: all three are "AI" on a vendor spreadsheet. But System 1 needs almost no new governance beyond what already exists for any automated decision system. System 2 needs a bias/accuracy review. System 3 needs the full Playbook 4 treatment — and conflating the three, in either direction, either wastes review time on low-risk systems or under-reviews the one that actually needs it. 🎯

How is it used in real life?

Most enterprises have been running "AI" — meaning classical ML and non-generative deep learning — for years, often without much fanfare: fraud scoring, demand forecasting, predictive autoscaling, churn prediction, document classification. These systems tend to be narrow (one model, one job), deterministic-ish (same input reliably gives the same or very similar output), and their risks are well-understood within most risk frameworks. What's new since 2022 is the explosion of general-purpose generative models — a single LLM that can draft emails, write code, summarize documents, answer policy questions, and hold a conversation, all using the same underlying model with different prompts. That generality is exactly what makes LLMs so useful — and exactly what makes them harder to govern with traditional "one model, one well-defined job" frameworks. The rest of this series is almost entirely about that innermost ring, because it's the ring that's genuinely new to most enterprise architects. 🆕

What mistakes do people make?

  • 🚩 Treating "AI" as one undifferentiated risk category. Applying an LLM-appropriate governance checklist (prompt injection review, hallucination monitoring, output audit logging) to a decade-old fraud model is overkill that burns review cycles and erodes goodwill for when it's genuinely needed. The reverse — treating a new LLM-based agent with the same light-touch review as an old classification model — is the more dangerous mistake, and the one Playbook 4 spends the most time on.
  • 🚩 Assuming "deep learning" automatically means "generative" or "risky in the LLM sense." A huge amount of deep learning in production today is still classification or scoring — detecting anomalous logins, flagging suspicious transactions, routing support tickets. These systems don't hallucinate in the way LLMs do, because they're not generating free-form content; their failure mode is "wrong classification," not "fabricated fact."
  • 🚩 Assuming older ML is automatically "safer" by comparison. Bias, fairness, and explainability issues are real in classical ML too — they're just a different kind of risk (statistical bias baked into training data) than the risks unique to generative AI (a model fabricating a confident-sounding but false answer). "Older" doesn't mean "lower risk," just "differently risky" — and Playbook 4 covers governance considerations for both.

How should an architect think about it?

🟢 Architect's Take
Whenever "AI" appears in a proposal, a budget line, or a risk register, your first move should be to ask which ring of this diagram the actual system sits in — and don't accept "generative AI" as an answer if what's really being built is a classification model with a chat UI bolted on (this happens more often than you'd think — "AI-powered" is sometimes just marketing for "we added a search box"). The ring tells you: what data the system touches, whether its outputs are deterministic or probabilistic, what kind of explainability you can reasonably demand, and — critically for Playbook 4 — which governance playbook sections actually apply. Generative AI is the newest, least predictable, and most architecturally demanding ring covered in this series — but it's also frequently a much smaller slice of a given system than the marketing suggests. Separating "the LLM part" from "the conventional software part" of any AI proposal is often the single most useful first step in a review. 🔍
🟣 Deep dive: Why is it called a "neural network" if there's no brain involved?

The terminology comes from 1940s-50s neuroscience-inspired research. Early researchers modeled an artificial "neuron" as a simple mathematical unit: it takes several weighted inputs, sums them, and — if the sum crosses a threshold — passes a signal onward, loosely mimicking how a biological neuron "fires." Stack millions of these simple units across many layers, with the weights adjusted through training, and the resulting network can approximate extraordinarily complex functions — including "given this sequence of tokens, what's the most statistically likely next token," which is the core operation inside every LLM. There's no biological brain, no consciousness, and no understanding in the human sense involved at any point — it's matrix multiplication, many billions of times per second, on specialized hardware (Playbook 5 covers exactly why that hardware looks the way it does). The "neural" framing persists mostly for historical reasons and because, frankly, "a really big pile of weighted sums and thresholds" doesn't make for a great conference talk title. 🧠➡️🔢

List three systems at your company that someone has called "AI" in the last year (a chatbot pilot, a fraud tool, an autocomplete feature, whatever comes to mind). For each one, try to place it in the right ring of the diagram above. Notice: did any of them get the same level of scrutiny in a review, despite being in different rings? That mismatch — over- or under-scrutiny relative to the ring — is exactly the kind of thing this series will train you to spot instantly. 🔎
1.2

🔢 LLMs, Tokens & Parameters

What is it?

At its absolute core — strip away every product feature, every chat interface, every "agent" capability — an LLM is a next-token predictor. You give it a sequence of tokens (small chunks of text, roughly ¾ of an English word on average — a common word might be one token, while "tokenization" itself might split into "token" + "ization"), and it computes a probability distribution over every possible "next token" — essentially a giant ranked list of "here's how likely each possible next chunk of text is, given everything so far." It samples one token from that distribution, appends it to the sequence, and repeats. That's it. That single mechanism, applied a few hundred or thousand times in a row, is what produces a coherent paragraph, a working code snippet, or a step-by-step explanation. Everything else — "chat," "agents," "reasoning" — is this same mechanism wrapped in increasingly sophisticated scaffolding.

Parameters are the internal numeric values — think of them as an enormous number of dials — that the model learned during training, and which determine exactly how it computes that next-token probability distribution. A "70-billion-parameter model" has roughly 70 billion such values. Modern frontier models have parameter counts in the hundreds of billions to (rumored, since most labs don't publish exact figures for their largest models) over a trillion. More parameters generally — though not always — correlates with more capacity to capture subtle patterns, at the direct cost of more compute needed to run the model (which is a central theme of Playbook 5).

🔵 Analogy — Your Phone's Autocomplete, On an Almost Incomprehensible Scale
You already have deep, intuitive experience with next-token prediction — it's the autocomplete on your phone's keyboard. Type "let's grab " and your phone suggests "lunch," "coffee," or "dinner" based on your texting history and common patterns. That's a tiny model predicting the next word from a short recent context. An LLM does the conceptually identical thing — predict the next chunk of text — but with two enormous differences: it considers the entire conversation as context (not just the last couple of words), and its sense of "what's likely" was shaped by training on a meaningful fraction of all digitized human writing — books, code repositories, technical documentation, forums, articles. The jump from "predicts your next word based on your texting habits" to "predicts the next chunk of a technical explanation based on having effectively read most of the internet" is the entire story of why this technology suddenly feels intelligent. The mechanism barely changed; the scale changed by roughly nine orders of magnitude. 📱➡️🌌
🟠 Analogy — Parameters as an Almost Inconceivably Large Mixing Board
Picture a recording studio mixing board — but instead of dozens of sliders for volume, EQ, and effects, imagine one with 70 billion sliders. During training, the model is fed enormous numbers of examples of "input → desired output," and an algorithm called backpropagation nudges every relevant slider very slightly after each example, repeated trillions of times, until the overall board configuration reliably produces good outputs across the training data. Inference — actually using the model to answer your question — means the sliders are now frozen in place; your prompt goes in, and the fixed slider configuration determines what comes out. Crucially, there's no "lookup table" or "database of facts" anywhere in this picture — what we loosely call the model's "knowledge" is entirely encoded in the positions of those 70 billion sliders, in a form that's not human-readable or directly editable. This is why "fine-tuning" (Module 1.5) and "fixing a wrong fact the model knows" are such different operations from editing a row in a database — you're nudging sliders, not updating records. 🎛️

Why does it exist?

The breakthrough that made all of this practical was the Transformer architecture, introduced in a famous 2017 paper memorably titled "Attention Is All You Need." Before Transformers, the dominant approach to language models processed text strictly left-to-right, one token at a time, in sequence — which meant training was inherently slow and hard to parallelize across hardware, because step 50 depended on step 49 depending on step 48, and so on. Transformers introduced a mechanism called attention, which lets every token in a sequence directly "look at" every other token simultaneously to figure out relationships — "this pronoun refers to that noun," "this closing bracket matches that opening one twelve tokens back." Because this can be computed in parallel rather than strictly sequentially, it maps extremely well onto GPU hardware (which excels at doing huge numbers of similar calculations simultaneously — more in Playbook 5). This parallelism is the unglamorous but absolutely central reason it became feasible to train models on internet-scale text in reasonable amounts of time.

When should I care about tokens specifically?

Because tokens are the universal unit of cost, capacity, and throughput across literally every LLM product you'll ever evaluate. API pricing is quoted per million tokens. Context windows — how much a model can "see" at once — are measured in tokens. Rate limits are measured in tokens per minute. Even latency is roughly proportional to token counts (more output tokens to generate = more time). If someone hasn't internalized "think in tokens," they literally cannot reason about why a feature is slow, why a bill is higher than expected, or why a request got cut off mid-response. It's the same foundational unit that "requests per second" or "rows scanned" is for API and database performance conversations — you don't need to calculate it precisely in your head, but you need to know it's the thing being calculated.

Common English words ≈ 1 token each; technical vocab, code, and identifiers often cost more
🏢 ENTERPRISE USE CASE Why the Helpdesk Bot Pilot's Bill Tripled 📈

A pilot internal helpdesk assistant was budgeted at roughly $200/month, based on an estimate of "short questions, maybe 50 tokens in, 100 tokens out, a few hundred queries a day." The pilot launched, and within two weeks finance flagged that the bill had roughly tripled from the projection.

The investigation found the gap immediately: real users weren't asking short questions. They were pasting entire error logs, multi-paragraph email threads, and screenshots-converted-to-text from failed login attempts, then asking "what does this mean?" A 500-line application log can easily run 8,000-15,000 tokens — roughly 80-150x the originally estimated input size. And because the model was (reasonably) configured to be thorough in its explanations, output lengths were also 3-4x the estimate.

The fix wasn't "use a cheaper model" — it was twofold: (1) add a pre-processing step that truncates or summarizes pasted logs to the relevant portion before they hit the LLM (itself sometimes a smaller, cheaper model's job — see Module 1.8 on SLMs), and (2) set explicit, tighter output-length targets in the system prompt. This is precisely the kind of "estimate from realistic usage patterns, not best-case ones" thinking that Playbook 5's cost modeling section builds into a repeatable process. The lesson: token estimates based on how you'd phrase a question are almost always wrong — real users paste everything. 📋💸

What mistakes do people make?

  • 🚩 Assuming "1 word ≈ 1 token" universally. It's a reasonable rough estimate for plain English prose (~0.75 words per token on average), but technical vocabulary, code, identifiers, non-English text, and anything with unusual capitalization or punctuation routinely uses noticeably more tokens per word — often 1.5-3x more.
  • 🚩 Equating parameter count with "better." A larger model isn't automatically the right choice for a given task — a smaller, well-suited model can match or beat a larger general-purpose one on a narrow job, at a fraction of the cost and latency. This is the entire premise of Module 1.8 (SLMs) and resurfaces throughout Playbook 2's evaluation framework.
  • 🚩 Forgetting that output tokens usually cost significantly more than input tokens — often 4-5x as much. A model configured (or prompted) to "explain your reasoning in detail" generates far more output tokens than one prompted to "answer concisely," and the cost difference compounds across every single request.

How should an architect think about it?

🟢 Architect's Take
Treat tokens the way you'd treat any metered, rate-limited resource in a third-party integration — like API calls against a payment gateway or rows scanned in a pay-per-query data warehouse. Every design review for an LLM-backed feature should include a rough token budget per request: expected input size (including system prompt, any retrieved context, conversation history), expected output size, and what happens at the tails (the user who pastes a 50-page PDF). Just as you wouldn't ship a payment integration without modeling API call volume under realistic — not best-case — load, don't ship an LLM feature without modeling token volume the same way. 📊
🟠 Economics preview
As of this writing, a typical mid-tier frontier model prices roughly in the range of $2-3 per million input tokens and $10-15 per million output tokens — meaning output tokens often cost 4-5x input tokens. We'll build full, scenario-based cost models (100 / 1,000 / 10,000 users) in Playbook 5 — but if you remember one number from this whole module, make it that ratio. 💰
✅ Quick Lab — 10 minutes
Take a real message you've sent in Slack or email recently — ideally one with some technical content (a log snippet, an error message, a config block). Paste it into any LLM chat interface and ask it "roughly how many tokens is this input?" Compare that number to a simple word count. How far off was "1 word ≈ 1 token" for your specific kind of writing? This 10-minute exercise will recalibrate your intuition more than any amount of reading about it. 🔬
1.3

🪟 Context Windows & Memory

What is it?

The context window is the maximum number of tokens an LLM can "see" — or more precisely, process — in a single request. This is a combined budget covering everything: the system prompt (instructions the application gives the model, usually invisible to the end user), the entire visible conversation history, any documents, retrieved snippets, or tool definitions included, and the response the model is currently generating. Exceed it, and something has to give — typically the oldest content gets dropped (truncated), or in some implementations the request simply fails.

Here's the detail that trips up almost everyone the first time: an LLM, by itself, has no memory between separate API calls. Each call is completely stateless — the model receives a block of text (the context), processes it, and returns a response, with no persistent state carried over afterward. What feels like "having a conversation" with a chatbot is, under the hood, the application resending the entire conversation history so far with every new message, every single time. The model isn't "remembering" your earlier messages in any ongoing sense — it's being handed a fresh transcript that happens to include them, every time.

🔵 Analogy — The Brilliant Consultant With Hourly Amnesia
Imagine your company hires an outside consultant who is, without exaggeration, one of the most well-read people on the planet — they've absorbed something close to a meaningful fraction of all published writing in your industry. There's one catch: every single time you start a meeting with them, they have zero memory of any previous meeting. None. To make this workable, before every meeting you hand them a fresh folder containing: a cover sheet with standing instructions ("you are our IAM advisor, be concise, never discuss competitor pricing"), a full transcript of every prior conversation you want them to be aware of, and any new documents relevant to today's topic. The consultant reads the entire folder — every time — and then responds based on everything in it. Now imagine that folder has a maximum size. If it gets too thick, pages start falling out the back — and crucially, the consultant has no idea those pages ever existed; from their perspective, the folder simply never contained them. That's a context window. The "conversation" you're having is really a ritual of reconstructing that folder, slightly larger, before every single exchange. 📁🧠💨
🟣 Analogy — Stateless HTTP Requests and Session Replay
If you've worked with stateless services and session tokens, here's the more technical framing: the context window is conceptually like an in-memory request scope that gets completely rebuilt from a log on every single request — there's no server-side session object persisting between calls. Each "turn" of a conversation is a fresh request that includes a serialized replay of everything relevant so far. The "memory systems" covered later in Playbook 3 (Module 3.5) are the architectural equivalent of introducing a real session store or database — instead of replaying the full raw log every time (which gets expensive and eventually overflows), you persist a summary or extracted facts somewhere, and inject just that summary into future context-window "folders." Conceptually identical to moving from "replay the full event log" to "maintain a materialized view" in a data pipeline. 🔁

Why does it exist as a limit?

Recall from Module 1.2 that the Transformer's "attention" mechanism lets every token look at every other token. That's powerful, but it has a cost: the amount of computation needed grows faster than linearly as the context gets longer — roughly speaking, doubling the context more than doubles the compute required to process it (the relationship is often described as quadratic in naive implementations, though modern techniques soften this somewhat). Longer context windows are absolutely possible — and have grown dramatically, from a few thousand tokens in early models to hundreds of thousands or even millions today — but they come with real compute and latency costs that get passed through as pricing and product differentiation. A model with a 1-million-token context window isn't "free" compared to one with 128K; you're often paying more, and waiting longer, even if you don't use the extra capacity.

🏢 ENTERPRISE USE CASE "It Forgot We Already Ruled That Out" 🔁

A platform engineer files feedback about an internal AI assistant: "I spent 40 minutes debugging our LDAP integration with this thing — pasted configs, logs, the works. Early on, we figured out together that it wasn't a certificate issue. Forty minutes later, completely out of nowhere, it suggested checking the certificates again, like we'd never discussed it. Is it just... broken?"

It's not broken — it's a capacity problem, and a very predictable one once you understand context windows. Over 40 minutes of back-and-forth with pasted configs and multi-hundred-line logs, the conversation's total token count grew well past the model's context window. The application's truncation strategy — likely "drop the oldest messages first" — eventually dropped the exact exchange where the certificate issue was ruled out. From the model's perspective, that exchange never happened; it's working from a "folder" (per the consultant analogy) that no longer contains those pages.

The architectural parallel: this is functionally identical to a load balancer or cache evicting the oldest entries under memory pressure, and a downstream service behaving as if that data never existed. The fix isn't "get a smarter model" — it's "implement a smarter eviction/compaction strategy," exactly as you would for a cache. Playbook 3's Memory Systems module covers patterns like rolling summarization, where older parts of a conversation get compressed into a dense summary before they'd otherwise be dropped — preserving the gist (including "we ruled out certificates") even after the raw transcript is gone. 🗜️

What mistakes do people make?

  • 🚩 Confusing "context window" with "memory." A 200K-token context window does not mean the assistant "remembers you" from yesterday — it means one session can be very long. Cross-session memory is a separate architectural feature (Playbook 3), not a property of the model itself.
  • 🚩 Assuming "bigger context window = strictly better." Beyond raw capacity, there's a well-documented effect sometimes called "lost in the middle" — information placed at the very start or very end of a long prompt tends to be recalled and weighted more reliably than information buried in the center, even when everything technically fits. More context isn't free accuracy; it can even dilute attention on the parts that matter most.
  • 🚩 Not accounting for "invisible" context consumers. The system prompt, tool/function definitions (for agents, Module 1.6), and any RAG-retrieved documents all silently consume the same shared budget as the visible conversation — a feature that "works fine in testing" with a short system prompt can behave very differently once a production system prompt with twenty tool definitions is added.

How should an architect think about it?

🟢 Architect's Take
Design every LLM-backed feature as if the context window is a hard, shared, and entirely visible-to-you budget — because it is. Sketch the budget explicitly during design: system prompt (X tokens) + tool definitions (Y tokens) + retrieved documents (Z tokens) + conversation history (variable, growing) + reserved space for the response (W tokens) ≤ context window. For any feature involving extended sessions — a 45-minute IAM troubleshooting conversation, a long code-review session — build in an explicit summarization or compaction strategy during design, not after the first "it forgot what we discussed" bug report lands in production. This is capacity planning, full stop — the same discipline you'd apply to a session store or a message queue's retention policy. 📐
🟣 Deep dive: "Lost in the middle" and why document order matters in RAG

Multiple independent studies on long-context model behavior have found a consistent pattern: when relevant information is placed near the beginning or end of a long prompt, models recall and use it reliably; when the same information is placed in the middle of a very long prompt, recall accuracy measurably drops — even though the information is technically "within" the context window and the model could, in principle, attend to it equally. The exact causes are still actively researched, but the practical implication is significant for RAG systems (Playbook 3, Module 3.2): the order in which retrieved document chunks are inserted into a prompt isn't just cosmetic — placing the most relevant retrieved chunk closest to the actual question (typically at the end, right before "Now answer: ...") often measurably improves answer quality compared to placing it first among several chunks, even with identical content overall. This is one of those details that seems like a minor implementation footnote until your RAG system's accuracy mysteriously improves by reordering nothing but where things sit in the prompt. 📑

Open a long-running chat conversation you've had with any AI assistant (ideally one that went on for 30+ messages or involved pasting large blocks of text). Scroll back to the very beginning and re-read your first few messages. Then ask the assistant, in the same conversation, a question that depends on something from those early messages. Does it still "remember"? If the conversation is long enough, you may catch it in exactly the "forgot the certificate thing" pattern from the use case above — and now you'll know why. 🕵️
1.4

🏋️ Training vs. Inference

What is it?

These two words get used loosely all the time — "training the model," "running inference" — and the distinction between them matters enormously, because they're economically and operationally almost unrelated activities that happen to involve the same underlying neural network.

Training is the process of creating the model in the first place — feeding it enormous amounts of data and repeatedly adjusting its billions of parameters (those "mixing board sliders" from Module 1.2) until it gets good at next-token prediction across that data. This happens once (well — once per model version) and is done by the foundation model provider (Zone 1 from the Landscape Map) on enormous clusters of specialized hardware, over a period of weeks to months, at a cost that can run into the tens or hundreds of millions of dollars for a frontier model.

Inference is everything that happens afterward — every single time anyone sends a prompt to that already-trained model and gets a response back. The sliders are frozen; inference is just running the fixed computation forward. Every API call you make, every chat message you send, every automated agent step — that's all inference. It happens millions or billions of times per day across a popular model's user base, each instance individually cheap, but the costs add up continuously, forever, for as long as the model is in use.

Same model, two completely different cost & ops profiles
🔵 Analogy — Building a Search Engine vs. Running a Search Query
Think about the difference between building a search engine's index — crawling billions of pages, processing them, constructing the index data structures, a massive one-time (well, periodically-repeated) batch operation involving enormous infrastructure — versus running a single search query against that already-built index, which takes milliseconds and happens billions of times a day. Nobody thinks "searching Google" is expensive per-query, even though "building Google's index" is one of the largest computing operations on the planet. Training an LLM is like building the index: extraordinarily expensive, done periodically, by a small number of organizations with the resources to do it. Inference is like running a query: individually cheap, but happening at such volume that it becomes the dominant ongoing cost — which is exactly the dynamic Playbook 5 unpacks when explaining where AI infrastructure spending actually goes. 🔍
🟢 Analogy — Compiling Code vs. Running It
For a more dev-flavored framing: training is like compiling a massive codebase — a slow, resource-intensive, batch process that happens on a build server, producing an artifact. Inference is like running the compiled binary — fast, lightweight per invocation, happens constantly in production. You wouldn't say "our app is slow because compilation takes 20 minutes" if compilation happens once a week on a build server — but you'd absolutely care if running the binary was slow, because that happens on every request. The same logic applies to "is AI expensive" — the eye-watering training costs you read about in the news are real, but they're a "build server" cost paid by foundation model providers; your organization's costs are almost entirely on the "running the binary" (inference) side. 🛠️

Why does this distinction matter so much?

Because it directly determines who pays for what, and when. When you use Claude, GPT, or Gemini via API, you are not paying anything toward that model's training cost in any direct sense — that cost was already sunk by the foundation model provider, who recoups it (hopefully) across millions of customers' inference usage over the model's lifetime, plus whatever other business value they derive. Your bill reflects inference only. This is why a brand-new, more expensive-to-train model can sometimes have lower inference pricing than its predecessor — training cost and inference price aren't directly linked the way "cost of goods" and "retail price" are for physical products; inference pricing is set based on inference compute costs and market positioning, largely independent of the (already sunk) training cost.

🏢 ENTERPRISE USE CASE "Are We Paying for the Model to Learn From Our Data?" 🤔

A common, very reasonable question from a security or legal stakeholder evaluating an LLM vendor: "If we send our internal documents to this model via the API, is the model being trained on our data? Will our proprietary information end up influencing what it tells other customers?"

Understanding the training/inference split lets you ask this question precisely — and understand vendor answers precisely. Standard API usage is inference: your data goes in, a response comes out, and (per most major providers' enterprise terms, though always verify the specific contract) it is not used to retrain the underlying model for other customers. This is architecturally different from fine-tuning (Module 1.5), where you might deliberately choose to further train a model on your own data — a separate, explicit, opt-in process with its own data handling terms.

The takeaway for a governance conversation: "does this vendor train on our data" has a very different answer for "standard inference API calls" versus "a fine-tuning service we explicitly opted into" — and conflating the two (in either direction) leads to either unfounded vendor anxiety or, worse, missing a real data-handling distinction that does apply to a fine-tuning arrangement. This becomes especially relevant in Playbook 4's data leakage discussion. 🔐

What mistakes do people make?

  • 🚩 Assuming your usage "teaches" the model in real time. Standard inference doesn't update the model's parameters at all — the model you queried this morning is byte-for-byte identical to the one you'll query this evening (until the provider ships a new version). Any sense that "it's learning from our conversations" is either about a separate, explicit fine-tuning process, or about application-level memory features (Playbook 3) that store and re-inject context — not the model itself changing.
  • 🚩 Conflating training costs with inference costs when estimating your own spend. News headlines about "$100 million to train a model" describe a cost your organization will never directly pay (unless you're training your own frontier model from scratch — extremely rare). Your budget conversation is 100% about inference costs at your usage volume — Playbook 5's entire focus.
  • 🚩 Assuming "the model knows about today's events." Because training happens at a point in time and then stops, every model has a "knowledge cutoff" — a date after which it has no training data. This is a direct, practical consequence of the training/inference split, and it's the reason tools (web search, RAG — Module 1.6) exist to give models access to current information at inference time.

How should an architect think about it?

🟢 Architect's Take
When evaluating any LLM-related cost or capability question, your first move is to sort it into "training" or "inference" — because almost every practical question your organization will actually face lives entirely on the inference side: pricing, latency, rate limits, data handling for standard usage, and capacity planning. The training side becomes relevant mainly in three specific contexts: (1) evaluating whether a model's knowledge cutoff is recent enough for your use case, (2) deciding whether fine-tuning (Module 1.5) makes sense for your scenario, and (3) understanding, at a strategic level, why foundation model providers make the business decisions they do (Playbook 5). For day-to-day architecture work, "inference" is where 95% of your attention belongs. 🎯
✅ Quick Lab — 5 minutes
Ask any LLM assistant: "What is your knowledge cutoff date, and what happened in the news after that date that you wouldn't know about?" Notice how it answers — a well-behaved model will clearly state its cutoff and acknowledge the gap. This single interaction is a live demonstration of the training/inference split: the model is reading today's date from your prompt's context (inference, happening now) while its actual knowledge was frozen at training time (months or years ago). 🗓️
1.5

🔗 Embeddings & Fine-Tuning

What is it?

These are two completely different concepts that get casually conflated because both involve "customizing" how a model relates to your data — but they solve different problems, sit in different layers of the AI Stack, and have very different cost profiles.

An embedding is a way of converting a piece of text (a word, sentence, paragraph, or document) into a long list of numbers — typically hundreds or thousands of numbers, called a "vector" — such that pieces of text with similar meaning end up with similar number-lists, mathematically close to each other in that high-dimensional space. This isn't generative at all — it's a translation from "text" to "a point in meaning-space." Embeddings are the foundation of semantic search: instead of matching exact keywords, you can find documents whose meaning is close to your query's meaning, even if they don't share any of the same words. This is the technology underpinning RAG (Module 1.6) and the entire Vector Database layer of the AI Stack.

Fine-tuning, in contrast, is a process of further training an already-trained model on a smaller, specialized dataset — nudging some of those billions of "mixing board sliders" (Module 1.2) specifically toward your domain, your tone, your task. It's training, just a small, targeted, much cheaper continuation of it, applied to a model that's already broadly capable.

🔵 Analogy — Embeddings Are a Library's Card Catalog, Reimagined
Imagine a librarian who, instead of organizing books alphabetically by title or author, organizes them by meaning — physically placing books on similar topics near each other on the shelves, no matter what they're titled or what language they're written in. A book about "troubleshooting LDAP synchronization failures" ends up shelved right next to one about "diagnosing Active Directory replication issues," even though they share almost no words in common — because their meanings are close. An embedding model is the process that determines shelf position for every document; the "shelf positions" themselves are the numeric vectors. When you later search "why isn't my directory sync working," the system converts your question into a shelf position too, and looks for books shelved nearby — regardless of whether your question's exact words appear in those books at all. This is semantic search, and it's a different (often complementary) approach to traditional keyword search, which only finds exact or fuzzy string matches. 📚🗺️
🟠 Analogy — Fine-Tuning Is Hiring a Generalist Consultant for a Two-Week Embedded Placement
Picture that brilliant, well-read consultant from Module 1.3's analogy. By default, they're an extraordinary generalist — broad knowledge, but no specific knowledge of your company's internal jargon, your specific ticket-categorization conventions, or the exact tone your support team uses. Fine-tuning is like having that consultant spend two intensive weeks embedded with your team, reviewing hundreds of examples of "here's an incoming ticket, here's how our best agents responded" — after which their general knowledge is unchanged, but their instincts for your specific patterns are now much sharper. They'll naturally use your terminology, match your tone, and recognize your common ticket types faster — without you needing to explain it all again every single conversation (which is what you'd otherwise have to do via the system prompt, every single time, at a token cost). The placement is expensive and time-consuming compared to a single conversation — but cheap compared to training a brand new consultant from scratch. 👔

When should I care — and which one, when?

This is genuinely one of the most common points of confusion in enterprise AI conversations, so let's be very direct about the decision: if your goal is "the model should know about our documents/data/policies," the answer is almost always embeddings + RAG (retrieval), not fine-tuning. If your goal is "the model should behave differently — different tone, different format, better at a narrow specialized task," fine-tuning becomes relevant — though often a sufficiently good system prompt (Playbook 3) achieves a similar result more cheaply and flexibly, which is why fine-tuning is reached for less often than newcomers expect.

Most "we want the AI to know our stuff" requests are actually RAG requests 🎯
🏢 ENTERPRISE USE CASE "Can We Fine-Tune It On Our IAM Policy Docs?" 📜

A platform team proposes fine-tuning an LLM on the company's complete IAM policy documentation — hundreds of pages covering access request procedures, role definitions, and compliance requirements — so that "the assistant just knows our policies."

This is the single most common fine-tuning misconception, and it's worth slowing down on. Fine-tuning on a document corpus doesn't give a model reliable, retrievable "knowledge" of that corpus's specific facts the way a database does — it nudges the model's general behavior and style based on patterns in that data, but it's notoriously unreliable for precise factual recall ("what exactly does section 4.2.1 say about contractor access reviews?"). Worse, when policies change — and IAM policies change constantly — a fine-tuned model requires a new fine-tuning run to "update" its knowledge, which is slow, costly, and hard to verify.

The better architecture (covered fully in Playbook 3): embed the policy documents into a vector database, and use RAG to retrieve the specific relevant sections at query time, feeding them directly into the model's context window alongside the user's question. When a policy changes, you re-embed that one document — done in seconds, no retraining. The model can also cite which document it pulled from, something a fine-tuned model fundamentally cannot do (it has no concept of "where" a behavior pattern came from). Fine-tuning remains useful — but for "make the assistant write in our company's specific tone for ticket responses," not "teach it our policy manual." 🎯

What mistakes do people make?

  • 🚩 Reaching for fine-tuning when RAG (or even just a good system prompt) would solve the problem faster, cheaper, and with easier maintenance — by far the most common mistake, as illustrated above.
  • 🚩 Treating embeddings as "understanding." An embedding model doesn't "understand" text in any deep sense — it's a (very useful) mathematical transformation that captures statistical similarity patterns from its own training. Two pieces of text can have very different real-world implications while being embedded close together if they're linguistically similar — semantic search is a powerful tool, not infallible understanding.
  • 🚩 Underestimating fine-tuning's ongoing cost. Fine-tuning isn't "set and forget" — as the underlying base model improves (new versions release regularly), your fine-tuned variant may need to be re-created on the new base to keep pace, and each fine-tuning run has real cost and requires a labeled dataset that itself needs maintenance.

How should an architect think about it?

🟢 Architect's Take
When a request comes in framed as "we need the AI to know about X," your job is to translate "know about" into one of: (a) retrieve it at query time (RAG — almost always the right default for "our documents/policies/data"), (b) include it directly in the system prompt (fine for small, stable, frequently-needed context), or (c) bake it into the model's behavior via fine-tuning (rare, and really about style and task-specialization, not factual knowledge). In practice, the overwhelming majority of enterprise "knowledge" requests are (a). Reserve fine-tuning conversations for cases where you've already tried a strong system prompt plus RAG and are hitting a genuine wall on tone, format consistency, or a narrow repetitive task at very high volume where the per-token savings of a shorter prompt justify the fine-tuning investment. 🧭
🟣 Deep dive: What's LoRA, and why does it matter for fine-tuning costs?

Full fine-tuning — updating all of a model's billions of parameters — is extremely resource-intensive, often requiring similar (though smaller-scale) infrastructure to training itself. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is a popular technique that makes fine-tuning dramatically cheaper by freezing the original model's parameters entirely and instead training a much smaller set of additional "adapter" parameters that get layered on top. Think of it like applying a transparent overlay with targeted edits on top of a printed document, rather than reprinting the entire document — the original stays untouched, and you can even swap different overlays in and out for different tasks using the same base model. This is a major reason fine-tuning has become accessible to far more organizations than it used to be — and it's part of why the SLM/local AI discussion in Module 1.8 is increasingly practical rather than purely theoretical.

✅ Quick Lab — 10 minutes
Think of a real "we want the AI to know about our X" request you've heard at work (or imagine one — "know our org chart," "know our deployment runbooks," "know our customer's account history"). Walk it through the architect's-take framework above: is this really (a) retrieval, (b) system-prompt context, or (c) behavioral fine-tuning? For most people, the honest answer for most requests is (a) — which is exactly why Module 1.6 (next) and Playbook 3 spend so much time on RAG. 🧩
1.6

🧰 RAG, Agents & MCP — First Pass

What is it?

This module introduces three concepts that will become the entire subject matter of Playbook 3 — here, the goal is just to get solid, intuitive first definitions, enough to follow conversations and to set up everything that follows in this playbook (especially Module 1.7 on hallucinations, which RAG directly addresses).

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) is a pattern where, before asking an LLM to answer a question, you first retrieve relevant information from an external source (typically using the embeddings/vector-search approach from Module 1.5) and include that retrieved information directly in the prompt. The model then generates its answer grounded in that retrieved material, rather than relying solely on whatever it absorbed during training (which, remember from Module 1.4, has a knowledge cutoff and no awareness of your private data at all).

A tool (or "function call") is a way for an LLM to say, in effect, "I need to take an action or fetch information outside of my own text-generation — please run this specific function with these specific arguments, and give me the result so I can continue." The model itself doesn't execute anything; it requests an action, and the surrounding application executes it and feeds the result back in.

An agent is an LLM placed inside a loop: observe the current situation → decide what to do (possibly "call a tool") → take that action → observe the result → decide again → repeat, until the task is judged complete. The "intelligence" isn't a new kind of model — it's the same next-token-predicting LLM from Module 1.2, now wrapped in a loop with access to tools and some memory of what's happened so far in the loop.

MCP (Model Context Protocol) is a standardized way of describing and exposing tools to an LLM/agent — think of it as a common "interface contract" so that any MCP-compatible tool (a ServiceNow connector, a database query tool, a file-search tool) can be plugged into any MCP-compatible agent framework, rather than every combination of model and tool needing custom integration code.

Same LLM core (Module 1.2), now wrapped in a loop with access to external actions
🔵 Analogy — RAG Is "Open-Book" vs. "Closed-Book" Exam Mode
Without RAG, asking an LLM a question about your company's specific policies is like asking someone to take a closed-book exam about a company handbook they've never seen — they'll answer confidently, drawing on general patterns of "what company handbooks usually say," and might even sound very plausible, but they have no actual access to your handbook's specific content. RAG converts this into an open-book exam: right before the question is asked, the relevant pages of the actual handbook are placed in front of them, and they're told "answer based on these pages." The model is exactly as smart as before — but now it has the actual source material in front of it for this specific question. This single shift — from closed-book to open-book — is the single biggest practical lever for making LLM outputs grounded rather than merely plausible, and it's why RAG is the default architecture for "AI that knows about our stuff." 📖
🟣 Analogy — Agents and Tools Are Just... an API-Calling Loop With a Reasoning Engine on Top
If you've built any kind of orchestration or automation — a Jenkins pipeline that calls different scripts based on conditions, a ServiceNow workflow that branches based on ticket attributes, a state machine that calls different APIs depending on state — you've built the shape of an agent already. The novelty isn't the loop structure (you know loops); it's that the "decide what to do next" step, which in your existing systems is hardcoded conditional logic (if ticket.category == 'access_request': call servicenow_api()), is now handled by an LLM reading the situation in natural language and deciding dynamically. MCP is just a standard way of describing "here are the APIs/tools available to call" — conceptually similar to an OpenAPI spec or a service catalog, but designed specifically so an LLM can read the available tools' descriptions and decide which one fits the current step. The "magic" is entirely in that decision step; the rest is the same API-orchestration plumbing you've built dozens of times. 🔌

When should I care — and how do these fit together?

A useful mental ordering, from "simplest" to "most complex," which Playbook 3 (Module 3.8, "Agent Hype vs. Reality") will formalize into a full decision framework: a plain prompt is enough when the task needs no external data and no actions — pure generation/transformation of what's already in the conversation. RAG adds external knowledge — the model can now "read" relevant documents it wasn't trained on. Tool calling adds external actions — the model can trigger real operations (create a ticket, query a live system) but typically as a single step or a small fixed sequence. A full agent adds autonomy over a multi-step process — the model decides not just "which tool" but "how many steps, in what order, until the goal is met," which introduces both power and risk (an agent that decides to take 47 steps when 3 would do, or that gets stuck in a loop, or — critically for Playbook 4 — that takes an action it shouldn't have without human approval).

🏢 ENTERPRISE USE CASE The IAM Assistant, Built Up in Layers 🏗️

Let's use the running example from this whole series — an internal IAM assistant — and watch it grow through each layer:

Layer 0 (plain prompt): An employee asks "What's the difference between a role and a permission?" The LLM answers from general training knowledge — no company-specific information needed, a plain prompt is perfectly adequate.

Layer 1 (RAG): An employee asks "What's our process for requesting temporary elevated access to the finance system?" This requires your company's specific policy — the LLM's training data has no idea about your internal process. RAG retrieves the relevant section of your access-request policy document and grounds the answer in it.

Layer 2 (tool calling): An employee asks "What's the status of my access request from yesterday?" This requires live data from ServiceNow — not a document, a real-time system query. A tool call (via MCP) hits the ServiceNow API for ticket status and returns it.

Layer 3 (full agent): An employee says "I need access to the finance reporting system for the Q3 close — can you help me get that set up?" This might require: looking up the correct role for "finance reporting access" (RAG against role documentation), checking the employee's current entitlements (tool call to AD/LDAP), determining if manager approval is required (RAG against policy + tool call to check org hierarchy), and creating the appropriate ServiceNow request (tool call) — a multi-step process where the order and combination of steps depends on what's discovered along the way. This is where a full agent earns its complexity — and where Playbook 4's approval- workflow and audit-logging discussions become essential, because this layer can take actions with real consequences. 🎯

What mistakes do people make?

  • 🚩 Reaching for "agent" when "RAG" or even "plain prompt" would do. Layer 3 sounds the most impressive, but Layer 0 and Layer 1 cover a huge fraction of real requests, with far less complexity, cost, and risk. This exact over-engineering pattern is the subject of Playbook 3's "Agent Hype vs. Reality" module.
  • 🚩 Assuming RAG "fixes" hallucinations completely. RAG dramatically reduces hallucination risk for questions the retrieved documents actually cover — but a model can still misread, misquote, or over-generalize from retrieved text, and will still hallucinate freely about anything the retrieved documents don't cover. RAG narrows the "open-book exam" scope; it doesn't grant perfect reading comprehension. More in Module 1.7.
  • 🚩 Treating MCP as a security boundary by itself. MCP standardizes how tools are described and called — it says nothing, by itself, about whether a given agent should be allowed to call a given tool with a given user's permissions. That's an access-control question layered on top, and it's central to Playbook 4's AI-for-IAM discussion.

How should an architect think about it?

🟢 Architect's Take
When scoping any new "AI assistant" request, run it through the four layers above before committing to an architecture: does this need external knowledge (→ RAG), external actions (→ tools), or autonomous multi-step decision-making (→ agent)? Most organizations' first few AI projects should live comfortably in Layers 0-2 — they're simpler to build, test, secure, and explain to an auditor. Layer 3 (full agents) is where the most interesting capabilities live, but also where Playbook 4's governance machinery — approval workflows, audit logs, blast-radius limits — stops being optional. Starting simple isn't just caution for its own sake; it's how you build the organizational trust and operational muscle needed before taking on Layer 3's real complexity. 🪜
✅ Quick Lab — 10 minutes
Pick a real internal process you know well (an onboarding step, a common support request, an approval workflow). Sketch which "layer" it would need if automated with an LLM: does it need company-specific knowledge (RAG)? Live system data or actions (tools)? A multi-step decision process where the path isn't fixed in advance (agent)? Most people are surprised how often the honest answer is "Layer 1 (RAG) would cover 80% of the value" — which is exactly the intuition Playbook 3 builds on. 🧪
1.7

👻 Hallucinations — Why LLMs Confabulate

What is it?

A hallucination is when an LLM generates output that is factually incorrect or fabricated — but delivered with the same confident, fluent, well-structured prose as a correct answer. The model has no separate truth-checking module — it only has the next-token-prediction mechanism from Module 1.2, which optimizes for "what text is most plausible given what came before," not "what text is most factually accurate." Some researchers prefer the term confabulation — a neurology term for patients who fill memory gaps with invented but sincerely believed content, with no intent to deceive. That's arguably more accurate: the model is filling a knowledge gap with statistically plausible-sounding text, with no internal signal that this is any different from generating a correct response.

🔵 Analogy — The Confident Colleague Who Never Says "I Don't Know"

Imagine a colleague who is extraordinarily articulate and broad in their knowledge — and who absolutely never hesitates or expresses uncertainty. You ask them about a specific internal policy. They answer: "Yes, that's covered in Section 4.7 of the Data Governance Policy, updated June 2023 — the requirement is 18 months." Sounds authoritative. Specific. Citable. You later discover that section covers something completely different, and the 18-month figure doesn't exist anywhere. They weren't lying — they had no internal signal that they were confabulating rather than recalling. From the inside, the experience of "knowing" and "plausibly filling a gap" felt identical. This is structurally what hallucination is. The LLM's confidence and fluency are properties of the generation mechanism — not indicators of accuracy. 🎭

🟠 Analogy — Autocomplete at the Worst Possible Moment

Return to the phone autocomplete framing from Module 1.2. Your phone sometimes inserts a plausible but wrong word — you catch it because the stakes are low and you're re-reading casually. Now imagine that autocomplete was so fluent you didn't catch it — and what it was completing wasn't a casual message but a compliance report, an access-request justification, or a patient dosage note. Hallucinations are exactly that pattern, at the level of facts, citations, and procedures. The danger scales directly with how consequential the domain is and how confidently the output reads. A plausible-but-wrong output that a human catches immediately is a minor embarrassment; one that flows into a downstream system unchecked is an incident. ⚠️

Why does hallucination happen?

  1. Training data has gaps and errors. The model learned from imperfect human text — errors, contradictions, outdated content. Conflicting "facts" may be blended or the most statistically frequent version favored, with no correction mechanism.
  2. Knowledge cutoff. Everything after the training cutoff doesn't exist for the model — but instead of refusing to answer, it generates the most plausible-sounding continuation of patterns it did learn, which may be outdated or fabricated.
  3. Long-tail facts are underrepresented. Well-documented public topics have enormous, consistent training signal — the model is well-calibrated there. Obscure or internal topics (your company's specific policy, a minor regulatory provision) have little to no training signal, so the model fills the gap statistically.
  4. "Plausible" ≠ "true." The training objective was to produce text that looks like human-written text on the topic — not to produce only verified statements. These are different objectives and produce different failure modes.
  5. Instruction-following can override accuracy instincts. If a prompt implies an answer exists ("summarize the Q3 report" when no report was provided), many models will attempt to comply rather than refuse — generating a plausible "summary" of a document that wasn't there.
Hallucination risk scales inversely with consistent training signal on that specific topic
🏢 ENTERPRISE USE CASE The Phantom Policy Section 📜

A compliance team pilots an AI assistant for employees to query data-handling policies. In testing, a QA engineer asks: "What does our policy say about retaining logs from third-party integrations?"

The assistant responds: "Per Section 7.4 of the Data Retention Policy (last updated March 2024), third-party integration logs must be retained for a minimum of 18 months, with exceptions for GDPR-covered data in EU regions."

Specific section, date, number, geographic nuance. Sounds authoritative. The QA engineer checks — Section 7.4 covers something unrelated. The 18-month figure, the EU exception, the March 2024 date: all fabricated. No RAG had been configured; the model generated the most plausible-sounding answer to the question by drawing on patterns of what data retention policies typically contain.

After RAG was added: relevant policy sections were retrieved and injected into context. The model cited actual text. When asked about something the policy didn't cover, it responded: "I don't see specific guidance on this in the provided documents — you may want to check with Legal." That "I don't see it" is the correct hallucination-resistant behavior. Grounding changes the failure mode from "confident fabrication" to "visible gap." 🎯

Mitigations

  • 🛡️ RAG — the single most effective mitigation for domain-specific hallucinations. Ground the model in actual documents at query time.
  • 🛡️ "Say I don't know" instructions — explicitly prompt for "I don't have reliable information on this" when the model is uncertain, and test whether it follows through.
  • 🛡️ Citation requirements — require the model to cite a source for every claim; verify citations are real and say what the model claims they say.
  • 🛡️ Human-in-the-loop review — for high-stakes outputs, never fully automate; require verification before downstream action.
  • 🛡️ Narrow scope — a model asked to answer only from a specific document set hallucinate far less than one asked to answer anything about anything.

How should an architect think about it?

🟢 Architect's Take
Treat hallucination risk the way you'd treat data-integrity risk in any system: assess the consequence of a wrong answer for each specific output, and design verification proportionate to that consequence. A creative-brainstorm assistant where a human reads and decides: light review is fine. An automated compliance report or access-request generator that flows directly into a system of record: RAG, citations, and human sign-off are non-negotiable. "The model might be wrong" is a constraint to design around — exactly as "the cache might be stale" is a constraint you design invalidation strategies around, not a reason to avoid caching. 🏗️
🟣 Deep dive: Do "reasoning" models reduce hallucinations?

Reasoning models (OpenAI o-series, Claude extended thinking) generate an explicit chain-of-thought before producing their final answer. They do show measurably lower hallucination rates on complex multi-step tasks — "thinking out loud" catches more internal contradictions before they become outputs. However, they can also produce elaborate-sounding but ultimately wrong reasoning chains that confidently reach a wrong conclusion. For high-stakes applications, reasoning models reduce hallucination risk but don't eliminate it — RAG, citation requirements, and human review remain necessary. Playbook 2 covers how to evaluate a specific model's reasoning quality practically.

✅ Quick Lab — 10 minutes
Ask any LLM about something real but relatively obscure and internal — a specific internal tool, a policy detail, a real but minor internal event. Then ask it to cite a specific section or date. Try to verify the citation. This exercise almost always produces at least one phantom citation — and the experience of "this sounds so specific and it's completely fabricated" is the best preparation for designing systems that guard against it. 🕵️
1.8

🤏 Small Language Models & Local AI

What is it?

Small Language Models (SLMs) are models in the 1–14 billion parameter range — versus hundreds of billions for frontier models — capable of running on a high-end laptop, a workstation, or modest enterprise server hardware, while remaining genuinely capable across a surprisingly wide range of tasks. Local AI is the practice of running these models on infrastructure you own and control — your laptop, on-premise servers, or private cloud — rather than calling a third-party cloud API. The primary tool for this today is Ollama, which makes running open-weight models locally about as straightforward as pulling a Docker image. A practical stack: Ollama for model serving + Open WebUI for a familiar chat interface + a quantized open-weight model (Llama, Gemma, Mistral, Qwen) as the engine.

🔵 Analogy — Mainframe vs. Developer Laptop, Compressed Into Three Years

In the 1980s, meaningful compute was mainframe-only. The idea of a developer doing real work on a personal computer was laughable. By the 1990s it was normal. By the 2000s a laptop had more compute than those mainframes. LLMs are following a similar trajectory, compressed dramatically. Two years ago "run a capable LLM locally" required a multi-GPU server. Today a laptop with 32GB RAM runs a model (Llama 3.1 8B, Gemma 2 9B) capable enough to genuinely help with a wide range of real tasks. The "mainframe" (frontier cloud models) will always lead on the most demanding tasks. But for a large slice of real work, the "developer laptop" equivalent is already here and practical. 💻

🟣 Analogy — PostgreSQL on a Laptop vs. Oracle RAC — Neither is "Inferior"

PostgreSQL running locally isn't an "inferior version" of Oracle RAC — it's the obviously correct choice for a huge range of tasks, and overkill would be the wrong direction entirely. Similarly, a 7B-parameter SLM isn't "a worse GPT-4" — it's a different tool for different contexts. For "classify this ticket into one of twelve categories," "summarize this 400-word doc into 3 bullets," "extract these fields from this form response" — a well-chosen SLM may perform identically to a frontier model on those specific tasks, at near-zero marginal cost per query with data never leaving your network. Choosing the right tool for the task is good engineering, not settling. 🔧

🏢 ENTERPRISE USE CASE The Pre-Screening Layer That Cut API Costs 60% 💸

A large enterprise was routing all incoming ServiceNow tickets through a frontier LLM for three jobs: (a) classify ticket type, (b) extract key fields, (c) generate a resolution draft. Volume: ~8,000 tickets/day. The API bill was significant — especially since steps (a) and (b) were narrow, well-defined tasks with consistent input patterns, while only step (c) genuinely needed frontier reasoning.

The refactored architecture: steps (a) and (b) were offloaded to a locally-deployed fine-tuned SLM (a quantized 7B model running on modest on-premise hardware, fine-tuned on ~5,000 labeled historical tickets). Accuracy for those specific tasks matched or exceeded the frontier model for that narrow job. Only step (c) — generating the resolution draft — remained on the frontier API, and only for tickets the SLM classified as complex (~30% of volume).

Result: API costs fell ~60%. Classification latency improved (no network round-trip). Data sovereignty improved (ticket content for classification never left the network). The frontier API remained where it added genuine value. This "tiered" pattern — SLM as fast, cheap, private first-pass; frontier as targeted second-pass — is one of the most cost-effective architectural patterns in enterprise AI, covered as a workflow design pattern in Playbook 3. 🏗️

What mistakes do people make?

  • 🚩 Dismissing SLMs without testing on the actual task. The only valid evaluation is testing on representative examples of your specific task — a 7B model that scores lower on a general benchmark may perform identically on your specific ticket-classification job.
  • 🚩 Treating "local" as automatically "secure." Local removes the data-leaves-your-network concern for inference — but the model still has the same hallucination and prompt-injection risks as any LLM. "On-prem" means a different, more controllable risk profile, not zero risk.
  • 🚩 Ignoring operational overhead. A self-hosted model is infrastructure you operate: availability, performance, version updates, security patching of the serving stack. That operational cost needs to be in your build-vs-buy calculation (Playbook 3).

How should an architect think about it?

🟢 Architect's Take
Add "should this component be an SLM?" to every LLM architecture review — especially for components that are high-volume, narrow in scope, latency-sensitive, or privacy-sensitive. The default "use the best frontier model for everything" is comfortable but often wasteful. The right architecture for most mature enterprise AI systems will be a mix of both tiers: frontier where capability is genuinely the differentiating factor, SLM where the task is narrow and volume or privacy makes local the better choice. Design for the task, not the prestige of the model. ⚖️

📋 What To Remember Forever

The one-page executive summary of Playbook 1. Revisit this before any AI architecture conversation. 🎯

🪆 The Four Rings

AI ⊃ ML ⊃ Deep Learning ⊃ Generative AI. Every "AI" conversation deserves the question: which ring? Ring determines governance, risk profile, and explainability requirements.

🔢 Tokens = The Universal Unit

Cost, context window, rate limits, latency — all measured in tokens. Output tokens typically cost 4-5× input tokens. Always estimate from realistic usage, not best-case scenarios.

📁 Context Window = The Folder

One shared budget: system prompt + docs + history + output. No cross-session memory by default. Design compaction strategies for long sessions from day one, not after the first "it forgot" bug.

🏗️ Training ≠ Inference

Training: one-time, done by the provider, already sunk cost. Inference: every API call, continuously, billed to you. Standard usage does not train the model on your data.

📚 RAG Before Fine-Tuning

"The AI should know our policies" → RAG, almost always. Fine-tuning is for behavioral style, not factual knowledge. Embeddings enable semantic search — meaning-based, not keyword-based.

🪜 Four Layers of AI Features

Plain prompt → RAG (knowledge) → Tools (actions) → Agent (autonomous multi-step). Start at the lowest layer that solves the problem. Most tasks genuinely don't need Layer 3.

👻 Hallucination Is Structural

Plausible ≠ true. Highest risk: internal knowledge, post-cutoff events, obscure facts. Mitigate with RAG, citation requirements, and human review scaled to consequence.

🤏 SLMs Are Real Tools

7-14B models run locally at near-zero marginal cost with data never leaving your network. For narrow, high-volume, or latency/privacy-sensitive tasks, often the better choice. Best architectures mix both tiers.
