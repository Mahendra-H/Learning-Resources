🎭 Orientation · ~25 min · Before Playbook 1

The AI Landscape Map

Before we get into tokens, context windows, and architecture diagrams, let's meet the cast. Who actually builds the models? Who builds the hardware underneath them? What's this "open source AI" thing everyone keeps mentioning? And what's all this tooling — LangChain, vector databases, agent frameworks — that seems to sit between "the model" and "the thing your engineers actually build"? This page is a map, not a manual — by the end you'll recognize names and know roughly where they sit, which is 80% of what you need to follow a conversation. 🧭

🔵 Analogy — The Cloud Ecosystem, Five Years Ago
Remember when "cloud" meant AWS, Azure, GCP, plus a constellation of SaaS tools built on top of them, plus an open-source ecosystem (Kubernetes, Terraform, etc.) that let you avoid lock-in if you wanted to? The AI world right now has almost exactly that same shape — just five years younger and moving faster. Foundation model providers are the "cloud platforms." Infrastructure providers are the chip and data-center layer underneath even those platforms. The open model ecosystem is your "self-hosted Kubernetes" option. And the developer ecosystem is the rapidly multiplying layer of tools that sit between "raw model" and "thing your team ships." If you've navigated one ecosystem shift, you already have the instincts for this one. 🌊

🗺️ The Four Zones, At a Glance

Here's the whole map as one picture. We'll walk through each zone in detail below, but keep this shape in your head — almost everything you read or hear about "the AI industry" is really news about one of these four zones, and a lot of confusion comes from not knowing which zone a headline is actually about. 📰

All four zones ultimately feed into the thing your team actually builds and ships

🧠 Zone 1 — Foundation Model Providers

🧠
The companies that train the frontier LLMs
These are the names you'll see at the top of every "model evaluation" conversation in Playbook 2 — they spend hundreds of millions to billions of dollars training each new generation of model.
OpenAI — GPT family, ChatGPT Anthropic — Claude family Google DeepMind — Gemini family Meta — Llama family (also open-weight, see Zone 3) xAI — Grok family

Each of these companies typically ships multiple "tiers" of model at once — a top-end "frontier" model that's expensive and slow but extremely capable, a mid-tier model that balances cost and capability (this is usually the one enterprises actually deploy), and a small/fast model for high-volume, latency-sensitive tasks. Playbook 2 will teach you how to evaluate across these tiers; for now, just know that "which company" and "which tier" are two separate questions, and both matter.

🔵 Analogy — Database Engines, Not Database Products
Comparing OpenAI vs. Anthropic vs. Google is a bit like comparing Oracle vs. PostgreSQL vs. SQL Server as database engines — except instead of one engine per vendor, each vendor offers a whole product line (think "Enterprise Edition," "Standard Edition," "Express Edition," except they're called things like "Opus," "Sonnet," and "Haiku," or "o1," "GPT-4o," and "GPT-4o-mini"). The naming conventions are genuinely confusing even to people who follow this closely — Playbook 2 gives you a framework so you don't need to memorize every name, just understand the shape of each vendor's lineup. 🏷️

⚡ Zone 2 — Infrastructure Providers

⚡
The chips, servers, and clouds everything else depends on
Every model in Zone 1 — and every open-source model in Zone 3 — runs on hardware from this zone. This is the layer Playbook 5 dives into deeply: why GPUs specifically, what a modern AI data center looks like, and where the enormous capital expenditure numbers you read about actually go.
NVIDIA — dominant AI chip maker (GPUs) AMD — growing GPU competitor AWS — cloud + custom AI chips (Trainium/Inferentia) Microsoft Azure — cloud + OpenAI partnership Google Cloud — cloud + custom AI chips (TPUs)
🟢 Analogy — The Power Grid Behind Every Data Center You've Ever Used
You've never had to think hard about who generates the electricity for your company's data centers — it's just "the grid," a layer so foundational it's invisible until something goes wrong (a regional outage, a price spike). NVIDIA and the major clouds are rapidly becoming that kind of layer for AI: invisible to most application developers, but the actual reason a new model release is delayed, or a provider's prices change, or a region runs out of capacity for a new feature. When Playbook 5 explains "why GPUs are expensive," it's really explaining the new power grid. 🔌
🟠 Why this zone matters to your budget
Even if your team never touches a GPU directly — you're just calling an API — the pricing of that API is downstream of this zone's economics. When a foundation model provider's prices drop, it's often because their underlying compute got cheaper or more efficient. We'll connect these dots fully in Playbook 5.

🦙 Zone 3 — The Open Model Ecosystem

🦙
Models you can download and run yourself
Unlike Zone 1's models (which you access only via API — you never touch the actual model weights), these models can be downloaded and run on your own infrastructure — your cloud, your data center, even a laptop for smaller versions.
Llama — Meta's open-weight family Qwen — Alibaba's open-weight family Gemma — Google's smaller open-weight family Mistral — French open-weight specialist
🟠 Analogy — SaaS vs. Self-Hosted Software, All Over Again
This is the exact same conversation enterprises have had for two decades about, say, email (Office 365/Gmail vs. self-hosted Exchange) or identity (Okta/Entra ID vs. self-hosted AD/LDAP). Zone 1 models are "SaaS": someone else runs the infrastructure, patches it, scales it — you just call an API and get a bill. Zone 3 models are "self-hosted": you take on the operational burden (and the infrastructure cost from Zone 2) in exchange for control — over data residency, customization, and avoiding per-token billing entirely. Neither is "right" — it's the same build-vs-buy tradeoff you've made a dozen times before, just with a new acronym. Playbook 3's "Open Source AI" module and "Build vs Buy" framework dig into exactly when each direction makes sense. ⚖️
🟢 A preview of the tradeoff
Roughly: choose Zone 1 (hosted) when you want the best raw capability with minimal operational overhead and your data governance allows sending data to a third party. Choose Zone 3 (open, self-hosted) when data residency is non-negotiable, you have predictable high volume that makes the fixed infrastructure cost worthwhile, or you need deep customization (fine-tuning on proprietary data). Full decision tree in Playbook 3. 🌳

🔧 Zone 4 — The AI Developer Ecosystem

🔧
The tooling layer between "a model" and "a product"
A raw LLM, by itself, isn't a chatbot, an assistant, or an agent — it's a prediction engine you talk to via API. This zone is the rapidly growing collection of frameworks and tools that handle the plumbing: connecting models to your data, your tools, and each other.
LangChain — popular orchestration framework LlamaIndex — data-connection / RAG framework Open WebUI — self-hosted chat interface Vector Databases — Pinecone, Weaviate, Chroma, pgvector… Agent Frameworks — multiple competing approaches
🟣 Analogy — The "Middleware" Layer You Already Know
If Zone 1/3 models are like a powerful but generic compute engine (think: a raw database engine with no ORM, no migrations tooling, no admin UI), Zone 4 is the ecosystem of frameworks that make that engine usable in a real application — your ORMs, your API gateways, your service meshes. Just as "which ORM should we use" is a real but secondary decision compared to "which database," "which agent framework" is a real but secondary decision compared to "which model." This zone moves extremely fast — new frameworks and patterns emerge monthly — which is part of why Playbook 5 includes an "Industry Monitoring" system: you don't need to track every new tool, but you do need a habit for noticing when something becomes the new standard. 🔄
🟣 A note for later
Many of the security concerns in Playbook 4 — prompt injection, context poisoning, data leakage — actually live in this zone, not in the models themselves. The model is often just doing exactly what it was told; the orchestration layer is where the "told by whom, with what data, with what permissions" questions get decided (or not decided, which is the problem).

🔗 How the Zones Relate — A Worked Example

Let's make this concrete with the running example from this whole series: an internal IAM assistant that helps employees with access requests, password resets, and policy questions.

ZoneWhat it provides for the IAM assistantExample choice
🧠 Foundation ModelThe "brain" that understands questions and drafts responsesClaude Sonnet via API
⚡ InfrastructureWhat that model (and your own services) actually run onAnthropic's infra (for the model) + your AWS account (for the app)
🦙 Open ModelsAn alternative if data residency requires self-hostingLlama, self-hosted on your AWS GPUs — a fallback path
🔧 Developer EcosystemThe glue: RAG over your IAM docs, tool calls to ServiceNow/CyberArk, agent orchestrationLlamaIndex for retrieval + an MCP-based tool layer

Notice that a single project can touch all four zones — and most real conversations about "which AI should we use" are actually several separate decisions (one per zone) being discussed as if they were one decision. Untangling that is half the battle in any AI architecture conversation. 🧵

🔵 What to remember from this page
You don't need to memorize every name above — you need to recognize, when you hear a name in a meeting, which zone it belongs to, because that tells you what kind of decision is actually being discussed (a model choice? an infrastructure commitment? a build-vs-buy call? a tooling preference?). That's the whole point of this map. 🗺️
