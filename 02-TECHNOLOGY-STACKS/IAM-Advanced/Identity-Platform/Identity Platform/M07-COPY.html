Why this module matters

The operational core of enterprise IAM

Authentication (M03a, M03b) proves who you are. Authorization (M06) decides what you can do. IGA governs whether you should have that access in the first place — and can prove it to an auditor. Of the three disciplines, IGA is the most operationally complex and the most directly tied to regulatory compliance.

You work with Saviynt daily. This module treats it as an architect, not an operator. The goal is to understand the IGA platform architecture — how Saviynt's components connect, how HR-driven lifecycle workflows are designed, how SoD policy prevents compliance failures, and how identity analytics surfaces risk. By the end, you will be able to design an IGA program from scratch and articulate every architectural decision to business stakeholders and security auditors.

IGA is also where M02 (JML lifecycle), M04b (SCIM provisioning), and M06 (RBAC entitlements) converge into a coherent operational system. Every concept from the previous six modules has a concrete IGA implementation here.

HR-driven lifecycle

The authoritative source chain — HR to IGA to targets

The most important architectural decision in IGA is: what is the authoritative source for identity data? In most enterprises, it is the HR system. Everything flows from there. Getting this chain right is what separates an IGA deployment that works from one that creates as many exceptions as it resolves.

Diagram 2 — HR-driven lifecycle: complete Joiner provisioning flow
J
Joiner — precise architectural design decisions
The Joiner workflow has three design choices that determine its quality: (1) When does provisioning start? — T-2 days for core accounts (AD, email), Day 1 for applications, Day 3+ for systems requiring manager confirmation. (2) How are birthright entitlements determined? — Department + Job Level + Location as the minimum attributes. Role-based birthright rules in Saviynt map these to application access. (3) What triggers the workflow? — HR system API webhook (real-time, preferred) vs scheduled batch (hourly/daily, common fallback).
Key metric: onboarding completion rate (% of accounts provisioned on Day 1 vs still pending). Target: >95% for core systems. Track via Saviynt's onboarding dashboard.
M
Mover — the most under-governed lifecycle event
Triggered when an employee's department, job level, or manager changes in the HR system. Saviynt must: (1) Identify the delta between old-role entitlements and new-role entitlements, (2) Add new birthright entitlements, (3) Remove old entitlements that are no longer justified, (4) Flag entitlements that fall into neither category for manager certification. Privilege creep accumulates through poorly designed Mover workflows — old entitlements never removed.
Saviynt design pattern: "Mover Ruleset" — define which entitlements transfer automatically, which require re-approval, and which are automatically revoked. Default to revoke unless justified — not retain unless requested.
L
Leaver — timing is the security-critical dimension
Voluntary termination (2-week notice) and involuntary termination (immediate) require different timing policies. Involuntary: disable all accounts simultaneously within 15 minutes of HR event. Active sessions revoked. PAM vault credentials rotated. Voluntary: scheduled disable on last day, forward email for 30 days, archive and delete per retention policy. The Leaver workflow must integrate with SSSD cache flush (M05) and PAM session termination (M08).
Saviynt termination playbook: disable → revoke sessions → CyberArk rotation → SSSD flush via Ansible → 90-day archive → delete. Every step logged with timestamp for audit evidence.
Access requests

Access request & approval engine design

Birthright access covers the baseline. Everything beyond birthright — additional application access, elevated permissions, temporary access for a project — goes through the access request engine. Designing this engine well determines whether IGA makes users' lives easier or drives them to shadow IT workarounds.

Request portal design
What users see — the access catalogue
The access catalogue is the user-facing store of requestable entitlements. Architecture decisions: (1) What's in the catalogue? Only formally governed entitlements — not every AD group, not every application permission. Ungoverned access should not be requestable through a formal process until it's been classified. (2) How is it organized? By business capability ("I need to approve invoices") not by technical system ("I need SAP T-code XK01"). Business-language catalogue reduces requests for wrong permissions. (3) What metadata is required? Business justification (free text), duration (permanent vs time-limited), manager pre-approval for sensitive items.
Approval workflow design
The approval chain architecture
Approval workflow design determines whether IGA creates governance theatre or real access control. Anti-patterns: (1) Manager-only approval for everything — managers rubber-stamp requests because they don't understand the technical implications. (2) IT approval for everything — IT becomes a bottleneck with no business context for the decision.

Correct design: Risk-tiered approval chains. Low-risk (collaboration tools, standard SaaS): manager + auto-approve after 48hrs if no response. Medium-risk (finance apps, sensitive data): manager + app owner. High-risk (privileged access, sensitive systems): manager + app owner + security team. Emergency access: break-glass process with 2-person approval + mandatory post-approval review.
⚠️Design gotchaAuto-escalation after timeout — the governance loophole that causes audit findings
Many IGA deployments configure access requests to auto-approve if the approver doesn't respond within N days. The intent is to reduce friction. The result: a systematic governance bypass. Auditors find that 30% of access grants were auto-approved because managers didn't respond — and those auto-approvals are indistinguishable from intentional approvals in the logs. Design principle: auto-escalate (to the next approver up the chain), never auto-approve. If no approver responds after full escalation, route to the IGA operations team for disposition — do not grant by default.
Certification campaigns

Access certification architecture

Access certifications (also called access reviews or attestations) are the periodic governance process where managers and application owners review and attest that their team members' access is still appropriate. Required by SOX, SOC 2, ISO 27001, PCI-DSS, and HIPAA. The architecture of how you run certifications determines whether they provide real governance or just compliance theatre.

Certification campaign types — three different purposes
User-centric campaigns: "Review all access for users in your team." Managers review every entitlement each of their direct reports holds. Best for: annual compliance certifications, post-acquisition reviews, high-risk population reviews. Weakness: managers often rubber-stamp without understanding the technical implications of each entitlement.

Application-centric campaigns: "Review all users who have access to this application." Application owners review who has access and whether it's still appropriate. Best for: sensitive application governance, system decommissions, post-security-incident reviews. Strength: the reviewer knows the application's risk level.

Entitlement-centric campaigns: "Review all users who hold this specific entitlement." IGA team reviews who holds a high-risk entitlement (e.g., Admin role in a financial system). Best for: SoD-sensitive entitlements, privileged access governance, high-risk role populations.
Campaign design principle 1
Risk-based prioritization
Not all access requires annual review. Design a risk-tiered cadence: privileged access (PAM, admin roles) → quarterly. Sensitive application access (finance, HR) → semi-annual. Standard collaboration tools → annual. Low-risk / read-only → every 18 months. Risk score from identity analytics (M07 section below) should automatically trigger out-of-cycle reviews for anomalous users.
Campaign design principle 2
Context-rich reviewer experience
Reviewers who see only "approve / revoke" with a list of application names make uninformed decisions. Saviynt should surface: last login date (has this user actually used this access?), access granted date (how long have they had it?), comparison to peer group (does everyone in their department have this?), risk indicators (does this access create an SoD conflict?). Context drives informed decisions, not rubber stamps.
Campaign design principle 3
Remediating revocations
When a reviewer marks access for revocation, Saviynt must automatically trigger deprovisioning — not create a ticket for IT to manually process. Manual remediation workflows create a backlog that defeats the purpose of certification. The certification campaign's value is its automatic enforcement: revoke decision = automatic SCIM call to target system = account modified within 24 hours. Track remediation completion rate as a KPI.
Segregation of duties

SoD policy design — the compliance backbone

Segregation of Duties (SoD) is a fundamental internal control principle: no single person should be able to both initiate and approve a transaction. In IAM terms: no user should hold both the "Create Vendor" and "Approve Payment" entitlements simultaneously. SoD violations are SOX findings. Designing them correctly in Saviynt is one of the highest-value IGA architecture activities.

SoD policy anatomy in Saviynt
A Saviynt SoD rule has four components: (1) Conflicting entitlement set A — the set of permissions that conflict with set B. Can be a role, an application entitlement, or a function. (2) Conflicting entitlement set B — if a user holds any entitlement from Set A AND any from Set B, the policy is violated. (3) Risk level — Critical (auto-block, require compensating control), High (flag, require approval), Medium (flag, include in certification). (4) Mitigating control — a compensating control that allows a violation to be accepted (e.g., "this user has an approved exception for this SoD because they are in a small office with no separation possible, and a supervisor review process is in place").

Example — financial controls SoD: Set A: {Vendor-Create, Vendor-Modify} × Set B: {Payment-Approve, Payment-Release} = CRITICAL SoD. No user should hold any combination of these across SAP, Oracle, or the payment gateway.
Preventive SoD controls
Block at request time
When a user requests an entitlement that would create an SoD violation, Saviynt blocks the request at the access request engine — before it reaches an approver. The requestor sees: "This access conflicts with your existing Vendor-Create entitlement. You must either request an exception or relinquish the conflicting access."

Architect's design note: Preventive controls are vastly more efficient than detective controls. Blocking at request time means no violation was ever created — no remediation needed, no audit finding. Detective controls (finding violations in certification campaigns) mean the violation existed for up to a year before being corrected.
Detective SoD controls
Find violations that already exist
Saviynt continuously evaluates all user entitlements against all SoD rules. Violations are surfaced in: (1) Certification campaigns — reviewers are alerted when a user they're reviewing has an SoD violation. (2) SoD dashboards — real-time view of current violation count, severity, and age. (3) Access request approvals — approvers are shown existing SoD violations before making an approval decision.

The risk score connection: Users with unmitigated SoD violations should have elevated risk scores in identity analytics. High-risk users should appear in targeted campaign reviews, not just annual reviews.
Role engineering

Role mining & role engineering

M06 covered role explosion as RBAC's primary failure mode. The solution is role engineering — designing a role structure that accurately reflects business functions, is maintainable, and doesn't grow unboundedly. Saviynt's role mining capability is the data-driven tool for discovering the right role structure from actual access data.

Role mining — bottom-up
Discover roles from access patterns
Saviynt's role mining analyzes the actual entitlement assignments across all users and identifies natural clusters — groups of users who have the same (or very similar) set of entitlements. These clusters are role candidates.

Algorithm output: "80% of users in the Finance department with Job Level = Analyst hold exactly these 7 entitlements: {M365-E3, SharePoint-Finance-Read, SAP-FI-Display, Salesforce-Finance-Read, ServiceNow-Requester, Workday-Self-Service, Concur-Submitter}. This is a candidate for the 'Finance-Analyst' business role."

Architect's action: Validate candidate roles with business stakeholders. Is this the intended access for Finance Analysts? Are there entitlements in the cluster that shouldn't be there? Convert validated clusters into formal roles with owners and review cadence.
Role engineering — top-down
Design roles from business functions
The complement to role mining. Start from the org chart and job descriptions — what does a "Finance Analyst" need to do their job? Work with business stakeholders to define the minimum necessary access for each job function.

The four-step role engineering process: (1) Identify distinct job functions (not job titles — functions). Finance Analyst, Finance Manager, Finance Controller are distinct functions with different access needs. (2) For each function, identify all systems they use and the access level required. (3) Create business roles mapping functions to entitlements. (4) Assign role owners who attest annually that the role still accurately represents the function.

Target: 80% of access covered by business roles. 20% handled by individual access requests. Never 100% role-based — exceptions will always exist.

Identity analytics

Risk-based governance — identity analytics

Identity analytics transforms IGA from a reactive compliance tool into a proactive risk management capability. Instead of waiting for annual certifications to find stale access, analytics surfaces anomalies continuously — users with outlier access patterns, accounts that haven't been used in 90 days, roles with unexpectedly broad entitlements.

Signal 1
Peer group outlier analysis
Compare a user's access to the peer group defined by their department, job level, and location. A Finance Analyst in the NY office with 3× the average entitlements of other Finance Analysts in the same office is an outlier — flag for review. Saviynt's analytics engine computes peer group baselines and surfaces deviations as risk indicators.
Signal 2
Access usage analytics
Access that was requested but never used is a risk signal — it means the business justification didn't materialize, but the access persists. Saviynt integrates with application logs and authentication systems to detect: last login date per application, last transaction date in business applications (SAP, Oracle), command history in privileged sessions. Unused access for 90+ days → auto-revocation or mandatory certification.
Signal 3
Role-based risk scoring
Not all roles carry equal risk. A user holding 3 low-risk roles has a different risk profile than a user holding 1 high-risk privileged access role. Saviynt's risk engine weights entitlements by: sensitivity classification, SoD violation count, last certification outcome (was access questioned in last review?), privileged access flag, cloud admin rights. The composite score drives review prioritization.
Knowledge check

Test your IGA architecture depth

Q1 Your enterprise's IGA system provisions access via SCIM to 80 applications. A manager moves from Finance to Engineering. Design the complete Mover workflow including which entitlements are automatically removed, which require re-approval, and how privilege creep is prevented. ▼
Mover workflow architecture for Finance → Engineering transition:

Step 1 — Trigger: HR system (Workday) fires department change event via API webhook. Saviynt receives: EmployeeID, OldDepartment=Finance, NewDepartment=Engineering, NewJobLevel, EffectiveDate.

Step 2 — Entitlement delta calculation: Saviynt computes: (A) Finance birthright entitlements (what Finance Analysts get automatically) — these are revoked. (B) Engineering birthright entitlements — these are provisioned. (C) Individually-requested entitlements beyond birthright — these require disposition decision.

Step 3 — Automatic actions (no approval): Remove Finance-specific entitlements (Finance LDAP groups, SAP-FI access, SharePoint-Finance site). Provision Engineering-specific birthright (Engineering JIRA, GitHub org membership, dev environment access). These happen on the effective date without approval — they are policy-defined.

Step 4 — Manager certification for exceptions: Individual access grants that fall outside both Finance and Engineering birthright definitions are placed in a triggered certification campaign for the new manager. "Alice has SAP-Procurement-Approval — is this still needed in her Engineering role?" Manager has 5 business days to approve-retain or revoke. Default on non-response: escalate to IGA ops team, not auto-approve.

Step 5 — Privilege creep prevention: Any entitlement not covered by the new role's birthright AND not explicitly re-approved by the new manager within 5 days is automatically revoked. This is the "prove it forward" model — the burden of justification is on retention, not on revocation.

Step 6 — Audit trail: All actions logged: what was removed (automatic), what was provisioned (automatic), what was certified (manager action), what was escalated (no-response). Complete audit trail available for the next SOX review.
Q2 Design an SoD policy for a Procure-to-Pay process in SAP. Identify the conflicting function pairs, define the risk levels, and explain how Saviynt should enforce them preventively vs detectivley. ▼
Procure-to-Pay SoD policy design:

Conflicting function pairs (SOX-driven):
Rule 1 — CRITICAL: {Create Vendor, Modify Vendor} × {Approve Payment, Release Payment} — No one should be able to both add/edit a vendor AND approve payments to that vendor. Classic fraud vector: create a shell company as a vendor, then approve payments to it.
Rule 2 — CRITICAL: {Create Purchase Order} × {Goods Receipt} — No one should create a PO and then confirm receipt of goods (inventory theft vector).
Rule 3 — HIGH: {Create Purchase Order} × {Approve Purchase Order} — Self-approval of POs above the materiality threshold.
Rule 4 — HIGH: {Manage Bank Master Data} × {Any Payment Function} — Ability to change bank account numbers plus approve payments.
Rule 5 — MEDIUM: {Access Payroll Master Data} × {Process Payroll} — Ghost employee fraud vector.

Preventive enforcement in Saviynt: Rules 1 and 2 (CRITICAL) are enforced at access request time. If a user requests "Approve Payment" and already holds "Create Vendor," Saviynt blocks the request immediately with a conflict notification. The only path to resolution: submit a compensating control exception (approved by CISO + CFO + external auditor), or relinquish the conflicting entitlement.

Detective enforcement: Saviynt runs continuous SoD evaluation against all users' current entitlements. Violations appear in: (1) Daily SoD violation dashboard — count by severity, by business unit. (2) Quarterly targeted certifications for users with active SoD violations. (3) SOX audit evidence package — generated automatically, showing all violations, their resolution date, and the approver of any exception.
Q3 A SOX audit identifies that 35% of access grants in the last certification campaign were "auto-approved" because managers didn't respond within the timeout window. The auditor flags this as a control failure. How do you remediate immediately and redesign the certification architecture to prevent recurrence? ▼
Immediate remediation: All auto-approved items are not validated certifications — they must be re-certified manually. Identify all access grants where the certification decision was system-generated (not manager-inputted). Place these users in an emergency targeted certification campaign with a 10-business-day deadline. Non-response in the emergency campaign → automatic revocation (not escalation). Report remediation completion to the auditor with evidence.

Root cause analysis: Why did managers not respond? (1) Too many items per reviewer — cognitive overload leads to abandonment. (2) Timeout too short — campaign window didn't align with manager availability (vacation season, quarter-end). (3) No consequence for non-response — managers knew auto-approval would handle it. (4) Campaign notification went to wrong channel (email vs Slack vs ServiceNow task).

Architectural redesign:
(1) Eliminate auto-approve entirely — replace with auto-escalate. Non-response after 5 days → escalate to manager's manager. After 10 days → escalate to department head. After 15 days → route to IGA operations for manual disposition. Default on no disposition: revoke.
(2) Reduce review load — Risk-tier the campaign. Managers only certify entitlements above a risk threshold in the primary campaign. Low-risk/unchanged entitlements are pre-approved (manager confirms with one click that nothing changed) or excluded from the campaign entirely.
(3) Accountability metrics — Report manager certification completion rates to HR and senior leadership quarterly. Make non-completion a performance metric. Managers who systematically don't certify should be flagged in HR systems.
(4) Timing policy — Never run certification campaigns during December, summer vacation peaks, or financial close periods. Calendar campaigns for March/September for maximum manager availability.
Q4 How does Saviynt's identity analytics risk scoring connect to your authorization architecture (M06 OPA policies)? Design the integration between IGA risk signals and real-time access control decisions. ▼
IGA risk signals → authorization policy integration:

This is one of the most powerful — and least commonly implemented — patterns in enterprise IAM. The idea: Saviynt computes a user's identity risk score based on access anomalies, SoD violations, and unused entitlement patterns. That risk score should influence real-time authorization decisions — not just periodic certifications.

Integration architecture:
(1) Saviynt API → risk score attribute: Saviynt exposes a REST API endpoint: GET /api/v5/risk/users/{userId} returning {"riskScore": 78, "riskFactors": ["sod_violation", "outlier_access", "unused_90_days"]}. This is polled by the attribute pipeline or pushed via webhook on score change.
(2) Risk score → user attribute in PIP: The OPA Policy Information Point (PIP from M06) subscribes to risk score updates. User's current risk score is cached with a 15-minute TTL in the PIP's attribute store.
(3) OPA policy uses risk score:
deny { input.action == "approve_payment"; data.users[input.subject].risk_score > 70 }
require_mfa_stepup { data.users[input.subject].risk_score > 50; input.resource.sensitivity == "high" }
(4) Result: A user with a high Saviynt risk score (SoD violation, unused access) is dynamically required to re-authenticate with stronger MFA before performing high-sensitivity actions — even if their base access token is still valid. This closes the gap between IGA's governance layer and the real-time enforcement layer.

Key takeaways — M07 · Identity Governance & Administration
  • IGA is where JML lifecycle (M02), SCIM provisioning (M04b), and RBAC entitlements (M06) converge into an operational system. The architect's job is to design the platform architecture that makes these work reliably at enterprise scale.
  • The authoritative source chain is the most important IGA design decision: HR system → Saviynt → target systems. Every identity decision flows from the HR record. No HR record = no access, regardless of who asks.
  • Mover workflows are the most under-governed JML event. The default must be "revoke unless justified" — not "retain unless requested." Privilege creep is a Mover workflow design failure, not a user behavior problem.
  • Access certifications must auto-escalate, never auto-approve. Auto-approve on timeout is a systematic governance bypass that produces audit findings. Escalate to the next approver in the chain, and to IGA ops as the final backstop.
  • SoD preventive controls are vastly more efficient than detective controls. Blocking a conflict at access request time costs seconds. Discovering it in an annual certification and remediating it costs weeks of effort and may still produce an audit finding for the period of violation.
  • Role engineering is a design discipline, not a configuration task. 80% of access should be covered by business roles designed from job functions. Role mining discovers natural clusters from actual usage data. Role explosion is a role governance failure.
  • Identity analytics connects IGA governance to real-time authorization. Saviynt risk scores can feed OPA policies to dynamically tighten access controls for high-risk users — closing the gap between periodic certifications and continuous enforcement.

Module M08 covers Privileged Access Management (PAM) with CyberArk as the primary lens. PAM is IGA's specialized counterpart for the 1–5% of accounts that carry the highest risk: system administrators, database administrators, emergency access accounts, shared privileged accounts, and cloud admin roles. These accounts can cause catastrophic damage if compromised — and they require controls that go far beyond what IGA provides: session recording, session isolation, just-in-time access, zero standing privilege.

The connection to M07 is direct: IGA provisions and manages the lifecycle of privileged accounts (who should have a CyberArk-managed account), while PAM controls how those accounts are used (session management, recording, JIT access). They are complementary governance layers — IGA is the governance control plane, PAM is the operational enforcement plane for privileged access.
