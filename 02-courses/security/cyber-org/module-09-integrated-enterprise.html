The Scenario

Tuesday morning. A financial enterprise. A threat actor with a plan.

100,000 employees. $50B in assets under management. Six months of reconnaissance. One overlooked service account.

M
Mentor — Former CISO

I want to set the scene before we begin. The organization you are about to observe is a composite of several incidents I have either led or consulted on.

It is a financial services enterprise. 100,000 employees. $50B in assets under management. Regulated by three separate regulatory bodies.

The threat actor has been doing reconnaissance for six months. They purchased credentials from a dark web market. Tested them. Found one that worked — a service account with domain admin privileges that was never rotated and never enrolled in PAM.

They waited. They planned. They had an objective: encrypt the core trading system during market hours and demand $25M in ransom. Their window: Tuesday morning between 9 AM and market open at 9:30 AM.

What they did not know: Maria had changed shifts that week. She was covering the overnight. And at 6:47 AM, she saw something that looked almost normal but was not quite right. Let us begin.

6:47 AM · Tuesday · Security Operations Center

Three monitors. Fourteen open alert queues. Cold coffee. A service account authentication from an IP address it had never used before.

At 3 AM. On a Tuesday.

Maria's hand stopped moving. She leaned forward. She clicked.

The Incident

The next 24 hours.

Every decision matters. Every team has a role. Every minute has a cost.

    06:47 AM SOC · TIER 1
    The Alert
    Maria sees the anomaly. A service account authenticating from an unknown IP at an unusual time. She does not dismiss it. She does not create a ticket and move on. She escalates immediately to Tier 2 — breaking protocol on response time because something about this does not sit right. Clock: 06:47.

    07:15 AM SOC · TIER 2
    Confirmed Threat
    Tier 2 pulls the logs. Correlation across 6 systems. The service account has been accessing systems it has no business reason to access — file servers, backup systems, domain controllers. Lateral movement confirmed. This is not an accident. Escalation to Incident Response initiated. Clock: 07:15.

  3. 07:31
    07:31 AM INCIDENT RESPONSE

  4. 07:45
    07:45 AM IAM TEAM IAM ACTION

    IR's first call is to IAM. The compromised service account is revoked immediately. All active sessions terminated across every system. PAM vault audit initiated — searching for any other account the attacker may have accessed or pivoted through. Three additional accounts flagged and suspended as precaution. The attacker loses access. Clock: 07:45. Time since detection: 58 minutes.

Known TTPs: ransomware deployment after 2–4 hours of lateral movement. We are inside that window — but the attacker has already been cut off. Intel shared across IR, SOC, and CISO. Clock: 08:10.

Post-Incident

What this attack taught the organization.

Three categories. No excuses. Only action.

✓ What Worked
SOC Tier 1 analyst instinct
One analyst's training and pattern recognition changed the entire outcome. Maria's decision to escalate rather than dismiss the alert was the first domino.
IR playbook for credential compromise
Pre-practiced, executed flawlessly under pressure. The bridge was open in minutes. No confusion about who owned what.
PAM program
Revoking the compromised account in 4 minutes cut the attacker off before they could reach their objective. PAM was the decisive technical control.
✗ What Failed
Unmanaged service account
A domain admin service account not enrolled in PAM. Never rotated. The attacker purchased credentials for it on the dark web. This was the entry point.
Unpatched VPN vulnerability
The CVE used to maintain persistence had been past remediation SLA for 47 days. VM team had it queued. It never got prioritized.
VM SLA breach on the exact exploit
The specific CVE used was on the VM team's radar. A process failure — not a technology failure — left it unpatched past the window that would have prevented this.
→ What Improved
PAM expansion approved by Board
Full PAM enrollment for all privileged accounts. No more unmanaged service accounts. Budget approved same day.
IGA access certification cycle approved
Quarterly access reviews for all privileged accounts. The 47 over-privileged accounts found in the audit triggered an immediate certification run.
VM escalation process redesigned
New SLA escalation triggers: any CVE with active exploitation in the wild goes to emergency queue automatically.
New SOC detection rule
Detection rule created for this exact TTP. Service account authentication from new IP outside business hours now triggers an automatic Tier 2 alert.
The ROI Argument

The attack succeeded in gaining access. It failed in achieving its objective.

The difference: the IAM program revoked the attacker's access in 4 minutes.

Without PAM, the attacker keeps access. With access, they deploy ransomware. With ransomware at 07:45 AM, they have an hour and 45 minutes before market open. The $25M ransom demand becomes a real business decision.

One PAM action. 4 minutes. $25M avoided.

This is not a technical ROI argument. This is a board-level business case. And this is why the IAM Architect's work matters.

The Architect's Role

What the IAM Architect did.

Not the team. Not the technology. The Architect specifically.

Before the Incident
Designed the PAM program that put every privileged account under vault control.
Advocated for the PAM expansion budget that was approved in hindsight — too late for the compromised service account, but just in time for 47 others.
Defined the privileged account policy that IR followed when they called at 07:45.
Built the IR identity runbook that enabled 4-minute credential revocation.
During the Incident
Authorized the emergency credential revocation for the compromised account.
Led the full privileged account audit that found the 47 additional risks.
Briefed IR on the attacker's likely next identity moves based on known TTPs.
Maintained live status of all IAM actions on the incident bridge.
After the Incident
Presented the PAM expansion program to CISO with Board approval language already drafted.
Led the IGA access certification design that addresses the root cause of the unmanaged service account.
Updated the IR playbook with new identity-specific response procedures.
Prepared the identity section of the Board presentation: one slide, three numbers, zero jargon.

The IAM Architect did not put out the fire. They built the system that stopped the fire from spreading — and then designed the improvements that prevent the next one.

That is the difference between a practitioner and an architect. Practitioners respond. Architects design systems that make the right response possible.

A Final Thought

The IAM Architect is not the hero of this story.

Maria is the hero. The IR commander is the hero. The team that revoked credentials in 4 minutes is the hero.

The IAM Architect is the person who made sure the heroes had the tools and processes they needed when the moment arrived.

That is the highest form of security leadership.
