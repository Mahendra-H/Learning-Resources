Opening Story

The admin account nobody knew existed.

The Ghost That Owned Everything

Three years into a CISO role at a financial services firm, I commissioned a PAM discovery exercise.

The goal was simple: find every privileged account in the environment and bring it under control.

We found 847 privileged accounts.

Our records showed 203.

The other 644 were what we call ghost accounts — service accounts created by projects that ended, admin accounts created for one-time tasks, shared credentials that had been passed around for years, default admin accounts on systems that were never changed after installation.

644 accounts with domain admin equivalent access. Nobody knew they existed. Nobody owned them. Nobody had changed their passwords in years.

In the underground markets, one of them had already been sold three times.

We found out because an attacker who had purchased it six months earlier was still using it — quietly — accessing financial records, watching our environment, moving slowly toward our most sensitive systems.

47 days before we found them.

The PAM program we built after that discovery was the single most impactful security initiative in my tenure.

Not because of the technology. Because of the organizational discipline it required to maintain.

The most dangerous account in any enterprise is not the one the attacker creates.

It is the one your own organization forgot it had.

Section 03 · Concepts

Three programs. One identity ecosystem.

The most common confusion in enterprise security is treating IAM, IGA, and PAM as competing products. They are not. They are three layers of a single, complete program.

Mentor · Former CISO

Let me use your engineering background to make this permanent.

IAM is like your authentication system — it verifies who you are, creates your identity in the directory, manages your credentials, and controls how you sign in across every system.

IGA is like your authorization policy engine — it decides what you should be able to do based on your role, checks whether you still need that access, and produces the audit evidence that proves the right people have the right access.

PAM is like your production access control — it governs who can touch the most critical systems, under what conditions, with mandatory approval workflows and full session recording. It is the tightest layer. The one attackers want most.

The Critical Distinction — Permanent

IAM, IGA, and PAM are not competing products. They are three layers of a complete identity program.

IAM is the foundation — who exists and how they authenticate. Without IAM, there is no identity infrastructure to build anything else on.

IGA is the governance layer — what they should have access to and whether they still need it. Without IGA, access accumulates until an auditor finds it or an attacker exploits it.

PAM is the privileged control layer — the tightest controls for the accounts with the most power. Without PAM, the most dangerous accounts in the enterprise are the least controlled.

An enterprise with strong IAM but no IGA has identities without governance.
An enterprise with IGA but no PAM has governance without privileged control.
All three together form a complete identity program.

Section 05 · IGA Deep Dive

IGA Deep Dive.

Governance is the difference between access that is right and access that just exists.

Joiner / Mover / Leaver
Mentor · Former CISO

The Joiner/Mover/Leaver process sounds administrative. It is actually one of the most security-critical processes in the enterprise.

The terminated employee who still has access 48 hours after leaving is a JML failure. The contractor who was moved to a new project but kept access to the old one is a JML failure. The new hire who has access to production on day one is a JML failure.

Every one of those is an audit finding. Every one of those is a potential breach.

IGA exists to make JML systematic, auditable, and automatic.

Role Management
Mentor · Former CISO

Role-based access control is the foundation of access governance.

Instead of assigning individual permissions to individual people — which creates thousands of unique permission sets that no one can audit or manage — you assign roles.

Engineer role. Finance Analyst role. Manager role.

IGA defines what each role is allowed to access, enforces Segregation of Duties — ensuring no single person can both create and approve a payment, for example — and manages role lifecycle as the organization changes over time.

The most common failure I see in IGA programs is role explosion: organizations create so many fine-grained roles that the model becomes unmanageable. A well-governed IGA program maintains a clean role hierarchy with regular review and pruning.

Access Certifications
Mentor · Former CISO

Even with perfect role management, access drifts over time.

Someone was given temporary access that was never revoked. A role was updated and no one checked whether the new entitlements were still appropriate. A manager approved an access request without fully understanding what it allows.

Access certifications are the periodic review process where managers confirm that the access their team members have is still appropriate.

Done well: fast, targeted, and meaningful — managers review only exceptions and high-risk items, not every single entitlement.

Done poorly: a checkbox exercise that takes three weeks, achieves 95% rubber-stamp approvals, and creates false assurance that would not survive an audit.

Access Certifications & Compliance — The Direct Connection

Access certification is the IGA function most directly connected to audit and compliance.

SOC 2, ISO 27001, PCI-DSS, HIPAA — all require evidence that access is periodically reviewed and that inappropriate access is removed. The IAM team produces this evidence. The GRC team uses it.

This is one of the most direct connections between IAM and the governance layer you will meet in Module 08. When an auditor asks for user access review evidence, the answer comes from your IGA platform.

The IAM Architect who understands this connection — who designs the IGA program with audit evidence in mind — is the one the CISO trusts with the board presentation.

Section 06 · PAM Deep Dive

PAM Deep Dive.

The accounts that can do the most damage require the most control.

Privileged Account Types
Mentor · Former CISO

Not all privileged accounts are equal.

Each type carries different risk. Each type requires different PAM controls. And the ones most organizations overlook are the most dangerous.

HIGHEST RISK
Domain Admin Accounts
Full control of Active Directory. Can create, modify, or delete any object in the directory. If compromised: complete domain takeover. Every enterprise PAM program starts here.
HIGH RISK
Local Admin Accounts
Administrative access to individual systems. Often the initial foothold for lateral movement. Hardest to inventory — every endpoint may have a unique local admin credential.
MOST OVERLOOKED
Service Accounts
Non-human accounts that run applications and automated processes. Often have high privileges. Passwords rarely rotated. Frequently undiscovered. The ones attackers find first because no human notices a login.
CRITICAL CONTROL
Emergency / Break-Glass Accounts
Used only in crises — when normal access channels fail. Highest possible privilege. Tightest controls. Every use triggers automatic audit alert and mandatory review.
THIRD-PARTY RISK
Third-Party Vendor Accounts
External access with high privilege — vendors, consultants, MSPs. Often have persistent access long after engagement ends. JIT provisioning is the right model: access only when needed, fully logged, auto-expired.
EMBEDDED RISK
Application / Embedded Credentials
Credentials hard-coded or embedded in applications, scripts, and pipelines. No rotation. No visibility. Often discovered only in code repositories — or in a breach investigation. Secrets management platforms are the PAM answer here.

Session Recording
Mentor · Former CISO

PAM session recording is one of the most powerful forensic capabilities in any enterprise security program.

When a privileged session is recorded — every command typed, every file accessed, every screen change captured — you have a complete audit trail of everything that happened under that account.

This matters in three scenarios:

Audit: Prove exactly what your admins did in a regulated system. No disputes, no ambiguity — the recording is the evidence.

Incident: When a privileged account is compromised, session recordings show exactly what the attacker did — every step of the lateral movement, every file accessed, every command executed. This is how you scope a breach.

Insider threat: Behavioral analytics on session recordings can detect unusual patterns before damage is done — an admin accessing systems outside their normal scope, at unusual hours, in unusual volume.

Session recording is not surveillance. It is accountability infrastructure.

Mentor · Former CISO — What I Actually Look For When I Hire

When I hire an IAM Architect, I am not looking for the best SailPoint developer or the best CyberArk administrator.

I am looking for someone who can do five things:

Design: Create an identity architecture that serves the business for the next five years — not just solves the problem in front of them today.

Communicate: Explain complex identity concepts to the CISO, the CFO, and the VP of Engineering in language each one understands — and get budget approved because of it.

Navigate: Build relationships with every team in the security organization and every business unit that touches identity — and be the person those teams call when they have an identity problem.

Decide: Make platform and design decisions that have multi-million dollar implications — and own those decisions even when they are difficult.

Lead: Develop and mentor an IAM team while managing vendor relationships and executive expectations simultaneously.

The technical knowledge is a prerequisite. These five capabilities are the job.

Mentor · Former CISO — Three Things I Look For Before Promoting

I want to give you something practical before we leave this section.

Three signals I look for when considering promoting an IAM Engineer to IAM Architect:

One: They have stopped asking what to build and started asking why we should build it. An Architect thinks about business outcomes first, technical solutions second.

Two: They have developed a genuine relationship with at least one team outside of IAM — usually SOC, IR, or Security Architecture — and are seen as a trusted partner by that team. Not just someone who fulfills tickets.

Three: They have presented at least once to the CISO or an executive stakeholder and received positive feedback. Not a technical deep-dive — a business communication. If they can do that once, they can do it every month.

Technical expertise opens the door. Organizational navigation builds the career.
