WHERE WE BEGIN

3:17 AM. The alert fires.

REAL SCENARIO
The Night The SOC Saved Everything
It was 3:17 AM on a Tuesday. A Tier 1 SOC analyst — six months into the job, working her third overnight shift of the week — saw an alert that 99% of people in her position would have dismissed as a false positive. Unusual authentication pattern. A service account logging in from an IP it had never used before. At 3 AM. She did not dismiss it. She escalated it to Tier 2. Tier 2 started pulling logs. Within 20 minutes, they confirmed it: a nation-state actor had compromised a service account and was moving laterally toward our financial systems. Threat Intelligence was woken up. Incident Response was activated. IAM revoked the compromised account's access. Total time from alert to containment: 47 minutes. Estimated damage prevented: $23 million. That analyst's name was Maria. She had been in cybersecurity for six months. I gave her a promotion the next morning. Because what she did that night is exactly what we build these teams to do.
MENTOR THINKING

The SOC is not a room full of screens. It is a team of humans trained to notice what does not belong — and to act on it when everyone else is asleep.

MENTOR

I want you to understand something before we go inside each team.


These four teams — SOC, IR, Threat Intelligence, Threat Hunting — are not separate departments that happen to work near each other.


They are one integrated defensive system.


Each team feeds the others. Each team depends on the others. And every single one of them depends on IAM in ways we will make explicit as we go.


Let us start with the team that never sleeps.

TEAM ONE

The Security Operations Center.

They watch. All day. All night. Every day.

MENTOR

Let me introduce you to the SOC.


Not the technology. The organization.


The SOC is a team of analysts organized into tiers, working in shifts that cover 24 hours a day, 365 days a year.


Their job is to monitor everything the enterprise does — every login, every file access, every network connection — and identify the signals that indicate an attack is in progress.


They are the nerve center of the entire defensive operation.

LEARNER

When you say tiers — what does that mean in practice? Is it a seniority thing?

MENTOR

Seniority is part of it. But it is really about depth of investigation.


Tier 1 is the first line. They see every alert. Their job is triage: is this real or a false positive? They work fast. They handle volume.


Tier 2 goes deeper. When Tier 1 escalates, Tier 2 investigates. They pull logs, correlate events, and determine the scope and severity.


Tier 3 are the senior hunters. They handle the most complex cases, develop new detection rules, and work directly with IR when an incident is confirmed.


Think of it like your engineering on-call rotations — except the on-call never ends, and the incidents have regulatory implications.

LEARNER

That actually makes a lot of sense. Like L1, L2, L3 support — but for attacks.

MENTOR

Exactly the right analogy.

TEAM TWO

Incident Response.

They do not prevent fires. They put them out.

MENTOR

I want to be precise about something that confuses a lot of people.


The SOC detects. IR responds.


Those are different jobs. Different skills. Different mindsets.


The SOC is a monitoring operation. They watch. They triage. They escalate.


IR is an emergency response operation. When they get the call, something is already wrong. Their job is to limit the damage — fast.

LEARNER

So the SOC is like a monitoring system and IR is like the on-call incident commander who takes over when things go critical?

MENTOR

That is an excellent analogy and I am going to steal it.


The IR team is your incident commander. They declare the incident, they run the bridge, they coordinate every team, and they own the post-incident report.


The key difference from your engineering incident response: when IR declares an incident, they may be dealing with an adversary who is actively working against them in real time.

SOC
  • Continuous monitoring, 24/7
  • Alert triage and initial investigation
  • Escalates confirmed incidents to IR
  • Owns detection, not response
HANDOFF
→
IR
  • Activates on confirmed incident
  • Owns containment and eradication
  • Coordinates all response teams
  • Owns post-incident review
THE INCIDENT LIFECYCLE
MENTOR

Let me show you how IR thinks about an incident from beginning to end.


There are six stages. Every IR team uses some version of this model.

1
Detection
Alert from SOC or external source. IR is activated. Clock starts now.
2
Triage
Scope the incident. What is affected? How far has the attacker moved? What is the business impact?
3
Containment
Stop the bleeding. Isolate affected systems. IAM revokes compromised credentials. Attacker is cut off.
IAM is activated here —
revoking access is the fastest
way to stop lateral movement.
4
Eradication
Remove the attacker. Delete malware. Close the entry point. Verify the environment is clean.
5
Recovery
Restore systems. Bring operations back online. Monitor closely for re-entry attempts.
6
Lessons Learned
Post-incident review. Root cause analysis. Improvements to detection, response, and controls. Report to CISO and Board.
REAL SCENARIO
The 47-Minute Containment
Back to Maria's alert. When Tier 2 confirmed the breach and escalated to IR, here is what happened in the next 47 minutes: Minutes 0–8: IR declares the incident. Incident commander identified. Bridge opened. CISO notified. Legal notified. Minutes 8–19: Scope assessment. IR traces the compromised service account's access path through the network. Seven systems touched. No data exfiltration confirmed yet. Minutes 19–31: Containment activated. IAM revokes the compromised service account. All sessions terminated. Affected systems isolated. Attacker loses access. Minutes 31–47: Eradication begins. Malware identified on two systems. Removal process initiated. Entry point (unpatched VPN) identified. 47 minutes. The attacker had been inside for eleven minutes before Maria's alert. Without a functioning IR process, those eleven minutes could have become eleven weeks.

TEAM THREE

Threat Intelligence.

They study the enemy so the enemy cannot surprise them.

MENTOR

Here is a question I ask every engineer who joins a security team:


Who was behind the attack you just responded to? Why did they target you specifically? What are they likely to do next?


Most security teams cannot answer these questions.


Threat Intelligence teams can.


And that difference is worth more than almost any technical control you could implement.

LEARNER

So they are basically security researchers? People who study attackers the way a competitive intelligence team studies competitors?

MENTOR

That is exactly what they are.


And like competitive intelligence, they operate at different levels.


Strategic Intel: who are the threat actors targeting our industry? What are their motivations? This goes to the CISO and Board.


Tactical Intel: what techniques are these actors using right now? This goes to the SOC and IR teams.


Operational Intel: is there an active campaign targeting our organization? This triggers immediate defensive action.


Technical Intel: specific IP addresses, malware hashes, phishing domains. This goes directly into the SIEM as detection rules.

TEAM FOUR

Threat Hunting.

They do not wait for the alert. They go looking.

MENTOR

Every team we have talked about so far is fundamentally reactive.


The SOC reacts to alerts. IR reacts to confirmed incidents. Threat Intelligence reacts to new information.


Threat Hunting is the exception.


Hunters do not wait for an alert to fire. They start with a hypothesis — "I believe an attacker is already inside our environment, hiding from our detection tools" — and then they go looking for evidence.


They are the team that finds the attacker who has been in your network for eleven weeks before a single alert fired.

LEARNER

So they are essentially doing manual investigation before anything is confirmed? That seems like searching for a needle in a very large haystack.

MENTOR

It is. And the good hunters are extraordinary at it.


They combine deep knowledge of attacker techniques with deep knowledge of what normal looks like in a specific environment.


The moment they see something that is technically normal but contextually wrong — like a service account authenticating at 3 AM from an unusual IP address — they pull the thread.


Does that scenario sound familiar?

MENTOR THINKING

Maria was doing instinctive threat hunting. She was not reacting to a high-priority alert. She was asking: why does this look almost normal but not quite? That question is what separates great analysts from good ones.

Reactive Security
  • Waits for alert to fire
  • Responds to known signatures
  • Alert → Investigate → Respond
  • Finds threats matching known patterns
  • Misses attackers who avoid detection tools
↕
Threat Hunting
  • Assumes breach is already present
  • Starts with hypothesis
  • Hypothesis → Hunt → Discover → Respond
  • Finds threats designed to evade alerts
  • Catches what the SOC missed
KEY INSIGHT

The average dwell time — how long an attacker lives inside a network before detection — is 197 days in organizations without active Threat Hunting programs. In organizations with mature Threat Hunting: under 24 hours. That difference is measured in millions of dollars and in whether a breach becomes a breach or becomes a headline.
