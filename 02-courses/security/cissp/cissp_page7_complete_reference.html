🏛️ PKI — Full Architecture
Root CA
The ultimate trust anchor. Offline and heavily protected. Signs Intermediate CAs only. Root CA compromise = catastrophic — entire PKI must be rebuilt.
Intermediate CA (ICA)
Online, signs end-entity certificates. If compromised, only its issued certificates need revocation — Root CA remains trusted.
End-Entity Certificate
Issued to servers, users, or devices. Contains public key, identity, validity period, CA signature, and permitted uses (Key Usage extension).
RA — Registration Authority
Verifies requestor identity before the CA issues a certificate. Handles the vetting process without having signing authority.
CRL vs OCSP Revocation
CRL (Certificate Revocation List): large downloadable list, checked periodically — outdated by the time it is downloaded. OCSP (Online Certificate Status Protocol): real-time single-certificate status check — preferred. OCSP Stapling: server pre-fetches its own OCSP response and includes it in TLS handshake — eliminates client round-trip.
🔑 Key Management Lifecycle
1
Generation
Keys generated using cryptographically secure random number generators (CSPRNG). Key length must meet minimum standards (AES-256, RSA-2048+).
2
Distribution
Keys exchanged securely using asymmetric encryption or key wrap. Symmetric keys never transmitted in plaintext. PKI used for authentication during exchange.
3
Storage
Keys stored in HSMs (Hardware Security Modules) or key management systems. Never stored in plaintext. Key encryption keys (KEKs) used to encrypt data encryption keys.
4
Rotation
Keys changed on a regular schedule or after a suspected compromise. Rotation limits the amount of data exposed if a key is later discovered to be compromised.
5
Escrow & Recovery
Encryption keys must be backed up securely (escrow) so encrypted data can be recovered if the key is lost. Key escrow = third party holds a copy for emergency recovery.
6
Destruction
Cryptographic erasure: securely delete all key copies so data is permanently unreadable. Zeroisation: overwrite key memory with zeros immediately after use.
🖼️ Steganography

Steganography hides secret data inside ordinary-looking content — images, audio, video, documents. Unlike encryption (which hides the meaning), steganography hides the very existence of the message. The hidden message is invisible to casual observation.

CISSP Exam Points
Steganalysis = detecting steganography. Steganography is often combined with encryption for double protection. Used by APTs for covert data exfiltration through image files uploaded to cloud storage. Watermarking is a related technique for copyright protection.
⚛️ Quantum Cryptography Threats

Quantum computers threaten current asymmetric cryptography. Shor's algorithm can break RSA and ECC by factoring large numbers exponentially faster than classical computers. Grover's algorithm effectively halves the security of symmetric keys.

At risk: RSA, ECC, Diffie-Hellman — broken by Shor's algorithm
Resistant: AES-256 (Grover halves security → AES-256 effectively becomes AES-128 equivalent — still secure). SHA-3 hashing is also quantum-resistant.
Post-Quantum: NIST standardised post-quantum algorithms in 2024 — CRYSTALS-Kyber (key exchange) and CRYSTALS-Dilithium (signatures). These are lattice-based and resist quantum attacks.

Laws & Regulations Master Guide

Every major law and regulation tested on CISSP — jurisdiction, scope, key requirements, and the penalties that make them matter. Know these cold.

GDPR
General Data Protection Regulation
EU regulation protecting personal data of EU residents — applies to ANY organisation worldwide that processes EU resident data, regardless of where the organisation is located.
Key: 72-hour breach notification to supervisory authority · Right to erasure · Data portability · Privacy by design · DPO required for large-scale processing · Lawful basis for processing required
Max penalty: €20M or 4% of global annual turnover — whichever is higher
HIPAA
Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act
US federal law protecting Protected Health Information (PHI). Applies to covered entities (healthcare providers, health plans, clearinghouses) and their business associates who handle PHI.
Key: Privacy Rule (PHI use and disclosure) · Security Rule (administrative, physical, technical safeguards) · Breach Notification Rule (60 days to notify individuals and HHS) · Business Associate Agreements (BAAs) required
Penalties: $100 to $50,000 per violation · Max $1.9M per violation category per year
SOX
Sarbanes-Oxley Act
US federal law requiring accurate financial reporting for publicly traded companies. Section 404 mandates internal controls over financial reporting. IT security controls that affect financial data are in scope.
Key: Section 302 — CEO/CFO personal certification of financial statements · Section 404 — internal control assessment · Access controls on financial systems · Audit trails for financial data · Change management for financial systems
Criminal penalties: up to $5M fines and 20 years imprisonment for executives
PCI-DSS
Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard
Industry standard (not a law) enforced by payment card brands. Required for any organisation that stores, processes, or transmits cardholder data. 12 main requirements across 6 control objectives.
Key 12 Requirements: Build/maintain secure network · Protect cardholder data · Vulnerability management program · Access control · Monitor/test networks · Information security policy · Quarterly external vulnerability scans · Annual penetration test
Non-compliance penalties: $5,000–$100,000/month + potential loss of card processing ability
FISMA
Federal Information Security Management Act
US federal law requiring all US federal agencies and contractors to implement information security programs. Based on NIST standards and guidelines. FISMA compliance uses NIST SP 800-53 controls.
Key: Annual security reviews · Risk categorisation (FIPS 199) · Security controls (NIST SP 800-53) · Continuous monitoring · Plan of Action and Milestones (POA&M) · FedRAMP for cloud services used by federal agencies
Non-compliance can result in loss of federal funding and contract disqualification
CFAA
Computer Fraud and Abuse Act
US federal criminal law addressing computer-related crimes. Makes it illegal to access a computer without authorisation or exceed authorised access. Frequently cited in cybercrime prosecutions and security research debates.
Key: Unauthorised access is a federal crime · Exceeding authorised access is also covered · Applies to any "protected computer" (interstate commerce or government) · Important for penetration testing — written authorisation is legally critical · Bug bounty programmes have legal implications under CFAA
Criminal penalties: 1–20 years imprisonment depending on severity and intent
COPPA
Children's Online Privacy Protection Act
US federal law protecting the online privacy of children under 13. Requires verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information from children. Applies to operators of commercial websites and online services directed to children.
Key: Parental consent required before data collection from under-13s · Privacy notice must be posted prominently · Right to review and delete child's data · No conditioning of participation on disclosure of more information than necessary · FTC enforces COPPA
Civil penalties: up to $51,744 per violation
DMCA
Digital Millennium Copyright Act
US copyright law implementing two 1996 WIPO treaties. Criminalises production and dissemination of technology or devices designed to circumvent copyright protection measures (DRM). Important for security researchers working with protected systems.
Key: Anti-circumvention provisions (Section 1201) — cannot bypass DRM even for legal purposes · Safe harbour for ISPs and online platforms (Section 512) · Security research exemptions exist but are narrow · Reverse engineering for interoperability has limited exceptions
Criminal penalties: up to $500,000 fine and 5 years imprisonment for first offence

All Security Models Compared

Every security model tested on CISSP — side by side, with rules, use cases, and the single-sentence memory aid for each one.

🔒
Bell-LaPadula Model
CONFIDENTIALITY MODEL · Military/Government
Rule 1
No Read Up (Simple Security Property) — A subject cannot read an object with a higher classification than their clearance. A Secret-cleared user cannot read Top Secret data.
Rule 2
No Write Down (Star * Property) — A subject cannot write to an object with a lower classification. A Top Secret user cannot write to a Confidential file (prevents downward information flow).
Rule 3
Discretionary Security Property — Access matrix governs discretionary access between objects at the same level.
🎯 Memory Aid: "No Read Up, No Write Down" — Information flows UPWARD only. Protects CONFIDENTIALITY. Use when you see: military classification, government, preventing leakage from high to low.
🛡️
Biba Model
INTEGRITY MODEL · Commercial
Rule 1
No Write Up (Star * Integrity Property) — A lower-integrity subject cannot write to a higher-integrity object. Prevents corruption of high-integrity data by untrusted sources.
Rule 2
No Read Down (Simple Integrity Property) — A higher-integrity subject cannot read from a lower-integrity object. Prevents contamination of trusted processes with untrusted data.
Rule 3
Invocation Property — A subject cannot invoke (call/use) a subject at a higher integrity level.
🎯 Memory Aid: "No Write Up, No Read Down" — Integrity flows DOWNWARD only. Protects INTEGRITY. Biba is the OPPOSITE direction from Bell-LaPadula.
💼
Clark-Wilson Model
INTEGRITY MODEL · Commercial/Banking
Key Concept
Access Triples: Subject (user) → Program (Transformation Procedure / TP) → Object (Constrained Data Item / CDI). Users can ONLY modify CDIs through approved TPs — never directly.
CDI
Constrained Data Items — High-integrity data that must be protected (e.g., financial account balances, medical records). Can only be modified by approved TPs.
IVP
Integrity Verification Procedures — Periodically check that CDIs have not been corrupted. Like a database reconciliation script that runs nightly.
🎯 Java analogy: A user can only modify an account balance (CDI) through your TransferFunds() method (TP) — never directly via a database UPDATE query. Protects INTEGRITY in commercial environments.
🧱
Brewer-Nash (Chinese Wall)
CONFLICT OF INTEREST MODEL
Core Rule
Once a subject accesses data from one company in a conflict class, they cannot access data from any competing company in the same conflict class. Access rights change dynamically based on what has been previously accessed.
Example
A consultant who has accessed Coca-Cola's financial data cannot access PepsiCo's financial data — they are in the same conflict class (cola beverages). The wall builds dynamically as access occurs.
🎯 Use when you see: conflict of interest, consultants with multiple clients, investment banking (Chinese Wall between trading and advisory), legal firms with competing clients.
⚙️
State Machine Model
FOUNDATIONAL SECURITY MODEL
Core Concept
A system is secure if it begins in a secure state, every possible transition between states is secure, and the system always arrives at a secure state. Security is evaluated as a mathematical state machine.
Application
Bell-LaPadula and Biba are both implemented as state machine models. The allowed states and transitions define what the model permits.
🎯 Foundational theory underlying most formal security models. If a question asks about the theoretical basis for formal security models, State Machine is the answer.
🪜
Lattice Model
ACCESS CONTROL MODEL
Core Concept
Subjects and objects are assigned security labels. A mathematical lattice structure defines the partial ordering between labels. Access is permitted only when the subject's label is greater than or equal to the object's label in the lattice ordering.
Application
Military classification systems use lattice ordering. Bell-LaPadula is implemented as a lattice model. Lattice bounds define the upper and lower limits of information flow between security levels.
🎯 The theoretical structure underlying Bell-LaPadula and MAC implementations. Lattice = partial ordering of security levels with defined upper and lower bounds.

Physical Security Complete Guide

Completely missing from Pages 0-6. Physical security appears on every CISSP exam. CPTED, access controls, environmental controls, and the exact exam traps you need to know.

🌿 CPTED — Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design

CPTED is a proactive design philosophy that uses the physical environment to deter criminal activity and enhance security without relying solely on guards or electronic controls. It is tested directly on CISSP as a physical security strategy.

Natural Surveillance
Design spaces so that people can observe activity naturally. Open sight lines, proper lighting, windows facing parking areas. Makes potential criminals feel watched and exposed.
Natural Access Control
Use landscaping, fencing, and pathways to guide people through designated entry points. Bollards, hedges, and walls channel movement and define clear boundaries between public and private areas.
Territorial Reinforcement
Create a clear sense of ownership and territory. Signs, paving changes, lighting, and landscaping define where public space ends and private space begins. Deters casual intrusion through psychological cues.
Target Hardening
Physical barriers that resist forced entry — reinforced doors, security glass, deadbolts, access control systems. Makes it physically difficult or time-consuming to attack the target.
Maintenance
Well-maintained environments signal that the property is actively cared for and monitored. Broken windows, graffiti, and neglect signal opportunity. The Broken Windows theory supports this CPTED principle.
Activity Support
Design spaces to attract legitimate users and activity. A busy, active space with legitimate occupants naturally deters criminal activity through the presence of people who would observe and report suspicious behaviour.
🚧
Fencing and Barriers
3-4 ft fencing: psychological deterrent only. 6-7 ft fencing: too high to climb easily. 8+ ft with barbed wire: serious deterrent. Bollards: prevent vehicle ramming attacks (critical for embassies and data centres).
Exam: Bollards protect against vehicle-born IED attacks
🔐
Mantrap / Airlock
Two-door entry system where the first door must close and lock before the second door can open. Prevents tailgating (piggybacking). Only one person enters at a time. Common in data centres and secure facilities.
Exam: Best control against tailgating attacks
📹
CCTV and Surveillance
CCTV is a DETECTIVE control — it records and detects, does not prevent. Must be combined with preventative controls. PTZ (Pan-Tilt-Zoom) cameras for active monitoring. Retention period must meet regulatory requirements.
Exam: CCTV = Detective, not Preventative
💡
Lighting
Lighting is a DETERRENT control. Well-lit areas reduce criminal opportunity. 2 foot-candles for general areas. 5 foot-candles for high-security areas. Critical for parking areas, entry points, and perimeter. Motion-activated lighting is cost-effective.
Exam: Lighting = Deterrent control category
🔒
Lock Types
Preset locks: factory-set combination. Programmable locks: code can be changed. Cipher locks: keypad entry, no physical key needed — audit trail possible. Biometric locks: fingerprint or retina entry. Electronic access control: badge/card readers with logging.
Exam: Cipher locks provide audit trail — important distinction
👮
Security Guards
Most expensive and most flexible physical security control. Can respond dynamically to incidents. Subject to human error, social engineering, and fatigue. Must be supplemented by technical controls. Two-person integrity (dual control) for critical areas.
Exam: Guards = expensive but flexible; not infallible
🔥
Fire Suppression
Water sprinklers: effective but damage IT equipment. Halon: effective but ozone-depleting — banned. FM-200: Halon replacement, safe for electronics. CO2: displaces oxygen — effective but dangerous to humans (clear the room first). Wet pipe vs dry pipe sprinkler systems.
Exam: FM-200 is the Halon replacement for data centres
⚡
Power Controls
UPS (Uninterruptible Power Supply): provides power during brief outages — minutes to hours. Generator: long-term power during extended outages. Surge protector: protects against voltage spikes. Clean power: filtered power removing interference. Dual power feeds from different substations for redundancy.
Exam: UPS = short-term; Generator = long-term power
🌡️
Environmental Controls
Temperature: data centres maintained 64-80°F (18-27°C). Humidity: 45-55% relative humidity. Too low = static electricity risk. Too high = condensation risk. Hot aisle/cold aisle design for efficient cooling. CRAC (Computer Room Air Conditioning) units for precision cooling.
Exam: Humidity too low = electrostatic discharge (ESD) risk

🔍 CASB — Cloud Access Security Broker

A CASB is a security policy enforcement point placed between cloud service consumers and cloud service providers. It extends an organisation's security policies into cloud environments that would otherwise be outside their direct control.

Visibility
Discovers all cloud services in use (including Shadow IT — unauthorised cloud apps employees use without IT approval)
Compliance
Ensures cloud usage meets regulatory requirements (GDPR, HIPAA, PCI-DSS) — identifies where sensitive data is stored in the cloud
Data Security
Applies DLP policies, encryption, and access controls to cloud-stored data across sanctioned and unsanctioned services
Threat Protection
Detects anomalous user behaviour in cloud applications — compromised accounts, insider threats, malware distribution
☁️ Cloud Security Key Concepts
FedRAMP
US federal government cloud security authorisation programme. Cloud providers must receive FedRAMP authorisation before US federal agencies can use their services. Based on NIST SP 800-53 controls.
CSA STAR
Cloud Security Alliance Security Trust Assurance and Risk. A cloud-specific certification built on ISO 27001 plus the CSA Cloud Controls Matrix (CCM). Three levels: Self-Assessment, Third-Party Audit, Continuous Monitoring.
Shadow IT
Cloud services used by employees without IT department knowledge or approval. A major data governance risk — company data may be stored in unapproved services outside the organisation's security controls. CASB is the primary detection and control tool.
Cloud Data Residency
GDPR and some national laws require that personal data of their citizens be stored within specific geographic boundaries. Organisations must verify their cloud provider's data centre locations and ensure compliance with data residency requirements.
Cloud Forensics
Investigating incidents in cloud environments is challenging because the customer does not have physical access to hardware, logs may be held by the provider, and multi-tenancy means data could theoretically be co-located with other customers' data.

Malware & Attack Types Complete Reference

Every malware type, social engineering technique, and network attack you need to know — with definitions, distinguishing characteristics, and countermeasures.

🦠 Malware Types — Know Every One
Virus
Attaches itself to legitimate executable files and requires human action to spread (running the infected file). Replicates by inserting copies of itself into other programs. Classic file infectors, boot sector viruses, macro viruses.
Countermeasure: Antivirus with signature and behavioural detection. User training to avoid untrusted executables.
Worm
Self-replicating malware that spreads automatically across networks without requiring human action or a host file. Exploits network vulnerabilities to propagate. WannaCry, Conficker, Stuxnet were worms. Can cause massive network congestion.
Countermeasure: Patch management (worms exploit known vulnerabilities), network segmentation, IPS with worm detection signatures.
Trojan Horse
Malware disguised as legitimate software. Unlike viruses and worms, Trojans do NOT self-replicate. User is tricked into running the malicious code. Trojans can install backdoors, keyloggers, or remote access tools (RATs) once executed.
Countermeasure: Application whitelisting, user training, code signing, download from trusted sources only.
Rootkit
Malware designed to gain administrative/root access and hide its presence from the operating system. Modifies OS components to conceal files, processes, and registry entries. Extremely difficult to detect and remove. Bootkit = rootkit targeting the boot sector.
Countermeasure: Secure Boot, TPM, integrity checking, offline scanning. Often requires OS reinstall for complete remediation.
Ransomware
Encrypts victim's files and demands payment (usually cryptocurrency) for the decryption key. Modern ransomware (double extortion) also exfiltrates data and threatens to publish it. Delivered via phishing, RDP exploits, or supply chain attacks.
Countermeasure: Immutable offline backups (3-2-1 rule), patch management, email filtering, EDR, least privilege (limits blast radius).
Spyware
Secretly monitors user activity and collects information — keystrokes, screenshots, browsing history, credentials — without the user's knowledge or consent. Often bundled with legitimate software downloads. Stalkerware is a related category targeting intimate partner surveillance.
Countermeasure: Anti-spyware tools, application whitelisting, user awareness, monitoring for unusual data exfiltration patterns.
Keylogger
Records every keystroke typed — captures passwords, credit card numbers, and confidential communications. Can be software-based (malware) or hardware-based (physical device attached between keyboard and computer). Hardware keyloggers are invisible to software scanning.
Countermeasure: Physical inspection of workstations, software keylogger detection, MFA (captured password alone insufficient), virtual keyboards for sensitive input.
Botnet / Bot
A network of compromised computers (bots/zombies) controlled by an attacker (bot herder) through a command-and-control (C2) server. Used for DDoS attacks, spam campaigns, cryptocurrency mining, and credential stuffing. Individual bots may show no obvious symptoms.
Countermeasure: Endpoint protection, network monitoring for C2 traffic, DNS filtering, takedown of C2 infrastructure.
Logic Bomb
Malicious code that is dormant until a specific condition is met — a date/time, a specific user action, or the deletion of an employee record. Often planted by insider threats (disgruntled employees). Designed to cause damage at a specific trigger point.
Countermeasure: Code review, mandatory access controls on code deployment, monitoring for scheduled tasks, insider threat detection programme.
Fileless Malware
Operates entirely in memory without writing files to disk — making signature-based detection ineffective. Uses legitimate system tools (PowerShell, WMI, certutil) in Living-off-the-Land (LotL) attacks. Extremely difficult to detect and leaves minimal forensic artefacts.
Countermeasure: Behavioural-based EDR, memory scanning, PowerShell logging and constrained language mode, application control.
🎭 Social Engineering Attacks
🎣 Digital Social Engineering
Phishing
Mass email attack impersonating legitimate entities to steal credentials or deliver malware. Generic, sent to many targets simultaneously.
Spear Phishing
Targeted phishing against a specific individual or organisation. Uses personalised details (name, job title, recent events) to increase credibility.
Whaling
Spear phishing targeting senior executives (C-suite). High value targets with access to financial systems and sensitive data.
Vishing
Voice phishing — impersonating IT support, banks, or government agencies over the phone to extract information or credentials.
Smishing
SMS phishing — malicious links or requests delivered via text message. Often impersonates package delivery services or banks.
Pharming
Redirecting users from legitimate websites to fraudulent ones by poisoning DNS or modifying the hosts file. User types correct URL but lands on attacker's site.
🚪 Physical Social Engineering
Tailgating
Following an authorised person through a secure door without presenting credentials. Attacker walks closely behind to enter before the door closes. Prevented by mantraps.
Piggybacking
Similar to tailgating, but the authorised person is aware and knowingly allows the unauthorised person to enter — a policy violation even when done out of politeness.
Pretexting
Creating a fabricated scenario (pretext) to manipulate a target into providing information. Example: impersonating IT support or auditors to gain access or extract data.
Baiting
Leaving infected USB drives or other media in places where targets will find them. Relies on human curiosity. The target plugs in the device, infecting their computer.
Quid Pro Quo
Offering something of value (technical help, prizes) in exchange for information or access. "I'll fix your computer if you give me your login credentials temporarily."
Dumpster Diving
Searching through discarded materials — printouts, old drives, directories, organisation charts — to find sensitive information. Prevented by shredding policies.
🌐 Network Attack Types
Denial of Service (DoS/DDoS) Attacks
SYN Flood
Sends thousands of TCP SYN packets without completing the 3-way handshake. Server's half-open connection table fills up — legitimate connections refused. Mitigate with SYN cookies.
Smurf Attack
Spoofs victim's IP as source and sends ICMP echo requests to a network broadcast address. All hosts on the network reply to the victim, overwhelming it with traffic.
Fraggle Attack
Same amplification concept as Smurf but uses UDP packets (port 7 echo or port 19 chargen) instead of ICMP. Also spoofs victim's IP address.
Ping of Death
Sends malformed oversized ICMP packets exceeding the 65,535 byte maximum. When reassembled, the packet causes a buffer overflow or system crash. Largely patched in modern systems.
Teardrop
Sends overlapping fragmented IP packets that cannot be properly reassembled. Causes the target system to crash during the reassembly process.
Man-in-the-Middle & Spoofing Attacks
ARP Poisoning / Spoofing
Sends fake ARP replies associating the attacker's MAC address with a legitimate IP address. Allows the attacker to intercept traffic between hosts on the same network segment.
DNS Cache Poisoning
Injects false DNS records into a DNS resolver's cache — redirecting users to malicious sites when they type the correct URL. DNSSEC prevents this attack by cryptographically signing DNS records.
IP Spoofing
Sends packets with a forged source IP address to impersonate another host or to hide the attacker's true location. Used in DoS amplification attacks and session hijacking.
Session Hijacking
Steals or predicts a valid session token to take over an authenticated session without knowing the user's credentials. Prevent with HTTPS (TLS), secure/httpOnly cookie flags, and short session timeouts.
War Driving
Driving around with a laptop and wireless adaptor to discover and map unprotected WiFi networks. Used to identify targets for wireless attacks. War walking / war flying are similar techniques.

BCP & DRP Complete Guide

Business Impact Analysis, all testing methods, plan components, and the key metrics. This is the full depth CISSP exams on BCP and DRP.

📊 Business Impact Analysis (BIA) — Step by Step
1
Identify Critical Business Functions
List all business processes and rank them by criticality. Which functions must continue for the organisation to survive? Which can be suspended for hours? Days? Weeks?
Output: Prioritised list of business functions
2
Identify Dependencies
For each critical function, identify what it depends on — IT systems, vendors, personnel, facilities, utilities, suppliers. A function is only as resilient as its weakest dependency.
Output: Dependency map for each critical function
3
Calculate Maximum Tolerable Downtime (MTD)
The absolute maximum time a business function can be offline before the organisation suffers irreversible harm — financial ruin, contract breach, regulatory penalty, or reputational collapse. MTD > RTO always.
Output: MTD per critical function (hours/days)
4
Set Recovery Time Objective (RTO)
The targeted time within which a business function must be restored after a disruption. RTO must be less than MTD — you must recover before the business suffers irreversible harm. RTO drives DR site selection (hot/warm/cold).
Output: RTO per critical function (e.g., 4 hours)
5
Set Recovery Point Objective (RPO)
The maximum amount of data loss (measured in time) the organisation can tolerate. If RPO is 1 hour, backups must be taken at least every hour. RPO drives backup frequency and replication strategy.
Output: RPO per critical function (e.g., 15 minutes)
6
Identify Financial and Operational Impacts
Quantify the cost of each hour/day of downtime per critical function. Include: lost revenue, SLA breach penalties, regulatory fines, recovery costs, and reputational damage. This data justifies the BCP investment cost.
Output: Cost-per-hour-of-downtime per function
🧪 BCP/DRP Testing Methods — Least to Most Disruptive
📋
Checklist Review
Team members individually review the BCP/DRP documentation to verify it is complete, current, and accurate. No simulation involved. Identifies outdated information and missing procedures. Lowest effort and cost.
Disruption: None
🗣️
Tabletop Exercise
Key stakeholders discuss a simulated disaster scenario around a table. No systems are actually moved or activated. Tests decision-making, communication, and plan completeness. Identifies gaps in roles and responsibilities.
Disruption: Minimal
🚶
Walk-Through Drill
Team members physically walk through their recovery procedures step by step — without activating actual systems. Validates that personnel know their roles and that procedures are practical and complete.
Disruption: Minimal
⚡
Simulation Test
A realistic disaster scenario is simulated and teams respond as if it were real — but production systems are not actually failed over. Tests the full response process without actual disruption to services.
Disruption: Low
🔄
Parallel Test
Recovery systems are activated and brought online at the alternate site while production systems continue running. Validates that the DR site can actually support operations — without cutting over from production. Safe but expensive.
Disruption: Moderate
🔀
Full Interruption Test
Production systems are actually shut down and operations are fully transferred to the DR site — a real failover. Most realistic and most thorough test. Highest risk — if the DR site fails, the organisation has no production capability.
Disruption: Full

MTDMaximum Tolerable Downtime — business death threshold
RTORecovery Time Objective — must be less than MTD
RPORecovery Point Objective — max data loss in time

MTTRMean Time To Repair — average time to fix a failed component
MTBFMean Time Between Failures — average lifespan before failure
📁 BCP Plan Components
Business Continuity Plan (BCP)
High-level strategy for maintaining business operations during any disruption. Covers all recovery strategies including people, processes, and technology.
Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP)
IT-focused subset of BCP. Covers specific procedures for restoring IT systems, data, and infrastructure after a disaster. Includes RTO/RPO targets per system.
Incident Response Plan (IRP)
Covers the immediate response to security incidents (PICERL). Scope is narrower than DRP — focused on security events rather than all disasters.
Crisis Communications Plan
Defines how to communicate with employees, customers, media, regulators, and stakeholders during a crisis. Designates spokespersons and pre-approved messaging.
Vital Records Programme
Identifies and protects critical business records that are essential for recovery. Includes contracts, financial records, personnel data, and regulatory filings. Backup copies stored offsite.

Advanced Exam Techniques

Keyword spotting, multi-select strategy, eliminating distractors systematically, and the CAT pacing formula. Everything Page 4 did not cover.

Technique 01
🔍 CISSP Keyword Spotting
CISSP questions contain trigger keywords that change the correct answer. Spot these before reading the answer options — they determine the decision framework you apply.
FIRST → Most immediate action, follows the framework sequence (PICERL, risk process)
BEST / MOST → Most comprehensive, strategic, or risk-balanced answer
IMPORTANT → Addresses the root cause or foundational issue, not a symptom
NEXT → The immediately following step in the applicable framework or process
ALWAYS/NEVER → Absolute answers — usually wrong in CISSP (few absolutes in security)
Technique 02
☑️ Multi-Select (Select All That Apply) Strategy
CISSP CAT includes multi-select questions where you must choose ALL correct answers. These are harder because partial credit is not awarded — you must get every correct option and avoid every wrong option.
Step-by-Step Strategy
1. Read each option INDEPENDENTLY — ask "Is this TRUE on its own?" without comparing to other options.

2. Use domain knowledge to mark each option clearly: Definitely Yes / Definitely No / Unsure.

3. For "Unsure" options: apply the Manager Mindset. Does this align with what a security professional would actually implement?

4. Never choose an option just because it "sounds security-ish" — it must be specifically correct for the scenario described.

5. If you have 3 definite Yes answers and 1 Unsure, include the Unsure only if it follows security best practice fundamentals.
Technique 03
🎯 Systematic Distractor Elimination
CISSP always has one clearly correct answer, one clearly wrong answer, and two "almost correct" distractors. Systematic elimination narrows the decision to the final two.
Eliminate First: Absolute Answers
Options containing "always", "never", "immediately shut down", "deny all" — CISSP rewards balanced responses. Absolutes are almost always wrong.
Eliminate Second: Technical/Implementation Answers
Options that say "write code to fix it", "configure the firewall", or "patch immediately" — CISSP rewards governance and strategic responses over technical implementation.
Choose: Most Comprehensive + Strategic
Between the final two options, choose the answer that is broader in scope, more proactive, and addresses the root cause rather than the immediate symptom.
Technique 04
⏱️ CAT Pacing Formula
Precise time management ensures you never run out of time and never rush important decisions.
Target per question~80 seconds

NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)

The NIST CSF organises security activities into 5 core functions that together form a continuous lifecycle. It is widely adopted by private sector organisations and is the most referenced security framework in the US. CISSP tests knowledge of each function and what controls belong in each.

ID
Identify
Asset management, risk assessment, governance, business environment understanding. Know what you have before you can protect it.
PR
Protect
Access controls, data security, protective technology, awareness training, maintenance. Implement safeguards to limit impact of a potential event.
DE
Detect
Anomalies and events, continuous monitoring, detection processes. Identify the occurrence of a cybersecurity event in a timely manner.
RS
Respond
Response planning, communications, analysis, mitigation, improvements. Take action regarding a detected cybersecurity incident.
RC
Recover
Recovery planning, improvements, communications. Maintain resilience plans and restore capabilities impaired due to a cybersecurity incident.
🧠 Memory: I Protect Detecting Resilient Communities → Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover
NIST Risk Management Framework (RMF)

The NIST RMF provides a 7-step process for integrating security and risk management into the system development lifecycle. Required for all US federal information systems (FISMA). CISSP tests the RMF steps and their purpose.

1
Prepare — Establish context and priorities for managing security risk across the organisation
2
Categorise — Classify the information system and data by impact level (Low/Moderate/High) using FIPS 199
3
Select — Choose appropriate security controls from NIST SP 800-53 based on system categorisation
4
Implement — Deploy the selected controls and document how they are implemented in the system
5
Assess — Evaluate whether controls are implemented correctly and operating effectively as intended
6
Authorise — Senior official formally accepts the residual risk and authorises the system to operate (ATO)
7
Monitor — Continuously monitor controls, report security status, and update the risk management process
🧠 Memory: Please Call Security And Add Monitoring → Prepare, Categorise, Select, Assess, Authorise, Monitor
📜 Security Governance Document Hierarchy

CISSP tests the hierarchy of security governance documents — specifically: what each type is, who creates it, who must follow it, and how mandatory it is. Many candidates confuse these. Know the distinction cold.

POLICY
High-level management statement of intent — MANDATORY
Created by executive management (CISO, Board). Defines the overall security direction. Non-specific — does not say HOW. Example: "All sensitive data must be encrypted at rest." Everyone in the organisation must comply. Failure to comply = disciplinary action.
STANDARD
Specific mandatory requirements supporting the policy — MANDATORY
Created by security team. Specifies exactly WHAT must be done to comply with the policy. Example: "All sensitive data at rest must use AES-256 encryption." Mandatory compliance — not optional. Standards translate policy into specific, measurable requirements.
BASELINE
Minimum security configuration for specific systems — MANDATORY
Specifies the minimum acceptable security configuration for a specific type of system. Example: "All Linux servers must have SSH root login disabled, firewall enabled, and automatic security patches applied." Baselines implement standards at the system configuration level.
GUIDELINE
Recommended best practices — OPTIONAL / Advisory
Provides recommended approaches and best practices. NOT mandatory — staff may deviate if they have a legitimate reason. Example: "It is recommended that developers use a password manager and enable 2FA on their personal devices." Guidelines offer flexibility where mandates would be impractical.
PROCEDURE
Step-by-step instructions for specific tasks — MANDATORY where applicable
The most granular document. Provides exact step-by-step instructions for performing a specific security task. Example: "Incident Response Procedure: Step 1 — Call the SOC hotline at extension 4499. Step 2 — Do not power off any affected systems. Step 3 — Preserve logs by..." Procedures implement baselines and standards operationally.
🧠 Memory: Policy Sets, Standards Specify, Baselines Build, Guidelines Guide, Procedures Perform — Mandatory: Policy → Standard → Baseline → Procedure. Optional: Guideline only.
👁️ Biometrics — FAR, FRR, CER, and Authentication Types
📊 Biometric Error Rates — The Three Critical Metrics
FAR — False Acceptance Rate
Rate at which an unauthorised person is incorrectly ACCEPTED as legitimate. An attacker impersonates a legitimate user and the system grants access. FAR = security risk. Lower FAR = more secure system. A system with FAR of 0.001% incorrectly accepts 1 in 100,000 unauthorised attempts.
Also called: Type II Error / False Match Rate (FMR)
FRR — False Rejection Rate
Rate at which a legitimate authorised user is incorrectly REJECTED by the system. A real employee tries to scan their fingerprint and is told "access denied" because the scan did not match. FRR = usability problem. Higher FRR = frustrated users. A system with FRR of 5% rejects 1 in 20 legitimate attempts.
Also called: Type I Error / False Non-Match Rate (FNMR)
CER — Crossover Error Rate
The point at which FAR equals FRR — the crossover point on a graph where the two error rate lines intersect. CER is the single most important metric for comparing biometric systems. LOWER CER = BETTER overall biometric accuracy. A system with CER of 1% is better than one with CER of 5%. Use CER to compare systems: lower CER wins.
Also called: Equal Error Rate (EER) — Lower is ALWAYS better
🧠 Key relationship: Adjusting sensitivity moves FAR and FRR in OPPOSITE directions. Increase sensitivity → FAR decreases (more secure) but FRR increases (less usable). The CER is the optimal balance point.
🔬 Biometric Types — From Weakest to Strongest
🖊️
Signature Dynamics
Behavioural — captures speed, pressure, and rhythm of signing. Easy to spoof with practice. Lowest assurance level. Used for low-security document signing.
⌨️
Keystroke Dynamics
Behavioural — typing rhythm and speed pattern. Can be used for continuous authentication. Affected by illness, injury, or emotional state changes.
🗣️
Voice Recognition
Physiological and behavioural. Affected by illness (cold, laryngitis). Vulnerable to recordings of the authorised user's voice.
👁️
Facial Recognition
Physiological. Non-intrusive, passive. Affected by ageing, lighting changes, and cosmetic changes. 3D facial recognition is significantly more accurate than 2D.
☝️
Fingerprint
Physiological. Most widely deployed biometric. Low CER. Can be affected by cuts, burns, or dry skin. Latent prints can be lifted and used for spoof attacks.
🫀
Hand Geometry
Physiological — shape of the hand and fingers. Common in physical access control (time and attendance). Relatively low accuracy compared to iris and fingerprint.
👁️
Iris / Retina Scan — MOST ACCURATE
Iris: unique pattern in the coloured part of the eye. Retina: unique pattern of blood vessels at back of the eye. Both have extremely low CER. Retina scanning requires close proximity and bright light — uncomfortable. Iris scanning is more user-friendly. Both are the most accurate biometric types available.
📚 SETA — Security Education, Training & Awareness

SETA is the programme that ensures all employees understand their security responsibilities. Domain 1 tests this as a management control. CISSP distinguishes between three levels of SETA delivery:

Awareness
Broadest level — all employees. Simple reminders, posters, emails, screen savers. Goal: change behaviour to reduce risk. Example: "Do not click suspicious links." No deep knowledge required — recognition and behaviour change only.
Training
Role-specific — employees with specific security duties. Hands-on skill development. Example: IT staff completing incident response training. Includes exercises, labs, and role-specific procedures. Deeper than awareness but narrower audience.
Education
Deepest level — security professionals and leadership. Formal education programmes, certifications (like CISSP itself), university degrees. Understanding of WHY — not just what to do. Produces security professionals who can design and govern programmes.
⚖️ eDiscovery & Legal Holds

eDiscovery is the process of identifying, collecting, and producing electronically stored information (ESI) in response to a legal request or litigation. CISSP Domain 7 tests the security professional's role in supporting the legal process.

Legal Hold
A directive to preserve all potentially relevant data when litigation is reasonably anticipated. Normal data retention schedules are SUSPENDED for held data. Deleting data under a legal hold = spoliation, which carries severe legal consequences including adverse inference by the court.
Chain of Custody in eDiscovery
All evidence collected for legal proceedings must maintain chain of custody — documented, unbroken, unmodified. Use write blockers when imaging drives. Hash every evidence file immediately. Court requires proof that evidence has not been tampered with since collection.
SOAR — Security Orchestration, Automation and Response
SOAR platforms automate repetitive security tasks and orchestrate incident response workflows. Difference from SIEM: SIEM detects and alerts. SOAR automatically responds based on predefined playbooks. Example: SOAR automatically blocks a suspicious IP when the SIEM fires a specific alert, opens a ticket, and notifies the analyst — all without human intervention.
Threat Hunting
Proactive security activity where analysts actively search for threats that have evaded automated detection. Unlike reactive SIEM alerting, threat hunting starts with a hypothesis — "I believe an attacker using technique X is present" — and searches for evidence. Requires skilled analysts and rich log data.
