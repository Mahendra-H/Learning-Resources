What IGA Is and Why It Exists

IGA stands for Identity Governance and Administration — two distinct functions that enterprise platforms like Saviynt collapse into a single system but which serve fundamentally different purposes. Understanding that distinction is the foundation of IGA architecture thinking.

Identity Administration is the operational function: provisioning accounts when employees join, re-provisioning when they move, deprovisioning when they leave, fulfilling access requests, assigning roles. It answers the question "how do we give people the right access?" Administration is about execution.

Identity Governance is the oversight function: are the right people still in the right roles? Are there SoD conflicts in the current access state? Are entitlements still appropriate for the business function they serve? Governance asks "is the access we've given still correct?" — and it asks that question continuously, not just at hire time. Governance is about accountability.

Without IGA, identity administration degrades into helpdesk ticket chaos: every access request is a manual ticket, every leaver deprovisioning is a multi-day manual process, and no one systematically reviews whether access granted six months ago is still appropriate. At IAM Maturity Level 2, this is standard operating procedure. At Level 3 and above, it is an unacceptable compliance and security gap.

The Three Audit Questions IGA Must Answer

Every SOX auditor, every HIPAA compliance review, every internal security audit reduces to three questions about identity access. Your IGA platform exists to answer them with evidence, not assertions:

  1. Who has access to what? — Entitlement visibility across all connected applications, at a point in time and historically. Saviynt's Identity Warehouse answers this.
  2. How did they get it? — The provisioning audit trail: who requested, who approved, when was it granted, through what workflow. Every access grant must have an audit record.
  3. Is it still appropriate? — Access certification evidence: who reviewed the access, when, what decision they made, what was revoked. The answer cannot be "we assume so." It must be demonstrated.

IGA vs PAM — Where the Boundary Is

IGA and PAM (Privileged Access Management) are frequently confused because they share the word "access" and some platforms (including Saviynt) offer capabilities in both domains. The primary distinction: IGA governs standard entitlements for all users across business applications — Salesforce profiles, SAP roles, GitHub organization membership, AD group assignments. PAM governs privileged access to systems and infrastructure — root credentials, domain admin accounts, database superuser passwords, service account secrets. Module 10 covers PAM in full.

The overlap region is real: Saviynt has PAM features; CyberArk has some lifecycle management capabilities. But when designing an enterprise IAM program, the distinction holds: IGA governs the broad access landscape for the entire workforce; PAM governs the high-risk access layer for the subset of users who need privileged credentials.

Saviynt vs SailPoint — Architectural Positioning

Saviynt Enterprise Identity Cloud (EIC) is cloud-native SaaS-first, with a strong unified platform for IGA + PAM + Application GRC. Its architecture favors modern API-first integration, out-of-box analytics, and a connector framework built for cloud applications. It is the right choice for organizations moving toward cloud-first identity infrastructure.

SailPoint IdentityNow (SaaS) and IdentityIQ (on-premises) have a longer enterprise pedigree, a larger pre-built connector library, and stronger capabilities in file access governance (unstructured data entitlements). SailPoint is the right choice for organizations with heavy on-premises infrastructure, complex legacy connector requirements, or existing SailPoint investments.

The decision is rarely purely technical — it is influenced by existing contracts, implementation partner availability, and organizational cloud readiness. The architectural principles in this module apply to both platforms; Saviynt is used as the primary reference because it is your current stack.

Joiner-Mover-Leaver Architecture

The JML lifecycle is most cleanly understood as a state machine: an identity exists in one state at a time (Pre-hire, Active, On Leave, Transferred, Terminated, Archived), and transitions between states are triggered by authoritative events from the HR system. Each transition carries a defined set of provisioning actions that Saviynt must execute reliably, completely, and with a verifiable audit trail.

The Authoritative Source

The HR system — Workday, SAP SuccessFactors, ServiceNow HR, or equivalent — is the authoritative source for lifecycle events. This means IGA must listen to it, not replace it. When an employee is hired, the record of that hire lives in the HR system. When an employee is terminated, the termination is recorded in HR first. Saviynt's role is to detect these events (via SCIM push, REST webhook, file import, or scheduled poll) and translate them into provisioning actions across all connected systems.

This creates an important architectural dependency: the quality of IGA automation is bounded by the quality of HR data. If HR doesn't record a department transfer in the system, Saviynt never sees the Mover event, and the role delta is never computed. IGA architects must work with HR teams to ensure that HR record updates are timely, accurate, and machine-readable. The elegant technical architecture breaks down on stale HR data.

Joiner Architecture

The Joiner flow begins when HR creates a new employee record, typically before the employee's start date (pre-boarding). Saviynt detects the new record, creates the canonical identity, evaluates birthright access based on HR attributes (job code, department, location, employee type), and triggers the provisioning workflow for all systems that employee needs on day one.

Birthright access is the architectural cornerstone of good Joiner design: it defines what every employee in a given role receives automatically, without a request workflow. A Software Engineer at job code SWE-3 gets AD account, email, GitHub organization membership, and the engineering Slack workspace — without any manual requests. The fewer manual requests required on day one, the better the onboarding experience and the lower the helpdesk load.

Mover Architecture and the Access Accumulation Problem

When an employee changes roles, department, or manager, Saviynt must compute the role delta: what access does the new role require? What did the old role require? The delta determines what to add and what to remove. In practice, most IGA implementations handle the "add" side of the delta far better than the "remove" side — because adding access has few political obstacles, while removing access is often blocked by "they might still need it."

The result is access accumulation: each time an employee moves, they gain new access without losing old access. After three or four role changes over a career, the employee has accumulated access appropriate for four different roles simultaneously — a severe over-permission state that no single role justification covers. Leaver Architecture and the Deprovisioning SLA

Leaver processing is operationally simple in concept — revoke all access when an employee leaves — but architecturally critical in execution. The key dimensions are speed (how fast after termination?), completeness (are all connected systems deprovisioned?), and verification (did it actually work?). For involuntary terminations — performance-related, for cause, or security incidents — the speed requirement is measured in minutes, not hours. The account must be disabled before the employee is aware of the termination.

Saviynt's leaver workflow must be fully automated for the critical path: HR termination event → AD account disabled. Manual steps anywhere in this path create unacceptable windows. The 24-hour SLA for full application deprovisioning is a security standard, not a suggestion. Applications that require longer than 24 hours for deprovisioning (due to batch processing, manual fulfillment, or disconnected connectors) represent leaver risk that must be tracked explicitly.

Contractor and Non-Employee Lifecycle

Contractors, consultants, and temporary workers have a parallel lifecycle that most enterprises handle less rigorously than employee lifecycle. The key differences: contractors often have defined end dates (time-limited access), may not be in the primary HR system (requiring a separate identity governance track), and may have more restricted access requirements. Saviynt supports non-employee lifecycle through separate identity stores or "service identities," but the governance controls must be explicitly designed — they don't inherit from the employee model automatically.

Provisioning & Deprovisioning Architecture

Provisioning and deprovisioning are the same architectural mechanism running in opposite directions. Provisioning creates an account and grants entitlements; deprovisioning disables the account and revokes them. The connector framework, workflow engine, and audit trail are identical — the direction of the operation differs. Understanding this symmetry matters: if your provisioning architecture has gaps (slow connectors, missing applications), your deprovisioning architecture has the same gaps, with worse security consequences.

Connector Architecture

Saviynt connects to target applications through a connector framework that supports multiple integration protocols. The connector type determines what operations are available, how reliable the integration is, and what the reconciliation behavior looks like:

  • SCIM 2.0 connectors — the modern standard. Standardized REST API for user lifecycle operations. Saviynt speaks SCIM to Salesforce, GitHub, AWS IAM Identity Center, PingDirectory, Workday, and hundreds of cloud applications. Preferred for any modern SaaS application.
  • LDAP connectors — for Active Directory and OpenLDAP. Full user lifecycle, group management, and attribute sync via the LDAP protocol. The most mature connector type in Saviynt's library.
  • REST API connectors — custom HTTP integrations with applications that expose a REST API but don't implement SCIM. More brittle than SCIM (no standard schema), but covers many modern internal applications.
  • SAP connectors — specialized integration with SAP ERP for role and t-code assignment. SAP's authorization model is complex enough that it requires dedicated connector logic separate from generic REST.
  • Database connectors — direct database access for legacy applications that expose no API. The connector reads and writes user tables directly. High risk: schema changes in the application break the connector silently.
  • File-based connectors — CSV import/export for mainframe systems and legacy applications with no API surface. Batch-oriented; deprovisioning has inherent latency (next batch window).

Birthright Access and Automatic Provisioning

Birthright access is the set of entitlements every identity in a given role receives automatically, without an approval workflow, based on HR attributes at join time. The mapping rule is: HR attributes → business role → entitlements across applications. A well-designed birthright model means a new employee has everything they need on day one without a single helpdesk ticket. The design challenge is calibrating birthright to the minimum necessary access — over-broad birthright creates entitlement sprawl from day one.

JIT vs Pre-Provisioning

Just-in-time (JIT) provisioning creates an account at the moment of first login, using information from the SAML assertion (name, email, role claims) to create the account in the target application. It requires no pre-provisioning workflow — the account appears when needed. The tradeoff: JIT cannot pre-assign complex entitlements, can't provision systems that don't use SSO, and creates an account at first login regardless of whether that access has been approved through governance.

Pre-provisioning via IGA creates the account in advance, through an approved workflow, before the user ever authenticates. It supports complex entitlement assignment, full audit trails, and deprovisioning on schedule. The tradeoff: it requires a connector to every target application and adds operational overhead. The right answer is almost always pre-provisioning via IGA for critical applications and JIT as a fallback for low-sensitivity SaaS tools.

Reconciliation — The Most Underinvested Capability

Reconciliation is Saviynt's process of importing the actual current state of accounts and entitlements from target applications and comparing it to what Saviynt believes should exist. The delta reveals: orphaned accounts (accounts in the application that IGA never created), entitlement drift (entitlements that were granted manually outside IGA), and deprovisioning failures (accounts that should have been removed but weren't).

Without regular reconciliation, Saviynt's view of "who has access to what" diverges from the actual state of the environment. Certifications run against the IGA view — not against reality. This means certifications can confirm access that doesn't exist (false governance) or miss access that does exist (real risk). Reconciliation is what keeps the identity warehouse accurate.

Access Request & Approval Workflow Architecture

When birthright access doesn't cover what an employee needs, the access request workflow is the governance-controlled mechanism for granting additional entitlements. Every request that flows through Saviynt's request portal generates an immutable audit record: who asked, what they asked for, who approved, when it was granted, and how long the access lasts. This audit trail is the operational evidence that access is controlled — and it's what auditors examine.

Request Types

  • Individual entitlement request — a specific permission in a specific application. "I need the Finance-Admin profile in Salesforce." The most granular request type; appropriate for unique access needs.
  • Role request — a business role that bundles multiple entitlements. "I need the Finance-Analyst role." Preferred over individual requests because it keeps entitlements aligned to the role model. One approval for many entitlements.
  • Temporary access request — any of the above with an expiry date. The entitlement is granted, and Saviynt schedules automatic revocation at the specified date. Essential for project-based access, contractor access, and time-limited business needs.
  • Emergency access request — immediate provisioning for urgent business situations. Bypasses the standard approval chain (with required justification), grants access immediately, and triggers a mandatory post-access review. Every emergency access grant must be reviewed within 24–48 hours.

Approval Chain Architecture

The approval chain is the governance mechanism that ensures access is authorized before it is granted. Saviynt's workflow engine supports sequential approval (Step 1 must complete before Step 2 starts), parallel approval (Steps 1 and 2 can run simultaneously), and dynamic routing (approval path determined by request attributes at runtime).

The standard chain for a standard entitlement request: Line manager approval (does the requestor's work justify this access?) → Application owner approval (data custodian confirms this is appropriate for this application) → Risk/Compliance review (if the request creates an SoD conflict, it escalates before granting). The key invariant: the requestor cannot be an approver in their own request. This is Saviynt workflow configuration, not a policy document — configure it, enforce it technically.

Self-Service Portal Design

The Saviynt access request portal is the user-facing surface of the IGA system. Its quality directly determines whether users request access through governance channels or find workarounds. A portal with technical entitlement names ("SFDC_FIN_ADMIN_2024_V3") that no business user understands produces workarounds. A portal with business-readable descriptions, usage context, and risk labels produces governance compliance. Invest in entitlement catalog quality — the data that powers the portal — as a first-class governance activity.

Temporary Access and Time-Limited Entitlements

Temporary access is one of IGA's most powerful governance controls. When access has an expiry date, the access cleanup problem is solved automatically — Saviynt revokes it on schedule without human action. The architectural requirement: Saviynt's revocation scheduler must be reliable, monitored, and have failure alerting. A revocation that silently fails is worse than no expiry date at all, because it creates an expectation of automatic cleanup that didn't happen.

Access Certification Architecture

Access certification is the periodic review process where access owners — managers, application owners, role owners — confirm that existing access is still appropriate. The governance question it asks is: "Does this person still need this access for their current role?" Asked systematically, at regular intervals, with documented decisions and automated remediation of revoked items, it is the primary mechanism for preventing access creep.

Without certification, Mover events accumulate uncorrected access over time (Section 9.2). Birthright assignments that were appropriate at hire time drift from reality as the business evolves. Privileged entitlements outlive their justification. Certification campaigns are the governance intervention that resets the access state to what is actually required — not what was granted historically.

Certification Campaign Types

  • User certification — a manager reviews all access for each of their direct reports. Broad scope; appropriate for quarterly compliance cycles. Generates the largest review item counts per reviewer.
  • Entitlement certification — an application owner reviews all users who hold a specific entitlement. Focused; appropriate for high-risk entitlements like admin profiles and privileged roles. The application owner is better positioned than a manager to judge entitlement appropriateness.
  • Role certification — a role owner reviews all members of a business role. Appropriate for role quality management alongside the role engineering process.
  • Application certification — all access to a specific application reviewed. Used for application decommissioning, post-incident review, or SOX scope applications requiring complete coverage.
  • Micro-certification — targeted, triggered review of specific high-risk access or recently changed access. The most efficient form: review exactly the access that poses the greatest current risk. Used in risk-driven governance programs.

The Certification Fatigue Problem

Certification fatigue is the single most common reason governance programs fail in practice. When a manager receives 300 review items in a certification campaign — each described by an opaque technical entitlement name — the rational response is to approve all 300 to clear the queue. This rubber-stamping produces 100% completion rates and 0% governance value. The campaign ran; nothing was actually reviewed.

Good campaign design prevents fatigue: fewer than 30 items per reviewer per campaign, business-readable entitlement descriptions, risk scores on high-risk items to focus attention, and campaign scope that reflects actual risk rather than a full data dump. Governance that nobody reads is not governance.

Escalation and Auto-Handling Policies

Every certification campaign must define what happens to items that reviewers don't act on within the campaign window. Two options: auto-certify (assume access is still appropriate if not reviewed — reduces noise but is the wrong security default) or auto-revoke (assume access should be removed if not reviewed — the right security default for privileged entitlements, may cause operational disruption if overused). The policy should be entitlement-risk-sensitive: auto-revoke for high-risk entitlements, auto-certify with extended monitoring for low-risk entitlements.

Audit Evidence Requirements

SOX Section 302 and 404, HIPAA, and most regulated frameworks require documented evidence of access reviews. Saviynt's certification campaigns produce: campaign completion rate (percentage of review items acted on by a human), decision distribution (certify vs revoke breakdown), revocation rate (total access removed), and remediation evidence (deprovisioning workflow execution records for each revoked item). A certification campaign with a 95% approve rate and under 1% revocation rate will draw auditor scrutiny — it suggests rubber-stamping rather than genuine review.

Role Engineering & Role Mining

Role engineering is the deliberate process of designing, defining, and governing business roles that represent meaningful groupings of entitlements aligned to business functions. It is the answer to role explosion (covered in Module 08) — the state where role counts grow unbounded because every access need generates a new technical role rather than being mapped to an existing business role model.

A well-engineered role model has three layers: business roles that are meaningful to the business (Finance-Analyst), IT roles that map to infrastructure-level access (AD groups, distribution lists), and application roles that represent the actual entitlements in each target system (Salesforce Standard User, SAP FI Analyst role). One business role request = one approval = multiple entitlements across multiple applications, all provisioned through the single role assignment.

Top-Down vs Bottom-Up Role Design

Top-down role design starts with the organizational model: job families, departments, levels, and functions. It defines what access each business function requires and builds roles from that business-first definition. This produces roles that align perfectly with the org chart and are easy to explain to business stakeholders. The challenge: it requires accurate knowledge of what each role actually needs — documentation that most enterprises don't have.

Bottom-up role mining analyzes existing access patterns: which users have similar entitlements? Do those users share common HR attributes? Saviynt's role mining engine clusters users by entitlement co-occurrence and correlates clusters with HR attributes (department, job code, location) to suggest roles derived from observed reality. The risk: it codifies the current access state, which may already be over-permissioned. Role mining is discovery, not authorization. The governance review step turns discovery into policy.

Role Lifecycle Management

Roles are not static definitions — they require their own lifecycle management. Every role needs an owner (accountable for its definition and membership), a review schedule (annual at minimum for most roles; quarterly for privileged roles), and a retirement process (when a business function is discontinued, its roles must be decommissioned and their members reassigned). Without active role lifecycle management, role definitions drift from business reality just as user access drifts from role requirements.

SailPoint Comparison — Role Management

SailPoint IdentityIQ has a mature role lifecycle management capability with strong role modeling tools built for complex on-premises environments. Its role mining is well-regarded for large enterprise deployments with heterogeneous legacy systems. Saviynt's role mining is newer but integrates more natively with its analytics engine and cloud connector library. For organizations with primarily cloud applications, Saviynt's role model aligns better to modern SaaS access patterns. For organizations with deep SAP, mainframe, and legacy ERP role complexity, SailPoint's role management tooling has the longer track record.

Segregation of Duties Architecture

Segregation of Duties (SoD) is the security control that prevents any single individual from accumulating enough access to commit and conceal fraud or error without detection. The foundational principle: if completing a harmful action requires two separate capabilities (create and approve, request and fulfill, record and reconcile), then those capabilities must be held by two different people. Any single person holding both capabilities can bypass the intended control.

The classic example is financial payment processing: the capability to create a payment (enter a vendor invoice in SAP) and the capability to approve a payment (release it for settlement) must be held by different individuals. A single person holding both can create fraudulent payments and approve them without any second-party check. This is not a theoretical risk — payment fraud via SoD violations is one of the most common causes of financial statement misstatement in SOX-regulated environments.

SoD Rule Architecture

SoD rules in Saviynt are defined as conflicting entitlement sets: Set A (one or more entitlements representing one business function) and Set B (one or more entitlements representing the conflicting function). A violation exists when a single identity holds at least one entitlement from Set A AND at least one from Set B simultaneously. Rules have a risk level (Critical, High, Medium), a business owner accountable for the rule, and optionally a set of documented mitigating controls that reduce risk when an exception is necessary.

Preventive vs Detective SoD

Preventive SoD operates at the moment of access request: before granting a new entitlement, Saviynt evaluates whether the requested entitlement creates a conflict with any entitlement the identity already holds. If a conflict is detected, the request is flagged and routed to compliance review before provisioning occurs. The entitlement is not granted until the conflict is resolved. This is the preferred control — it prevents violations from occurring in the first place.

Detective SoD operates continuously or on a schedule: Saviynt scans all identities against all SoD rules, identifies existing violations in the current access state, and generates alerts and certification items for review. Detective controls are necessary because not all violations are caught at request time (manual provisioning, legacy access, inherited role assignments), but they are a second line of defense, not a substitute for preventive controls. Both are required — neither alone is sufficient.

Cross-Application SoD — The Hidden Risk Layer

Within-application SoD (both conflicting entitlements in the same system like SAP) is relatively straightforward to detect — the application itself may have some awareness of its own role conflicts. Cross-application SoD is the significantly harder and higher-risk problem: when the conflicting entitlements span two different systems (create purchase order in SAP; approve purchase order in a separate approval workflow tool), neither application has visibility into the other's entitlements. Only the IGA layer — which aggregates access data from all connected applications into the identity warehouse — can detect this conflict. This cross-application SoD detection capability is one of Saviynt's core architectural value propositions.

SoD Exceptions and Mitigating Controls

There are legitimate business scenarios where a single individual genuinely needs both conflicting entitlements — small organizations where role separation isn't operationally feasible, emergency situations, or highly specialized functions with no practical substitute. These cases require a documented exception process: formal business justification, approval by the SoD rule owner (often the CFO for financial controls), a defined expiry date, and documented mitigating controls that compensate for the segregation gap. Typical mitigating controls include enhanced audit logging of the conflicting actions, secondary approval required for the high-risk operation, and periodic management review of all transactions by the exception holder. The exception is recorded in Saviynt, and the mitigation evidence is maintained for audit.

Cross-application SoD is where most enterprises have the largest undetected risk. 🎯 Use Case — Accounts Payable SoD Violation Discovery
A financial audit finds that 23 employees in the Accounts Payable department have both the ability to create vendor invoices in SAP (T-code FB60) AND the ability to approve them (SAP payment approval workflow). This SoD violation has existed for an average of 14 months per affected employee.

Why it happened architecturally: Access accumulation via Mover events — employees joined AP with only invoice creation access, then received payment approval workflow access through an access request as their role expanded, without Saviynt's SoD engine catching the conflict (because preventive SoD was not configured for this ruleset, or the rule didn't exist at request time).

What Saviynt should have done: Preventive SoD check at the moment the payment approval workflow access was requested — the request should have been routed to compliance review. Detective SoD scan should have identified the existing violations within 24 hours of the rule being active.

Remediation: Formal exception review for all 23 employees. For 20 — revoke the payment approval role (retain invoice creation; assign a separate approver). For 3 with genuine business need — document exceptions with compensating controls (management review of all transactions, dual approval override).

Architecture to prevent recurrence: Activate preventive SoD for the Payment-SoD-001 ruleset. Configure detective scan to run daily. Add this SoD rule pair to the quarterly certification campaign scope. Verify the rule covers cross-application variants of the same conflict.

Saviynt Architecture Deep Dive

Saviynt is a cloud-native Enterprise Identity Cloud (EIC). Its architecture consists of five core components, each handling a distinct layer of IGA functionality. Understanding how these components relate — and where each fits in the governance and provisioning workflows — is the difference between operating Saviynt and architecting with it.

Identity Warehouse

The Identity Warehouse is Saviynt's central repository: the single source of governance truth for all identity, account, and entitlement data across connected systems. Every connector reconciliation writes into the warehouse. Every SoD evaluation queries it. Every certification campaign is scoped from it. The warehouse is what makes cross-application SoD and cross-application certification possible — because it aggregates what each connected system knows about access into a unified queryable model.

The identity warehouse is not a real-time data store — it is accurate to the last reconciliation run. This is why reconciliation cadence is a critical architectural parameter: a warehouse that's seven days stale will produce SoD evaluations and certifications that reflect last week's access state, not today's.

Workflow Engine

The workflow engine is the orchestration layer that drives all IGA processes: JML lifecycle events, access request routing and approval chains, access certification campaigns, and SoD violation remediation workflows. It supports sequential and parallel approval patterns, dynamic routing based on request attributes (entitlement risk level, requesting department, cost center), automatic escalation on timeout, and webhook integration to external systems (ServiceNow, Slack notifications).

Analytics Engine

Saviynt's analytics engine powers risk scoring, role mining, access analytics, and compliance reporting. Risk scoring assigns each identity a composite risk score based on: number of entitlements held, SoD violations present, privileged access levels, recent certification decisions (especially items that were certified but shouldn't have been), and optionally SIEM alert signals imported from the security monitoring layer. Risk scores are used to prioritize certification campaign items — high-risk identities and high-risk entitlements are reviewed first, which is the mechanism that reduces certification fatigue while maintaining governance focus where it matters.

Deployment Models — EIC vs On-Premises

Saviynt Enterprise Identity Cloud (EIC) is the SaaS-delivered model: Saviynt manages the infrastructure, upgrades, and availability. The customer manages configuration, connector setup, and operational processes. EIC is the recommended and primary deployment model for new Saviynt implementations — it eliminates infrastructure management, provides automatic upgrades, and scales elastically. The tradeoff: data residency requirements in some jurisdictions may require additional controls.

Saviynt on-premises is available for organizations with strict data residency or air-gap requirements. It requires the customer to manage infrastructure, upgrades, and availability. Most organizations should prefer EIC unless specific regulatory requirements force on-premises deployment.

Saviynt vs SailPoint — Decision Framework

Choose Saviynt when: your environment is primarily cloud-native applications; you want IGA + PAM in a single platform (Saviynt's PAM-IGA convergence is a genuine differentiator); you prioritize modern API-first integration and analytics; or you are a greenfield IGA implementation without legacy SailPoint investment.

Choose SailPoint when: you have deep on-premises infrastructure with complex legacy connector requirements; you need strong file access governance (SharePoint, file shares — SailPoint's unstructured data governance is more mature); you have an existing IdentityIQ investment with high switching costs; or your implementation partner capability skews toward SailPoint.

In both cases, the architectural principles — identity warehouse, workflow engine, connector framework, certification campaigns, SoD rulesets — are the same. The implementation details differ; the design decisions don't.

IGA Integration Architecture

IGA does not operate in isolation — it is the orchestration center of the enterprise IAM stack. Every other IAM component either feeds authoritative data into Saviynt or receives provisioned access from it. Understanding the full integration map is essential for both IGA architects and anyone designing the broader IAM platform, because the quality of every downstream system's access control is bounded by the quality of IGA's provisioning.

HR System → Saviynt: The Authoritative Trigger

The HR system (Workday, SAP SuccessFactors) sends lifecycle events to Saviynt via SCIM push, REST webhook, or scheduled file import. Each method has different latency and reliability characteristics: SCIM push is near-real-time and event-driven (preferred); REST webhooks are configurable but depend on the HR system's outbound notification capability; file-based import introduces batch latency (typically hours). For Joiner events, latency affects pre-boarding quality. For Leaver events, latency is a security risk. The HR integration method choice is a security architecture decision, not just a technical preference.

Saviynt → Active Directory

Active Directory is typically the primary provisioning target because AD credentials are used for Windows authentication, email, and frequently as the identity source for SSO federation. Saviynt provisions users to AD via LDAP connector: creates user accounts, assigns group memberships (which translate to AD security groups), and manages user attribute synchronization. AD group membership is the mechanism through which IGA-managed access flows into SSO assertions — because PingFederate reads AD groups at authentication time.

Saviynt → PingDirectory: The SCIM Bridge

For environments using PingDirectory as the LDAP identity store (Module 05), Saviynt provisions via SCIM to PingDirectory. PingDirectory stores user profiles and group memberships. PingFederate queries PingDirectory at authentication time and includes group memberships as claims in SAML assertions. This creates the critical indirect chain: Saviynt provisions → PingDirectory stores → PingFederate reads → application receives claims. Any provisioning latency or failure in Saviynt's PingDirectory connector breaks this chain — users authenticate successfully but receive wrong or missing role claims.

Saviynt → CyberArk: The PAM Bridge

For privileged identities, Saviynt integrates with CyberArk (covered fully in Module 10) in both directions: Saviynt requests PAM account vaulting when a user is provisioned with privileged entitlements (so that CyberArk manages the actual credentials), and CyberArk surfaces privileged entitlement data back to Saviynt so that privileged access is included in IGA certification campaigns. This bi-directional integration is the mechanism that prevents privileged access from being an ungoverned island outside the IGA program.

Saviynt → SIEM: Audit Event Export

Saviynt exports provisioning events, certification decisions, and SoD violation records to the SIEM (Splunk, IBM QRadar, or equivalent). This export serves two purposes: security monitoring (the SIEM can correlate IGA events with authentication events to detect anomalies — a user who was just deprovisioned but is still authenticating is a high-priority alert) and audit evidence (SOX and HIPAA auditors often prefer SIEM-archived evidence over IGA system reports because SIEM logs are immutable and independent of the IGA system). Export Saviynt events to SIEM and retain them for the required audit period.

The IGA Operating Model

The integration architecture is only as good as its operational model. Each integration needs an owner: who monitors the connector health? Who responds to reconciliation failures? Who manages the service accounts used by each connector? The IGA operating model defines these accountabilities. Without it, a connector failure may go undetected for days, during which provisioning and deprovisioning events silently fail and the identity warehouse drifts from reality.

Q1 An employee transfers from Engineering to Finance. Saviynt runs the Mover workflow. After the workflow: the employee has Finance access (new) AND still has Engineering GitHub access (old) AND still has the Engineering AWS sandbox (old). Walk through the architectural gaps in Saviynt's Mover configuration that allowed this. +
Three architectural gaps contributed to this outcome:
  • Role delta not computing removals. The Mover workflow was configured to add access for the new role (Finance-Analyst) but not to identify and remove access no longer justified by the new role. A correct Mover configuration computes the delta in both directions: what the new role requires that the old role didn't (add), and what the old role required that the new role doesn't (remove). If only additions are automated, removals accumulate silently.
  • GitHub and AWS not connected as managed applications. If the Engineering GitHub organization and AWS sandbox are not connected to Saviynt as managed applications (no SCIM or REST connector configured), Saviynt has no awareness of those entitlements and cannot deprovision them during a Mover event. These are ungoverned applications — outside the identity warehouse — and therefore outside the lifecycle automation. The remediation is to onboard these applications as Saviynt connectors.
  • No access certification covering the transferred employee. Even without perfect Mover automation, a well-designed certification campaign would have surfaced these retained entitlements within the next 90-day cycle for the employee's new manager to review. The absence of a recent certification covering this employee means the governance backstop didn't fire either. Both the preventive control (Mover delta removal) and the detective control (certification) failed.
The permanent fix: Configure Mover workflow role delta to enforce removals, onboard GitHub and AWS as managed connectors, and verify the employee is included in the next user certification campaign scoped to their new manager.
Q2 Design the certification campaign strategy for an enterprise with 10,000 employees and 50 connected applications. How do you scope campaigns to avoid certification fatigue while still providing meaningful SOX audit evidence? +
A tiered campaign strategy prevents fatigue while satisfying SOX scope requirements:
  • Tier 1 — High-risk quarterly campaigns (SOX scope): For the ~15 applications in SOX scope, run quarterly entitlement certification campaigns scoped to high-risk entitlements only (admin profiles, privileged roles, SoD-adjacent access). Application owners as reviewers — they are more qualified than managers to judge entitlement appropriateness for specific applications. Cap at 25 items per reviewer per campaign. This directly satisfies SOX 302/404 quarterly review requirements for financial application access.
  • Tier 2 — Annual user certification (all employees): Manager-reviewed access certification for all 10,000 employees, run annually. To prevent fatigue with 50 applications: scope to entitlements held outside the user's current role model only (orphaned entitlements and manually-granted access), not birthright access. If birthright access was properly certified at role assignment, re-certifying it annually adds noise without governance value. A typical user with well-managed birthright access will have 5–10 items to review; only users with accumulated non-role access generate large queues.
  • Tier 3 — Micro-certifications (event-triggered): For role transfers, role changes, and any SoD exception holders — trigger immediate targeted reviews. A Mover event should trigger a 5-item micro-certification for the new manager: "confirm the transferred employee's retained access." This is the governance backstop for Mover workflow gaps.
  • Presentation quality: All campaigns must use business-readable entitlement descriptions, show risk scores on flagged items, and limit each reviewer to under 30 items. The entitlement catalog investment that makes this possible is as important as the campaign configuration itself.
SOX audit evidence produced: Quarterly completion reports with decision distribution for SOX-scope applications; annual user certification completion rates; remediation evidence for all revoked items. A well-structured multi-tier strategy will show 85–90% completion rates and 8–15% revocation rates — credible evidence of genuine review.
Q3 Saviynt's reconciliation finds 47 accounts in Salesforce that IGA doesn't know about. What are the possible explanations, what are the security implications of each, and what is the remediation architecture? +
Possible explanations and their security implications:
  • Accounts created manually by Salesforce admins (most common): Salesforce admins provisioned users directly in the Salesforce UI, bypassing Saviynt entirely. Security implication: these accounts have no IGA audit trail, no approval record, and no certification history. They are ungoverned — their access has never been reviewed and cannot be included in certification campaigns until IGA knows they exist. Risk level: high if any have elevated profiles.
  • Accounts from a legacy migration or system import: A data migration or historical system cutover created Salesforce accounts outside the IGA workflow. Security implication: same as above, plus these accounts may belong to former employees who were deprovisioned in IGA but not in Salesforce (a leaver deprovisioning gap from before Saviynt was connected). Risk level: critical if any belong to terminated employees.
  • Service accounts or integration accounts: Technical accounts used by APIs, integrations, or automation that were never part of the human identity lifecycle. Security implication: service accounts often have elevated permissions and are never reviewed in user-scoped certifications. Risk level: high if using privileged Salesforce API access.
  • Break-glass or emergency accounts: Accounts created during a Saviynt outage for emergency access, then not cleaned up. Security implication: no time limit, no audit trail of usage, no review.
Remediation architecture:
  1. Import all 47 accounts into Saviynt's identity warehouse (reconciliation handles discovery; manual correlation handles ownership assignment).
  2. For each account: correlate to an identity (active employee, terminated employee, or service account). Accounts correlating to terminated employees must be immediately disabled — this is a leaver deprovisioning failure requiring urgent remediation.
  3. Run an immediate entitlement certification for all 47 accounts, assigned to the Salesforce application owner. Every account must be reviewed and certified or revoked within 30 days.
  4. Implement a Salesforce connector configuration that prevents manual account creation outside IGA — or at minimum alerts the IGA team when accounts are created manually — so the reconciliation gap doesn't recur.
Q4 Your SOX auditor asks for evidence that no employee has both 'create payment' and 'approve payment' access. Walk through exactly what Saviynt produces as audit evidence — what report, what data, what it proves. +
Saviynt produces a multi-layer evidence package for this SOX control:
  • SoD Rule Definition Report: The Payment-SoD-001 rule definition — what the conflicting entitlement sets are (SAP FB60, F-02 vs SAP FBV0, Payment-Approver workflow role), who owns the rule (CFO), what the risk level is (Critical), and when it was last reviewed. This proves the control exists and is formally owned.
  • Continuous SoD Scan Report (point-in-time): As of the audit date, a scan of all active identities against Payment-SoD-001 showing zero violations (or a list of current violations with their documented exception status). This proves the control is active and the current access state is compliant — or demonstrates exceptions are formally managed.
  • Preventive SoD activity log: A log of every access request that was blocked or routed to compliance review due to Payment-SoD-001 during the audit period. This demonstrates the preventive control is operational — conflicts are being caught at request time, not just detected after the fact.
  • Access certification report for SOX scope: The completed certification campaign(s) for SAP during the audit period: who reviewed access, what decisions were made, what was revoked, what was certified. This proves that human review of the relevant entitlements occurred within the required period.
  • Remediation evidence: For any violations detected during the period — the remediation workflow execution log showing the entitlement was revoked, when, by whom, and the deprovisioning confirmation from the SAP connector. This closes the loop: violations were detected and remediated.
What this evidence package proves to the auditor: The SoD control is defined, technically enforced at both request time and continuously, reviewed by humans on a regular cadence, and violations (if any) are systematically identified and remediated. This satisfies SOX Section 404 requirements for operating effectiveness of the SoD control over financial payment processing.
Q5 Explain why PingFederate's ability to include group memberships in SAML assertions depends on Saviynt's provisioning quality. What breaks in the SSO layer if Saviynt's AD provisioning has a 24-hour lag? +
The dependency chain: PingFederate includes group memberships in SAML assertions by querying PingDirectory (or Active Directory) at authentication time using LDAP. It reads the memberOf attribute for the authenticating user and includes those groups as claims in the outbound SAML assertion. Applications receive these claims and use them for RBAC authorization decisions.

Saviynt is the system that writes group memberships to Active Directory (via LDAP connector) or PingDirectory (via SCIM connector). When Saviynt assigns a user to the Finance-Manager business role, it adds them to the Finance-Manager AD group. That group membership is what PingFederate reads. If Saviynt never writes the group, PingFederate never includes it.

What breaks with a 24-hour provisioning lag:
  • Joiner scenario: A new employee starts on Monday morning. The Joiner workflow has a 24-hour lag due to a manual step or connector failure. The employee authenticates via PingFederate — their account exists in AD (created by the manual step) but their group memberships haven't been provisioned yet. PingFederate includes an empty groups claim. The application RBAC engine finds no matching role — the employee gets no access or falls back to a guest/no-access state. From the employee's perspective: they can log in but can't do anything on day one.
  • Mover scenario: An employee is promoted to Finance-Manager on Tuesday. Saviynt processes the role delta and provisions the Finance-Manager AD group on Wednesday (24-hour lag). For all of Tuesday, the employee authenticates, PingFederate reads the old groups, and the application continues to enforce the old RBAC role. The employee cannot access Finance-Manager capabilities despite the HR record being updated.
  • Leaver scenario: This is the most serious case. A terminated employee's AD account is disabled immediately (correct), but their group memberships are not removed for 24 hours. If the application checks group membership in the SAML assertion rather than account status, a session that was established before account disablement may retain valid group claims for the assertion lifetime. Additionally, any system that relies on group membership for access control (not account status) may grant access during the lag window.
The design implication: Saviynt's AD/PingDirectory provisioning must be treated as near-real-time for the SSO chain to function correctly. For Leaver events, group revocation must occur in minutes (not hours) alongside account disablement — because group claims are the authorization mechanism, not just the authentication mechanism.

  • Business role model foundation: Use Saviynt's role mining on the 30 connected applications to identify the top 50 role candidates (clusters with the highest population and clearest HR attribute correlation). Governance-review and formalize these 50 as business roles. This is the foundation — not a complete role rationalization, but a starting point that enables meaningful certification campaigns for the highest-population roles.
  • Operating model formalized: Named owners for each connector (responsible for connector health and reconciliation monitoring), a quarterly IGA program review with CISO and compliance leadership, and an IGA operations runbook covering connector failure response, reconciliation exception handling, and escalation paths for SoD violations. IGA cannot be a set-and-forget deployment — it requires active operational governance.
📐 Governance Model
  • IGA Program Owner (Director of IAM or equivalent): accountable for overall program maturity, SOX compliance posture, and quarterly certification completion. Reports program health metrics to CISO monthly.
  • Application Connector Owners (one per application family): accountable for connector health, reconciliation schedule adherence, and immediate response to connector failures. Responsible for entitlement catalog quality (business-readable descriptions) for their applications.
  • SoD Rule Owners (business function leads — CFO for payment rules, CISO for privileged access rules): accountable for rule definition, exception approvals, and annual rule reviews. SoD rules are business controls, not IT configurations — they require business ownership.
  • Certification Reviewers (managers and application owners): accountable for completing certification tasks within the campaign window. Completion rates tracked and reported to their management chain. Non-completion escalates to their manager for the first campaign; to CISO for subsequent non-completions.
