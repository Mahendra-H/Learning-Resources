Authorization Architecture: RBAC, ABAC, PBAC & ReBAC & Policy Engines

Authentication answers one question: are you who you say you are? Authorization answers a completely different question: given that we know who you are, what are you allowed to do? These two questions are often conflated in enterprise IAM — handled by the same system, governed by the same team, discussed in the same meeting. But they are architecturally separate concerns, and the most expensive IAM mistakes happen when they're treated as the same problem. You've already authenticated millions of times through PingFederate. This module teaches you how enterprise systems decide what those authenticated users are actually allowed to access — and why the answer requires four different architectural models, a policy engine, enforcement points at every layer, and a clear separation of the policy decision from the policy enforcement. Welcome to Phase 4. 🔒

8.1

🔀 Authentication vs Authorization — The Full Distinction

Every enterprise IAM conversation eventually collides with the same confusion: someone calls a permission problem an "authentication issue," or a login failure gets routed to the team that owns role assignments. The confusion is understandable — the same product (PingFederate, Azure AD, Okta) frequently handles both concerns, the same SSO login screen is the entry point for both, and the same identity record drives both decisions. But authentication and authorization are not the same problem wearing different clothes. They solve different problems, they fail in catastrophically different ways, they require different technology, and they scale according to entirely different pressures. Getting this distinction architecturally precise — not just definitionally correct — is the foundation everything else in this module builds on.

Authentication: Establishing Identity

Authentication answers exactly one question: is this principal genuine? It establishes who is making the request. The products that do this work are the ones you've spent the last seven modules mastering: PingFederate, PingID, Active Directory, FIDO2 authenticators. The evidence authentication produces is transient and protocol-bound — tokens, SAML assertions, Kerberos tickets — proof that a credential was verified at a specific moment in time. When authentication fails, it fails as impersonation: someone successfully convinced the system they were someone they are not, typically through credential theft, session hijacking, or a flaw in the federation trust chain.

Authorization: Establishing Permission

Authorization answers a structurally different question: is this action permitted for this principal, in this context? Note that authorization is not a single yes/no gate evaluated once at login — it is evaluated continuously, for every action, against every resource. The products that do this work are policy engines, IGA platforms, and PAM systems: OPA, AWS IAM, Azure RBAC, Saviynt, TACACS+. The evidence authorization relies on is durable and structural — roles, attributes, policies, entitlements — data that describes what a principal is allowed to do, independent of any single login event. When authorization fails, it fails as privilege escalation, over-permissioning, access creep, or a Separation of Duties violation — failures that are often invisible for months because the system is functioning exactly as configured; it's just configured wrong.

The Conflation Problem

The confusion compounds because some systems genuinely do both — Active Directory is the clearest case. Kerberos handles authentication (proving you are jsmith), while group membership simultaneously drives authorization (deciding what jsmith can touch). One system, two architecturally distinct functions bundled into a single directory record. The discipline an architect needs here is to mentally separate the layers even when a single product implements both: ask "is this an identity-proving decision or a permission-granting decision?" every time you trace a failure, because the remediation path is completely different depending on the answer. A broken Kerberos trust is fixed by the federation team. A user with the wrong group membership is fixed by access governance. Conflating the two sends incidents to the wrong owner and delays resolution.

The Four Authorization Questions

Every authorization decision — regardless of which model implements it — answers four questions. These four questions define the entire authorization decision space, and as you'll see across the rest of this module, each authorization model (RBAC, ABAC, PBAC, ReBAC) is simply a different way of answering them with different degrees of expressiveness.

  • Q1 — Subject: What is asking? (the user, service account, or system identity)
  • Q2 — Resource: What do they want to access? (the application, record, document, API)
  • Q3 — Action: What do they want to do to it? (read, write, delete, approve, export)
  • Q4 — Context: Under what conditions? (time, location, device posture, risk level)

Center: the Authorization Decision — permit, deny, or step-up. Every model in this module answers these four questions with a different level of precision.

Authorization at Multiple Layers

A single user action rarely triggers just one authorization decision. It traverses multiple, stacked decisions, each enforced by a different system, at a different layer of the stack — and each one capable of independently denying the request.

🟡 Architect's Bridge Authorization is the access control layer in every Java API you have ever built. When you wrote @PreAuthorize("hasRole('ADMIN')") on a Spring method, you implemented RBAC. When you wrote a custom SecurityEvaluator that checked the caller's department attribute against the resource's classification level, you implemented ABAC. When you configured Spring Security to evaluate a policy expression at runtime, you implemented PBAC. The patterns you already know from API security apply directly to enterprise authorization architecture — the difference is scale, centralization, and the governance layer on top.
🔵 The Four Questions Define the Four Models RBAC answers: what role does the subject have, and does that role permit this action on this resource? ABAC adds: what attributes does the subject have, what attributes does the resource have, and what does the policy say about this combination? PBAC raises the abstraction level: what policies exist, and does this request satisfy them? ReBAC asks: what is the relationship between the subject and the resource, and does that relationship grant the action? Each model is an answer to the same four questions with different degrees of expressiveness — and that's the lens to hold onto for the rest of this module.

8.2

👥 RBAC — Role-Based Access Control

Role-Based Access Control is the model you already use, every day, whether or not anyone on your team calls it that. RBAC's defining architectural rule is simple and absolute: permissions are never assigned directly to users. Permissions are assigned to roles, and roles are assigned to users. This single indirection is what makes RBAC manageable at scale — instead of tracking thousands of individual user-permission pairs, an administrator tracks a much smaller set of role-permission pairs, and a separate, simpler set of user-role assignments.

The Four RBAC Components

  • Users — the principals who need access (people or service accounts)
  • Roles — named groupings that represent a business function (Finance-Analyst, DBA, Help-Desk-L1)
  • Permissions — the actual operations a role grants (read-report, write-report, approve-report)
  • Sessions — the runtime activation of a subset of a user's assigned roles for a specific working context

Users never hold permissions directly — they hold roles, and roles hold permissions.

RBAC Maturity Levels

RBAC is not a single model but a family, formalized by NIST into four increasingly capable levels:

  • RBAC0 — Flat roles: no hierarchy, no constraints. Every role stands alone with its own permission set.
  • RBAC1 — Hierarchical roles: roles inherit permissions from roles beneath them. Junior Analyst → Senior Analyst → Manager, where Manager inherits everything below.
  • RBAC2 — Constrained RBAC: adds Separation of Duties (SoD) constraints that prevent a user from holding two roles that together create a conflict of interest.
  • RBAC3 — Symmetric RBAC: combines hierarchy (RBAC1) and constraints (RBAC2) into a single unified model — the standard target for mature enterprise IGA implementations.

Permissions accumulate upward through the hierarchy — a Finance-Admin automatically holds every permission a Finance-Analyst holds.

Why RBAC Is the Foundation

Almost every enterprise authorization system is RBAC at its core, even when the vendor calls it something else. AWS managed policies, Salesforce profiles, SAP authorization objects, Workday security groups — strip away the branding and you find users assigned to named groupings that hold permission sets. RBAC's dominance is earned: it maps cleanly to how organizations already think about access ("she's a manager, so she can approve expenses"), it's auditable in plain language, and it's the model every compliance framework (SOX, HIPAA, PCI-DSS) expects to see evidence of.

AD Groups as RBAC Implementation

You've been working inside an RBAC system since Module 02. Active Directory security groups are RBAC: group = role, group membership = role assignment, and group-based file or application permissions = the permission set attached to that role. When PingFederate authenticates a user and reads their group memberships from PingDirectory, it is reading their RBAC role assignments — and when those memberships flow into a SAML AttributeStatement or an OIDC groups claim, the receiving application is being handed an authorization signal it can map to its own local roles.

RBAC's Limitations

  • Role explosion: the number of distinct roles grows faster than the organization can govern
  • Context blindness: the same role grants the same permissions regardless of time, location, or device — RBAC has no concept of "this role, but only during business hours"
  • Granularity ceiling: RBAC can express "can read reports" but not "can read only their own region's reports" without creating a new role for every region
  • Static assignment: roles don't adapt to risk signals or changing context — a compromised account retains full role privileges until someone manually intervenes
🟢 Architect's Note — Role Explosion Role explosion is the primary failure mode of pure RBAC at enterprise scale. It starts simply: Engineer, Senior-Engineer, Lead-Engineer. Then business units want their own roles: Frontend-Engineer, Backend-Engineer, Platform-Engineer. Then environments: Prod-Engineer, Dev-Engineer, Staging-Engineer. Then exceptions: the engineer who needs Finance-Reader access. Three years later: 3,000 roles, no one understands the model, and the IGA team spends 60% of their time managing role assignments rather than governing access. The architectural remedy: role mining (Module 09) to rationalize the role set, ABAC for the exceptions that drove role explosion, and governance rules that require justification for new role creation.
🟣 Security — SoD in RBAC (RBAC2) Separation of Duties constraints in RBAC prevent a single user from holding two roles that together create a fraud or error risk. Classic example: a user should never hold both "create-payment" and "approve-payment" roles simultaneously — those two roles combined allow them to create and approve their own fraudulent payment. SoD constraints in Saviynt are defined as rule sets: if a user is assigned role-A, they cannot also be assigned role-B. Saviynt evaluates SoD at provisioning time (a preventive control) and again at access certification (a detective control).
🟣 Deep dive: What is role mining and how do enterprises derive a rationalized role model from observed access patterns?
Role mining is a data-analysis discipline applied to existing access patterns — typically the current set of permission assignments across applications — to derive a rationalized role model rather than relying on roles that accumulated organically over years. The process generally runs bottom-up: cluster users by the permission sets they actually hold, identify common groupings that recur across many users, and propose those groupings as candidate roles. A top-down pass then validates each candidate role against actual job functions, removing clusters that reflect accidental similarity rather than genuine business roles. Saviynt and SailPoint both ship role-mining engines that surface these candidate roles for an IGA team to review, approve, and publish — turning three years of ad-hoc permission sprawl into a defensible, auditable role catalog. Role mining is typically the first remediation step when an access review uncovers role explosion, since it directly attacks the root cause: roles that no longer map cleanly to how the organization actually works.

8.3

🏷️ ABAC — Attribute-Based Access Control

Attribute-Based Access Control answers the same four authorization questions as RBAC, but with far more expressiveness. Instead of collapsing "who you are" into a single role label, ABAC evaluates the actual attributes of the subject, the resource, the action, and the environment — and makes a decision based on how those attributes relate to one another, at the moment of the request.

The ABAC Components

  • Subject attributes: user.department, user.clearance, user.location, user.role, user.employmentType
  • Resource attributes: document.classification, document.owner, record.region, data.sensitivity
  • Action attributes: read, write, delete, approve, export
  • Environment attributes: time, device posture, network, risk score, geographic location

An ABAC policy expresses a rule across these attributes. In natural language: "Allow access if the subject's clearance is at least as high as the resource's classification, AND the subject's department matches the resource's owning department, AND the environment's device posture is compliant." Expressed as policy logic, that becomes:

allow IF subject.clearance >= resource.classification
  AND subject.department == resource.owner_department
  AND environment.device_posture == 'compliant'

How ABAC Eliminates Role Explosion

Instead of creating a new role for every combination of permissions a business scenario requires, ABAC lets attributes describe the subject and the resource, and a single policy expresses the rule across all of them. There's no need for a Finance-Analyst-EMEA-Confidential role when an ABAC policy can simply evaluate region and classification attributes dynamically.

ABAC in Production: AWS IAM and Conditional Access

ABAC is not a theoretical model — it's already running every cloud authorization decision you make. AWS IAM Condition elements implement ABAC directly: checking request attributes, resource tag conditions, source IP ranges, and date/time windows is exactly the subject/resource/environment attribute evaluation ABAC describes. Similarly, Microsoft Entra ID Conditional Access policies implement ABAC at the authentication layer — subject (user identity and group), resource (the target application), and context (device compliance, location, sign-in risk) all feed into a single access decision.

ABAC's Limitations

  • Policy complexity: ABAC policies are significantly harder to audit at a glance than a simple role assignment — "what does this user have access to" requires evaluating every applicable policy, not reading a role list
  • Attribute quality dependency: ABAC is only as accurate as the attribute data feeding it — garbage attributes produce wrong decisions silently
  • Performance: evaluating attributes at runtime, especially when they require external lookups, is computationally more expensive than a static role check at enterprise scale
🟡 Architect's Bridge ABAC is exactly the conditional rule engine you've built in API gateways and Spring Security. When your gateway rule says "allow if request.caller.ipRange is in corporate-cidrs AND request.resource.sensitivity != HIGH AND request.time is within business_hours" — that is ABAC. Subject attributes (caller IP range), resource attributes (sensitivity), environment attributes (time). AWS IAM Condition blocks are ABAC. Spring Security @PreAuthorize expressions are ABAC. OPA policies are ABAC. You've been writing ABAC logic for years. This module gives you the architectural vocabulary to design it at enterprise scale.
🟣 Security — Attribute Quality Is ABAC's Achilles Heel An ABAC policy that evaluates subject.department == resource.owner_dept is only as accurate as the department attribute in your identity store. If users' department attributes are out of date (Mover lifecycle not properly handled in the directory — Module 01/02), ABAC makes wrong decisions — and unlike RBAC mistakes (a wrong role assignment is visible in an IGA access review), ABAC mistakes are invisible: the policy runs, the attribute matches, access is granted, and no one notices. Attribute data quality governance is the operational prerequisite for ABAC. Build it before deploying ABAC, not after.

8.4

📜 PBAC — Policy-Based Access Control

Policy-Based Access Control takes ABAC's attribute logic and raises it one level of abstraction: instead of attribute-evaluation logic living inside application code or scattered config files, PBAC externalizes it into formal, centrally managed, independently governed policies. PBAC is best understood not as a competitor to ABAC but as ABAC matured into an enterprise governance discipline.

How PBAC Differs From ABAC

In plain ABAC, the attribute-matching logic is frequently embedded directly in application code — a condition buried in a service class, evaluated inline. In PBAC, policies are:

  • Written in a formal policy language (XACML, Rego, Cedar)
  • Stored in a central policy repository, separate from application code
  • Evaluated by a dedicated policy engine — a Policy Decision Point (PDP, covered fully in 8.7)
  • Versioned, audited, and governed independently of the application's release cycle

XACML — The Original Formal Standard

XACML (eXtensible Access Control Markup Language) was the original OASIS standard for formalizing PBAC. It's XML-based — verbose by modern standards, but deliberately so: every request, policy, and decision has a rigorously defined structure, which made it possible for large enterprises to build interoperable policy engines from different vendors years before lighter-weight alternatives existed. XACML adoption in greenfield projects has slowed in favor of OPA and Cedar, but it remains present in many enterprise systems deployed during the 2010s, and its conceptual model (PDP/PEP/PAP/PIP, covered in 8.7; Permit/Deny/NotApplicable/Indeterminate decisions) still underpins how modern engines think about policy evaluation.

OPA — The Modern PBAC Engine

Open Policy Agent (OPA) is the dominant modern PBAC engine, particularly in cloud-native environments. Policies are written in Rego, a declarative query language purpose-built for expressing authorization logic over structured data. OPA integrates with applications and APIs as a sidecar or a centralized service — the calling service sends a JSON request describing the subject, resource, action, and context, and OPA returns a decision.

Cedar — AWS's Purpose-Built Policy Language

Cedar is Amazon's policy language, designed specifically for correctness and performance — it was built with formal verification in mind, so policies can be mathematically proven not to contain certain classes of errors (like unintentionally granting more access than intended). Cedar powers AWS Verified Permissions and is increasingly used as the policy language for purpose-built fine-grained authorization services.

Google Zanzibar and OpenFGA — A Preview

One more system deserves a mention here, even though its full treatment is Section 8.5: Google Zanzibar is the relationship-based policy system underlying Google Drive's sharing model. It answers authorization questions through relationship graph traversal rather than attribute matching — a different model entirely, called ReBAC, which the next section covers in depth.

PBAC in Enterprise IAM

  • Centralized policy management: change a policy in one place, and the change takes effect everywhere it's enforced — no redeploying a dozen services
  • Policy as code: policies live in version control, go through CI/CD, and can be automatically tested before deployment, exactly like application code
  • Audit: every policy decision can be logged together with the exact policy version that produced it — a critical capability for regulatory review

🟢 Architect's Note — PBAC With OPA Is Cloud-Native Standard Practice PBAC with OPA is the pattern for modern cloud-native authorization. Every Kubernetes admission controller, every API gateway policy, every service mesh authorization rule is a PBAC decision evaluated by OPA or a similar engine. As a Java platform engineer, the OPA sidecar pattern will be immediately familiar: your service calls the OPA sidecar via HTTP (POST /v1/data/company/access), passes the request context as JSON, and receives a permit/deny decision. No authorization logic in your service code. The policy is owned by the IAM/security team. You build the service. They govern what it allows. That separation of concerns is why PBAC exists.
🟣 Deep dive: How does XACML formalize PBAC with Request, Response, Policy, and PolicySet constructs?
XACML structures every authorization interaction around a handful of rigorously defined constructs. A Request describes the subject, resource, action, and environment attributes for a single access attempt — essentially a formal version of the four authorization questions from Section 8.1. A Policy contains one or more Rules, each with a target (when does this rule apply?), a condition (what must be true?), and an effect (Permit or Deny). Multiple Policies can be grouped into a PolicySet, with a combining algorithm that resolves conflicts when more than one policy applies to the same request — for example, "deny overrides," where any applicable Deny wins regardless of how many Permits also apply. The Response returns one of four decisions: Permit, Deny, NotApplicable (no policy matched this request), or Indeterminate (an error occurred during evaluation, such as a missing attribute). This formal structure — explicit combining algorithms, explicit handling of "no policy matched" versus "evaluation failed" — is what made XACML rigorous enough for regulated industries to build on, even as lighter languages like Rego have replaced it in newer architectures.

8.5

🕸️ ReBAC — Relationship-Based Access Control

Relationship-Based Access Control asks a fundamentally different question than the other three models. RBAC asks "what role does this subject hold?" ABAC asks "what attributes does this subject and resource have?" ReBAC asks: what is the relationship between this specific subject and this specific resource — and does that relationship grant the requested action?

The Google Zanzibar Origin

ReBAC's modern form traces directly to Google's internal authorization system, Zanzibar — built to solve a deceptively simple-sounding question at almost unimaginable scale: "can user U view document D?" for Google Drive. The challenge wasn't the question itself but the scale: billions of documents, billions of sharing relationships, and a requirement that access checks return in milliseconds with strong consistency guarantees. Zanzibar's answer was to model every permission as a relationship tuple and answer access questions through graph traversal rather than role or attribute lookup.

The Relationship Model

ReBAC represents permissions as relationship tuples — compact statements of the form resource → relationship → subject:

document:D1 → viewer → user:jsmith
folder:F1 → editor → group:engineering
org:company → admin → user:bwang

The power of ReBAC comes from relationship traversal: if jsmith is a viewer of folder:F1, and document D1 is contained within folder:F1, then jsmith inherits viewer access to D1 — without anyone ever creating a direct tuple between jsmith and D1. Access is computed by walking the relationship graph at query time, not by pre-enumerating every permission.

OpenFGA

OpenFGA is the leading open-source implementation of the Zanzibar model, donated to the CNCF and increasingly adopted by enterprises building fine-grained, relationship-driven authorization into SaaS products. It provides the relationship-tuple storage, the traversal engine, and an API for both writing relationships and checking access — the building blocks Google built internally, now available as infrastructure any team can deploy.

ReBAC vs RBAC: The Critical Distinction

RBAC assigns roles to users globally — "jsmith has the Editor role" grants edit access to every resource the Editor role covers. ReBAC expresses relationships between specific users and specific resources — "jsmith is editor of document:D1" grants edit access only to that one document. This is the same distinction between a blanket grant and a scoped grant that you intuitively understand from file-sharing tools: a global "Editor" role is a blunt instrument; a per-document relationship is a scalpel.

ReBACReview.subject is-direct-report-of Alice → traverses org chart relationship graphAlice sees ONLY her direct reports' reviews — most precise, naturally models business reality

ReBAC Use Cases in the Enterprise

  • Document/content management — the Google Drive sharing model itself
  • Project-based access — GitHub repository permissions, where collaborator relationships are scoped per repo
  • Hierarchical org access — a manager seeing their direct reports' data, derived from org-chart relationships
  • Fine-grained SaaS multi-tenancy — tenant-scoped resource sharing within a single application

ReBAC's Limitations

  • Complexity: the relationship graph grows large and intricate as the system matures, making it harder to reason about "what can this user ultimately reach" without running the traversal
  • Performance: graph traversal at Google scale requires purpose-built, heavily optimized storage — Zanzibar's underlying SpiceDB-style architecture exists specifically to make this fast
  • New paradigm: developers accustomed to thinking in roles need to learn to think in relationships — a genuine learning curve, not just new syntax
🟢 Architect's Bridge ReBAC is the permission model you've already seen in GitHub. You don't have a global "repository-editor" role that gives you edit access to all repositories. Instead: you are a collaborator on repo:payment-service with role "maintainer" (a relationship tuple). That relationship determines your access to that specific repository. Your access to repo:auth-service is a different relationship tuple — or none, if you were never added. GitHub's permission model is ReBAC. Google Drive's sharing model is ReBAC. Google Docs "share with specific people" is ReBAC. The pattern is everywhere in modern SaaS — you've been using it daily, just without the name.
🟣 Deep dive: What is Zanzibar's "new enemy problem," and why does it make ReBAC consistency so hard at scale?
ReBAC's relationship-graph model introduces a subtle consistency hazard that Google's Zanzibar paper named the "new enemy problem." Imagine a user removes a collaborator from a sensitive document, then immediately uploads a new sensitive paragraph. If the authorization check for that paragraph runs against a slightly stale replica of the relationship graph — one that hasn't yet caught up with the removal — the removed collaborator could briefly still see content the owner explicitly tried to hide from them, even though the removal "happened" first from the owner's point of view. This is the opposite of the usual eventual-consistency trade-off: most systems tolerate brief staleness because the cost of a slightly outdated read is low, but in authorization, a stale read can mean granting access that was just revoked. Zanzibar's solution is a consistency mechanism called "zookies" — tokens that let a request demand the relationship graph be evaluated at least as fresh as a specific prior write. OpenFGA implements an equivalent concept. The architectural lesson for anyone evaluating ReBAC: relationship-graph authorization isn't just a data-modeling decision, it's also a distributed-systems consistency decision, and the two can't be separated.

🔬 Fine-Grained Authorization Architecture

Every model covered so far — RBAC, ABAC, PBAC, ReBAC — can in principle be applied at any granularity. But most enterprise authorization, in practice, stops at "can this user access this application" or "can this user access this feature." Fine-Grained Authorization (FGA) is the discipline of pushing access control decisions down to the level of individual records, fields, or operations — not just "can this user access this application" but "can this user see this specific row in this table, this specific field in this record."

The Granularity Spectrum

Diagram 13 — Authorization Granularity Spectrum
GranularityQuestionTypical Enforcement
CoarseCan user access this application?JWT audience claim, SAML ACS URL
MediumCan user access this module/feature?RBAC role check in application
Fine-grainedCan user access this specific record?ReBAC relationship check / ABAC policy
Field-levelCan user see this specific field?Attribute masking / field-level policy
Operation-levelCan user perform this operation on this record at this time, under this context?Full PBAC/ABAC evaluation

As granularity increases, so does policy complexity and evaluation cost — each step down this spectrum is a deliberate architectural trade-off, not a free upgrade.

Why Coarse-Grained Authorization Isn't Enough

  • Healthcare: a nurse can access patient records, but only for the patients assigned to them — not the entire hospital census
  • Finance: an analyst can view reports, but not reports belonging to other business units
  • HR: a manager can view salary data for their direct reports only, not the entire company

In every one of these cases, "the user has the Analyst role" or "the user has the Nurse role" is necessary but not sufficient. Coarse RBAC gets the user in the door; fine-grained authorization decides what they see once inside.

Data-Level Security Implementation Patterns

  • Row-Level Security (RLS): the database itself enforces filtering — PostgreSQL and Oracle both support native RLS policies that transparently filter query results based on session context, regardless of how the query was written
  • Attribute-level masking: sensitive fields are masked or nulled based on the requesting subject's attributes — a salary field returns real data to HR but masked data to a peer
  • Query-time filtering: the application itself builds WHERE clauses based on the authorization context before the query ever reaches the database
  • View-based access: users query pre-filtered database views rather than base tables, with the filtering logic baked into the view definition
Diagram 14 — Row-Level Security Architecture
Application receives request from user jsmith
User's identity context: department=Finance, region=US-East
↓
Application builds query:
SELECT * FROM orders
WHERE owner_dept = :user_dept   ← from subject attribute
AND region = :user_region      ← from subject attribute
Database enforces: users only see their own org's data

Alternative pattern: database-native RLS enforces the same filter at the DB layer, regardless of how the application wrote the query — a stronger guarantee than query-time filtering alone.

The Permission Token Pattern

Some architectures embed fine-grained authorization decisions directly into tokens — a token that asserts not just "this user is authenticated" but "this user may access record IDs 4471, 4472, and 4490." This works for small, stable permission sets but breaks down quickly as the set of accessible records grows or changes frequently; most enterprise FGA architectures instead compute the decision at request time rather than trying to enumerate it in advance.

FGA and Performance

Fine-grained authorization adds latency — evaluating a policy per record, or per field, on every request is fundamentally more expensive than a single role check at login. Production FGA architectures manage this cost through caching (short-lived decision caches keyed by subject+resource+action), pre-computation (materializing accessible-record sets on a schedule rather than computing them live), and batched evaluation (asking "which of these 50 records can this user see" in one call rather than 50 separate calls).

FGA Products

A dedicated product category has emerged around this problem: AWS Verified Permissions (built on Cedar), OpenFGA, Oso, and Permify all provide purpose-built fine-grained authorization engines designed to make record-level and field-level decisions fast enough to sit in the request path of a production application.

🟢 Architect's Note — Externalize FGA, Don't Reimplement It Fine-grained authorization is where the separation of the authorization decision from the application matters most. If every service implements its own FGA logic — "show only records where owner_dept == user.department" — you get N implementations across N services, each slightly different, each independently vulnerable to mistakes. Externalized FGA (via OPA, Cedar, or OpenFGA) puts that logic in one place, governed by one team, tested by one test suite. When the business rule changes ("also allow cross-department managers to see records"), you change one policy. Not N services.
Use Case

A multinational bank operates a customer data platform serving 12 internal teams — retail, corporate, investment, compliance, fraud investigation, and audit, among others. Each team should see only customer records relevant to their function: fraud investigators need full account history for flagged accounts only; retail bankers need their own customers only; auditors need read-only access to everything, but with PII masked. Design the fine-grained authorization architecture for this platform: which granularity layer applies where, which enforcement pattern (RLS, query-time filtering, attribute masking) handles each requirement, and where in the stack the enforcement happens.

8.7

⚖️ PDP & PEP — The Policy Engine Architecture

Every authorization model covered so far — RBAC, ABAC, PBAC, ReBAC — needs somewhere to actually run. The PDP/PEP architecture is the most important structural pattern in enterprise authorization: it's the reference model that describes where policy decisions get made, where they get enforced, and why those two responsibilities must never live in the same place.

The Four Points

  • PDP — Policy Decision Point: the system that evaluates an access request against policies and returns a decision — Permit, Deny, or an obligation (e.g., "permit, but require step-up authentication")
  • PEP — Policy Enforcement Point: the system that intercepts the access request, calls the PDP, and enforces whatever decision comes back
  • PAP — Policy Administration Point: where policies are authored, stored, and managed — the governance front-end for the PDP's rule set
  • PIP — Policy Information Point: where the PDP fetches the attribute data it needs to evaluate a policy — the user's directory record, the resource's metadata, the environment's risk score

This four-point model traces directly to the XACML reference architecture, and while not every modern deployment uses all four names, the responsibilities they describe are present in every serious PBAC implementation.

Why PDP/PEP Separation Matters

  • The PEP enforces — it does not decide
  • The PDP decides — it does not enforce
  • This separation allows a single PDP to serve many PEPs across many services, with consistent policy applied everywhere
  • Policy changes happen at the PAP — never inside individual PEPs — so a rule change doesn't require redeploying every service that enforces it
  • The PEP is the "always enforce" point: if the PDP is unreachable, the PEP must fail closed — a principle important enough to warrant its own callout below
Diagram 15 — PDP / PEP / PAP / PIP Architecture: Full Model

🟢 Architect's Note — You Already Know This Pattern The PDP/PEP separation is the same separation of concerns you apply in Java service design. The service doesn't make business logic decisions inline — it calls a domain service that holds the business rules. The PEP (service) doesn't make authorization decisions inline — it calls the PDP (authorization service) that holds the policies. The result is the same: the logic is testable, replaceable, and independently governed. If you've ever extracted business logic from a controller into a service layer, you understand the PDP/PEP architecture instinctively. This module just gives it a name and shows you how it scales to enterprise authorization.
🟣 Security — PEP Fail-Closed Is Non-Negotiable If the PEP cannot reach the PDP (network timeout, PDP outage), it must deny access — not permit it. A PEP that fails open ("if I can't reach the policy engine, let the user through") creates a complete authorization bypass whenever the PDP is unavailable. Attackers who can isolate a PEP from its PDP — via network manipulation or by deliberately causing a PDP outage — gain unrestricted access. Design every PEP with fail-closed as the default, and test it: disconnect the PDP, confirm the PEP denies all requests, confirm the error is logged.
🟣 Deep dive: What does the XACML request/response model define, and what do NotApplicable and Indeterminate actually mean?
XACML defines four possible decision outcomes, and the distinction between the last two is one architects routinely get wrong. Permit means a policy explicitly allowed the request. Deny means a policy explicitly denied it. NotApplicable means no policy in the system addressed this request at all — it fell outside the scope of every policy that was evaluated. That's architecturally very different from Indeterminate, which means a policy that should have applied could not be evaluated — a missing attribute, an unreachable PIP, a malformed policy. The reason this distinction matters operationally: a well-designed PDP treats NotApplicable and Indeterminate completely differently from each other in its default behavior. NotApplicable typically falls through to a default-deny policy at the PolicySet level — "nothing said yes, so the answer is no." Indeterminate is a system failure mode and should be treated with the same fail-closed discipline as a PDP outage: if the engine couldn't actually evaluate the request, it must not be allowed to default to Permit just because no Deny was returned.
Use Case

It has 12 microservices, 3 external APIs, and data that ranges from public (interest rate tables) to confidential (individual account balances) to restricted (fraud investigation records). 🗂️ Entitlement Architecture

Every authorization model discussed so far — RBAC, ABAC, PBAC, ReBAC — ultimately resolves to the same atomic unit: the entitlement. An entitlement is the specific right to perform a specific action on a specific resource — the finest-grained unit of authorization in an enterprise IAM model, and the layer where identity governance actually does its work.

The Entitlement Hierarchy

Entitlements sit at the bottom of a layered structure, with roles and permissions organizing them above:

Entitlement (can-approve-PO > $50K in SAP)
  → Permission (approve-purchase-order)
    → Role (Finance-Manager)
      → User (jsmith)

The distinction between a role and an entitlement is the distinction between a business concept and a technical artifact. A role is a logical grouping of permissions tied to a business function — "Finance-Manager" means something to a manager doing an access review. An entitlement is the actual technical permission on a specific system — a SAP transaction code, a Salesforce permission set, a GitHub repository access level. Roles are how humans think about access. Entitlements are how systems implement it.

Application Entitlements

Every application defines its own entitlement model, and they rarely look alike: SAP expresses entitlements as transaction codes and authorization objects; Salesforce combines profiles with permission sets; GitHub expresses entitlements as per-repository access levels; Oracle EBS uses responsibilities. None of these map cleanly onto each other, which is precisely why a centralized entitlement catalog (covered next) is necessary at enterprise scale — without it, no one can answer "what does this person actually have access to across all our systems" without manually querying a dozen different applications, each with its own entitlement vocabulary.

The Entitlement Sprawl Problem

Each enterprise application typically exposes hundreds or thousands of entitlements, and every one of them theoretically requires governance — someone needs to know it exists, understand what access it grants, and periodically confirm that the people who hold it still need it. This is the central IGA challenge: the entitlement count across a mid-sized enterprise's application portfolio routinely runs into the tens of thousands, dwarfing the role count by an order of magnitude or more.

The Entitlement Catalog

An entitlement catalog is the IGA system's authoritative map of every technical permission across every managed application, paired with a business-readable name and description. Saviynt maintains this catalog as one of its core functions — ingesting raw entitlement data from each connected application and mapping cryptic technical values (a SAP T-code, a Salesforce permission-set API name) to descriptions a business approver can actually understand and certify.

Entitlements and IGA

Saviynt governs entitlement assignment through the full lifecycle: access requests route through approval workflows, periodic access certification campaigns ask managers to re-confirm that an entitlement assignment is still appropriate, and SoD conflict detection runs continuously against the entitlement catalog to catch toxic combinations before — or shortly after — they're granted.

Entitlements in Tokens

OIDC and SAML tokens typically carry only a subset of a user's full entitlement picture — group memberships, coarse roles, a handful of claims. Full entitlement sets rarely fit cleanly into a token: the token-size problem is real (large tokens slow every request and can exceed header-size limits), and entitlement data changes more frequently than a token's lifetime would tolerate. The practical pattern is that applications use the token to establish identity and coarse role, then look up the fuller entitlement set from the IGA platform or directory layer as needed — rather than trying to cram everything into the token itself.

Privileged Entitlements

A subset of entitlements represents meaningfully elevated, high-risk access — database admin rights, production deployment permissions, the ability to modify security configurations. These privileged entitlements are typically pulled out of standard IGA governance and managed instead by a dedicated PAM system like CyberArk, which adds session recording, just-in-time elevation, and tighter approval controls than standard entitlement governance provides. Module 10 covers this in full.

🔵 Entitlements Are the Atomic Unit Entitlements are the atomic unit of enterprise authorization. Every other concept — roles, permissions, policies — is an organizing layer on top of entitlements. When Saviynt runs an access certification campaign, it's asking managers to certify that specific entitlements on specific applications are still appropriate for each user. When a SoD rule fires, it's detecting that two specific entitlements would create a conflict if held simultaneously. When a provisioning workflow runs, it's assigning specific entitlements to a specific user in a specific application. The entitlement — the leaf node of the authorization tree — is where identity governance does its most important work.

🧭 Authorization Model Selection Framework

Eight sections in, you've now seen four distinct authorization models and the infrastructure that runs them. The question every architect eventually faces isn't "which model is best" — it's "which model fits this specific decision." This section gives you the selection framework.

When to Use RBAC

  • Relatively stable user populations and access patterns
  • Business roles map clearly to permission sets
  • The number of distinct roles is manageable (generally under 500)
  • Compliance frameworks require role-based evidence (SOX, PCI-DSS audits expect role-based access reviews)
  • Applications support role claims natively

Use for: enterprise SaaS apps, most internal apps, AD group-based authorization.

When to Use ABAC

  • Context matters — time, location, device, risk level genuinely change the decision
  • Roles would explode trying to handle every edge case
  • Resources carry classification attributes that should drive access directly
  • Data residency or regulatory context varies per-resource

Use for: cloud IAM (AWS IAM, Azure RBAC), API gateway policies, data classification-driven access.

When to Use PBAC

  • Authorization logic must be centrally governed, not scattered across services
  • Policies need to be versioned, audited, and tested like code
  • Multiple applications share common authorization logic
  • The authorization decision must be explainable and auditable for regulators

Use for: Kubernetes authorization, API platforms, regulated industries, multi-application enterprise deployments.

When to Use ReBAC

  • Access is defined by relationships between users and specific resources, not by global roles
  • Hierarchical resource ownership is central to the business model
  • Resource-specific sharing is a core use case, not an edge case

Use for: document/content management, project-based tools, multi-tenant SaaS, organizational hierarchies.

Hybrid Models — The Reality of Enterprise Architecture

Most mature enterprise architectures use multiple models simultaneously, layered by granularity rather than chosen as a single exclusive approach:

  • RBAC for coarse-grained application access
  • ABAC for context-sensitive exceptions on top of RBAC
  • PBAC for centralized policy governance across services
  • ReBAC for specific resource-sharing use cases
  • Fine-grained authorization at the data layer for record- and field-level control

The Evolution Path

Most enterprises follow a predictable maturity path: they start with RBAC because it's the simplest model to reason about and the easiest to explain to auditors. They hit RBAC's limits — role explosion, context blindness — and add ABAC to handle the exceptions that were driving role sprawl. As governance and audit requirements grow, they adopt PBAC to centralize and formalize policy logic that had been scattered across application code. Finally, specific product needs — document sharing, project-based collaboration — drive targeted ReBAC adoption for those particular resource types. Few enterprises arrive at ReBAC or PBAC first; the path runs through RBAC.

Knowledge Check

Q1 A developer says they've implemented "RBAC" in their microservice by checking if the user's JWT contains a specific claim value. Is this RBAC? If not, what is it? And what architectural problem does this approach have at enterprise scale?

Architect's Answer Technically, checking a JWT claim for a role value is RBAC — roles are the basis of the decision. But architecturally, this is a PEP without a PDP: the decision logic (which claim values permit which actions) is embedded in the service code, not in a centralized policy engine. At enterprise scale, this means N services each implement their own interpretation of the same role → permission logic, inevitably diverging. When a policy changes, every service must be redeployed. No single place records what the current access rules actually are. The architectural fix is to externalize the decision to a PDP (OPA sidecar, PBAC) while the service remains the PEP that enforces — not decides.

Q2 Your access review finds that 340 users have the Finance-Manager RBAC role but only 12 actually need to approve purchase orders. The remaining 328 inherited the role for a single read-only report. What authorization architectural problem does this reveal, and what are three approaches to solving it?

Architect's Answer This is role explosion driven by using a permission-level role (Finance-Manager) to grant a single narrow capability (read-only report access). Three remediation approaches: (1) Role splitting — decompose Finance-Manager into Finance-Manager (approvals only) + Finance-Reports-Reader (read-only), and assign the lighter role to the 328 users. (2) ABAC exception — remove report access from the role entirely and express it as an ABAC attribute policy: any user with department=Finance can read the report, eliminating the need for the role assignment. (3) Entitlement governance — run an access certification campaign that forces managers to re-justify each of the 340 assignments, removing anyone who can't articulate why they need Finance-Manager rather than a read-only access path.

Q3 Design the PDP/PEP architecture for a financial API platform with 8 microservices that all need to enforce the same access control policy: only users from the requesting user's own business unit can access that unit's financial data, unless they hold the "Finance-Group-Controller" entitlement. Where do PEPs sit? What PDP is called? How are attributes fetched?

Architect's Answer PEPs sit at the API gateway level (coarse gating) and as middleware in each of the 8 microservices (fine-grained). All PEPs call a shared OPA instance (PDP) deployed as a sidecar cluster. The OPA policy expresses the rule: allow IF subject.businessUnit == resource.businessUnit OR subject.entitlements contains "Finance-Group-Controller". Attributes are fetched from two PIPs: user attributes (businessUnit, entitlements) from PingDirectory/LDAP, injected into the JWT at login and verified by OPA; resource attributes (businessUnit ownership) from a resource metadata service OPA queries at evaluation time. OPA is deployed with redundant replicas. PEPs are configured fail-closed — if OPA is unreachable, all requests deny with a 503.

Q4 Explain why a healthcare system that implements RBAC alone ("Doctors can access patient records") is insufficient for HIPAA compliance — and which authorization models address each gap.

Architect's Answer RBAC alone creates three HIPAA compliance gaps. First, minimum necessary access: HIPAA requires healthcare workers to access only the PHI required for their current task — a Doctor role granting access to all patient records violates this; ReBAC (treating-physician relationship) scopes access to assigned patients. Second, context-sensitive access: HIPAA expects controls on when and from where PHI is accessed — ABAC enforces shift hours, device compliance, and network segment. Third, audit and accountability: HIPAA mandates that access decisions be logged with enough context to reconstruct the justification — PBAC with centralized decision logging provides this; inline RBAC checks in application code typically do not.

Q5 A PDP is unavailable for 3 minutes. The PEP is configured to fail-open (permit all requests if PDP unreachable). An attacker who caused the partition now has 3 minutes of unrestricted access. Walk through the design change that prevents this and the operational implications of fail-closed PEP behavior.

Architect's Answer The design change is straightforward: the PEP must return a deny (HTTP 403 or equivalent) with a retry-after signal whenever the PDP call times out or returns an error. This is a single configuration change in most PEP implementations — switching from a fallback permit to a fallback deny. The operational implications are real and must be planned for: any PDP outage now affects all requests, not silently. This means (1) the PDP must be treated as a tier-1, high-availability service with redundant replicas across availability zones; (2) runbooks must cover "PDP degraded" scenarios explicitly; (3) short-lived decision caches (30–60 seconds) on the PEP can buffer brief PDP hiccups without failing open — the cache serves recent decisions while the PDP recovers, and a cache miss fails closed, not open. The business conversation is also necessary: stakeholders who today accept "the app was briefly permissive" will need to accept "the app briefly returned errors" as the alternative — a trade-off between security and availability that must be consciously chosen.

Real-World Scenario — The Authorization Audit Finding

Situation

An external security audit of a 15,000-employee enterprise finds: 12,000 RBAC roles in the IGA system (role explosion); 340 users with conflicting SoD entitlements; authorization logic embedded in 47 microservices, each implementing its own rules differently; no central PDP — all authorization decisions are made inline in application code; and access certification last completed 14 months ago.

Root Cause Analysis

The 12,000-role count is the clearest indicator: roles were created reactively, one per exception, over many years — no governance gate existed to prevent new role creation. The SoD violations exist because no automated SoD engine evaluated new entitlement assignments at provisioning time — the controls were purely manual and therefore not consistently applied. Authorization logic in 47 services means 47 independent policy implementations, each with its own interpretation of the same business rules — divergence was inevitable and undetectable without an audit. The 14-month certification gap means no one has validated that any of the 12,000 roles are still appropriate for their current holders.

Immediate Risk Mitigation (0–30 days)

  • Emergency SoD remediation: pull the 340 conflicting entitlement assignments and route them through accelerated manager review. Any not justified within 10 business days are revoked.
  • Freeze new role creation: no new roles without architectural review and IGA team approval.
  • Access certification campaign: launch an emergency certification for the highest-risk applications (finance, HR, infrastructure access) even before the full cert program restarts.

90-Day Architecture

  • Role mining: use Saviynt's role mining engine to identify candidate rationalized roles from the observed permission pattern. Target: reduce 12,000 roles to under 2,000 defensible business roles.
  • Centralized PDP: deploy OPA as a shared policy decision point, integrated with the API gateway PEP. Begin migrating the 47 services from inline authorization to PEP → PDP calls, starting with the highest-risk services.
  • SoD automation: configure Saviynt preventive SoD controls for all provisioning workflows — any new entitlement assignment is evaluated against the SoD rulebook at request time, not discovered months later in audit.

12-Month Target State

A layered authorization architecture: RBAC via a rationalized role catalog (~1,500 roles) governs coarse application access. ABAC policies in OPA handle context-sensitive exceptions that previously drove role explosion. ReBAC via OpenFGA manages project- and document-specific sharing in the two platforms where that model fits. All 47 microservices are PEP-only — no embedded authorization logic. Access certification runs quarterly, automated by Saviynt, with certification rates tracked as a governance KPI.

Authorization Governance Model

An Authorization Steering Committee meets monthly: IAM engineering (owns PDP, policy repository), Application Security (reviews new policy changes), IGA (owns role catalog and entitlement governance), and Business Risk (certifies that the role model reflects actual business functions). Policy changes go through a PR review and automated test suite before deployment to the PDP — the same discipline applied to application code, now applied to authorization policy.
