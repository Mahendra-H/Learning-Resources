Scene 01 — The Line That Moves

The Shared Responsibility Model, Not the Marketing Slide

Every AWS training deck shows the same picture: AWS secures "of the cloud," you secure "in the cloud." It's not wrong, but it's a sentence, not a mental model — and the exam does not test sentences. It tests whether you can look at a service you've never touched before and correctly reason about where the line sits. That phrasing itself is worth pausing on: "of the cloud" means the physical and virtualization layer that makes AWS possible at all; "in the cloud" means everything you built, configured, or uploaded into that layer. The confusion isn't in the words — it's that people memorize the phrase instead of the reasoning behind it, and the reasoning is what breaks down under exam pressure.

Here's the part the marketing slide skips: the line is not fixed. It slides along a spectrum depending on how much of the stack AWS abstracts away from you. On one end sits EC2 — pure infrastructure-as-a-service. AWS secures the physical hosts, the hypervisor, and the network fabric beneath your instance; you own the guest operating system, every patch inside it, your security group rules, and anything you install. Move one step up to RDS and the line shifts: AWS now manages the underlying database engine's patching, backups, and the host OS, but you still choose your parameter groups, control network access via security groups, and decide what's inside the database and who can query it. Move further to ECS or EKS and the line fractures into three separate pieces at once — AWS operates the control plane (the API server, etcd, scheduler), but on EKS with self-managed or even managed node groups, you are still responsible for patching the worker node OS, and you are always responsible for what's baked into your container image, because AWS never inspects or patches your image contents. Keep moving toward Lambda, S3, and DynamoDB and the abstraction becomes nearly total: AWS owns the runtime, the OS, the scaling, the physical layer, and the patching of everything underneath your code. What's left is narrow but non-negotiable — your code's logic, your IAM permissions, and your data.

And here is the sentence the exam is actually testing, over and over, in different disguises: no matter how abstracted the service, three things never move off your side of the line — data classification (deciding what's sensitive), IAM configuration (deciding who can reach it), and client-side encryption choices (deciding whether you encrypt before it ever reaches AWS). A serverless function doesn't decide those things for you. Neither does S3. Those are judgment calls only you can make, and the exam will hand you a scenario about the most "hands-off" service in the catalog specifically to see if you remember that.

On the other end, some things never move at all, and the exam never treats these as ambiguous: physical data center security, hardware lifecycle, and global network infrastructure are always AWS, full stop, regardless of which service you're using. If an answer choice suggests a customer action related to physical security, it's a distractor.

Production Scenario

The Migration That Assumed Too Much

A mid-size fintech moved its transaction-matching service from on-prem VMs to EC2 over a single sprint. The infra team's mental model going in was simple: "AWS is now hosting our servers, so AWS is now securing our servers." Six weeks later, an external scan flagged a dozen EC2 instances still running a kernel version with a known privilege-escalation CVE — a patch that had been available for eleven weeks. Nobody had applied it, because nobody on the team believed patching the guest OS was still their job once the workload "lived in AWS." The instances were, technically, exactly as secure as AWS's shared responsibility model always said they would be: AWS had patched the hypervisor flawlessly. The guest OS — the thing actually running the vulnerable kernel — was never AWS's to patch in the first place. The fix wasn't a tool, it was a job assignment: patch management for guest OS layers became an explicit, owned, on-call responsibility with its own SLA, the same as it had been on-prem. The principle that would have prevented it: treating "moved to AWS" as synonymous with "AWS secures it," instead of asking, service by service, exactly where the line actually sits.

Reference — Patching Responsibility

The Three Patching Layers People Confuse

These three get tangled together constantly, and the exam relies on that: guest OS patching on EC2 (yours), the underlying host and hypervisor (always AWS, never yours, on any service), and RDS engine patching (AWS performs it, but you schedule it within a maintenance window — it is managed, not automatic on AWS's own silent timetable).

SERVICE OS / HOST RUNTIME / ENGINE APPLICATION CODE

Common mistake

Reading "AWS manages RDS patching" as "patching happens automatically, with no action from me." In reality, AWS applies the engine patch, but you approve or schedule the maintenance window — skip that step long enough and you're the reason a critical engine CVE stayed open. "Managed" is not the same word as "automatic," and the exam will use that gap deliberately.

Exam angle

Expect a scenario naming an unfamiliar or oddly specific service and asking "who patches X?" Don't pattern-match to the service name — reason from the abstraction level. Ask: does the customer manage the OS at all? If no OS is customer-visible, patching responsibility for that layer isn't yours. If an OS is customer-visible (even on a "managed" service), assume you own it unless told otherwise.

Scene 02 — Principles, Not Slogans

The Well-Architected Security Pillar

AWS's Well-Architected Framework names a short list of security design principles. Trainers list them and move on; the exam expects you to recognize each one in a scenario where it isn't labeled. Each principle below comes with the judgment call it actually forces you to make.

Every scenario question in this exam is quietly asking the same thing: did you reach for a single control, or a layered one?

Scene 03 — Defense-in-Depth

Five Layers, One Attacker

Defense-in-depth means no single layer is trusted to stop everything on its own — edge, network, compute, application, and data each assume the layers around them might fail. The exam never accepts "just use a WAF" or "just tighten IAM" as a complete answer, because real incidents rarely get stopped at the first layer they hit. They get stopped — or don't — layer by layer.

Walk one scenario through all five: an attacker finds a SQL injection flaw in a public web app hosted on EC2 behind an Application Load Balancer.

Common mistake

Treating one strong layer as sufficient because it "would have caught it." A WAF rule tuned for SQL injection is real protection — but if it's misconfigured, bypassed by an encoding trick, or simply not covering a newer endpoint, and it's the only control, the attacker walks straight through to the database. Layers exist because any single one can fail silently.

Exam angle

When a scenario asks "what's the BEST way to protect X," and one answer choice is a single control while another combines two or three complementary controls, the layered answer is usually correct — not because more is automatically better, but because the exam is testing whether you understand that no individual AWS service is designed to be a complete security boundary by itself.

Scene 04 — Why One Account Doesn't Scale

AWS Organizations, the Real Mechanics

AWS Organizations gives you a management account (formerly called the "master" or "payer" account) and any number of member accounts underneath it. The management account can create and close member accounts, consolidate billing, and apply organization-wide policies — but it should almost never run actual workloads itself, because it's the single most sensitive account in the whole structure; if it's compromised, everything beneath it is at risk. This is a genuinely common exam trap: people assume the management account is just "the admin account" and deploy things into it out of convenience.

Scene 05 — Landing Zone, Conceptually

More Than "Has Multiple Accounts"

A landing zone is not a synonym for "we use AWS Organizations." It's a standardized, guardrail-enforced baseline that every new account is born into — automatically, not by someone remembering to configure it by hand. Three things a real landing zone standardizes: account vending (new accounts come from a repeatable template, not an ad-hoc console click-through), baseline guardrails (SCPs, mandatory logging, mandatory encryption defaults applied the moment an account exists, not added later if someone remembers), and centralized logging (every account's CloudTrail and Config data flows to a dedicated, access-restricted logging account from day one).

The distinction that matters for the exam: an organization can have a dozen AWS accounts and zero landing zone — accounts created manually, each with different logging setups, different baseline permissions, inconsistent guardrails. That's "multiple accounts" without the standardization that makes multi-account actually safer. AWS Control Tower is the service that operationalizes landing zone concepts, but the deep mechanics of Control Tower, account factory, and guardrail enforcement are Module 12's job — here, just recognize what a landing zone is for.
