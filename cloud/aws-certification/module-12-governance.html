SCS-C03 · MODULE 12 · Governance
DOMAIN SHIFT · 2 → 6

Everything you built has been leading here.

Module 11 closed with you inside a single account, walking a security incident from detection through containment to recovery. Necessary work, but narrow work — one account, one blast radius, one timeline.

Zoom out now. The organization you actually operate inside isn't one account. It's dozens, sometimes hundreds — a workload account per team, a sandbox for experimentation, a log-archive account from Module 9 quietly ingesting CloudTrail from everywhere, a forensics account from Module 11 waiting for the next incident. None of that coheres into a security posture on its own. Something has to hold the shape.

That something is governance — Domain 6, 14% of the exam, and the domain this module opens. Module 3 gave you SCPs and RCPs as tools attached to individual accounts. Module 9 gave you centralized logging as a pattern. This module is where those threads finally assemble into one structure: an organization of accounts that is governed, not just monitored — where the right guardrail exists at the right scope before an engineer ever has the chance to violate it.

If Module 11 was about how you respond when something goes wrong in one account, this module is about designing the organization so that "wrong" becomes structurally rarer in the first place — and so that when it does happen, it's contained by design, not by luck.

CONCEPT 1 OF 6

AWS Organizations, at real depth

Module 1 introduced Organizations as the container that lets you manage multiple accounts centrally. Here's the part that actually matters for the exam and for real architecture: the organizational unit (OU) is not a folder for tidiness. It's the structural mechanism by which every policy you attach — SCPs restricting what's possible, RCPs restricting what resource policies can grant, tag policies enforcing naming — gets applied at a scope larger than one account.

OUs nest. You can put OUs inside OUs, and every account inherits every policy attached anywhere above it, all the way to the organization root. That inheritance is the entire point. Design your OU tree around real organizational boundaries — environment (prod vs. non-prod), business unit, compliance tier — and a single SCP attached at the right level governs every account under it, present and future, without touching a single account individually.

Consolidated billing is the other half of Organizations, and it's not just a finance feature. One consolidated view of spend across every linked account is also, quietly, an anomaly-detection signal — the same mindset Module 10 built around unusual API activity applies to unusual spend. A sandbox account suddenly running a spot-instance fleet at 3x its historical spend is worth the same kind of attention as an unusual GuardDuty finding.

A practical naming discipline matters more than it sounds like it should. OUs named after org-chart teams ("Platform-Team-OU", "Data-Team-OU") tend to need restructuring every time the company reorganizes, because the OU boundary was never actually about security in the first place — it just happened to track headcount. OUs named after the security-relevant property they enforce ("Prod-Tier1-Compliance", "Non-Prod-Sandbox") survive reorgs, because the property they're protecting — a compliance tier, an environment class — doesn't move just because reporting lines do. This is worth internalizing before you ever draw the tree: name OUs for what they guarantee, not for who currently owns the accounts inside them.

It's also worth being precise about what an OU actually is under the hood: a grouping construct with policies attached to it, not a container with its own independent identity or resources. An OU can't itself run workloads or be assumed into — it exists purely to be a scope that SCPs, RCPs, and tag policies attach to, and that accounts sit inside of for inheritance purposes. That distinction avoids a common early confusion where someone tries to treat an OU like it's an account.

Production scenario

A financial services company runs 60 AWS accounts under one Organization: one per product team, split further into dev/staging/prod. Their security team needs every account — current and any created next quarter — to inherit a baseline: no root user activity, mandatory encryption at rest, and CloudTrail that can't be disabled. They attach these controls as SCPs and RCPs at the root of the Organization, once, rather than replicating them into 60 individual account policies that would immediately start drifting out of sync. Six months later, when the company acquires a smaller competitor and onboards 15 new accounts, those accounts inherit the exact same baseline automatically, the moment they join the Organization under the right OU — no separate rollout project required.

Exam angle

The exam will describe an OU restructuring and ask what a moved account gains or loses. The answer always traces back to one rule: an account inherits every SCP and RCP attached to every OU above it in the hierarchy, all the way to the root. Move the account, change what it inherits.

CONCEPT 2 OF 6

AWS Control Tower — automating the baseline

Control Tower is best understood as three things bundled together. First, a landing zone: a pre-configured multi-account baseline — a Management account, a dedicated Log Archive account, a dedicated Audit account — set up correctly on day one instead of assembled by hand over months. Second, a set of guardrails, split into three tiers: mandatory (always on, cannot be disabled — things like preventing CloudTrail from being disabled), strongly recommended (best practice, deployable org-wide), and elective (optional, situational). Third, Account Factory: a repeatable way to provision new accounts that are compliant from the moment they're created.

Here's the distinction the exam leans on hardest in this section: Control Tower does not replace SCPs. It deploys and manages them as part of its guardrail system — a "guardrail" in Control Tower's vocabulary is frequently just an SCP under the hood, alongside AWS Config rules for the guardrails that are detective rather than preventive. Control Tower is the automation and management layer; SCPs and Config rules are still the actual enforcement mechanisms doing the work underneath it.

Control Tower also continuously checks each enrolled account against its expected baseline — a process it calls drift detection. If someone manually edits an SCP that Control Tower deployed, or disables the Config recorder in an enrolled account, Control Tower flags that account as drifted from its landing zone configuration. This matters because it means the guardrails aren't a one-time deployment that can silently erode — there's an ongoing mechanism specifically watching for the baseline being tampered with, which is a meaningfully different guarantee than "we configured it correctly once."

Click to expand: the three guardrail tiers, and what enforces each ▾

Mandatory — always enforced, no opt-out (e.g., prevent disabling of centralized logging). Backed by SCPs.
Strongly recommended — best-practice guardrails AWS recommends broadly (e.g., detect unencrypted EBS volumes). Often backed by Config rules — detective, not preventive.
Elective — situational, opt-in guardrails you choose based on your own risk posture. Can be either SCP-backed (preventive) or Config-rule-backed (detective) depending on the specific guardrail.

Production scenario

A newly acquired subsidiary needs to be onboarded into the parent company's AWS Organization within a week, with the same security baseline as every existing account — encrypted storage, centralized logging, no public S3 buckets. Instead of manually replicating dozens of settings, the platform team provisions the new accounts through Account Factory. Every account arrives already enrolled in the landing zone, already inheriting the baseline SCPs, already shipping logs to the Log Archive account — compliant from birth.

The instinct when governance feels behind is to retrofit — bolt SCPs and logging onto accounts that already exist and are already drifted. Account Factory flips that: a new account that's compliant from the moment it's created is a fundamentally stronger security posture than the best retrofit you could ever apply afterward. Design for that from the start if you can.

Trap

A common wrong answer: assuming Control Tower is a replacement for SCPs, and that removing Control Tower would leave accounts ungoverned. In reality the SCPs and Config rules it deployed persist and continue enforcing — Control Tower manages them, it doesn't uniquely embody them.

CONCEPT 3 OF 6

Landing zone patterns, made concrete

Module 1 introduced the landing zone conceptually. Here's a structure you'll actually recognize in the field — four top-level OUs, each doing a distinct job:

Security OU — houses the Log Archive account (Module 9's centralized CloudTrail/Config destination) and the Audit account (read-only cross-account visibility for the security team). Tighter guardrails than anywhere else in the org; almost nothing is elective here.
Sandbox OU — loosely governed experimentation space, deliberately permissive so engineers can prototype, with guardrails limited to the truly non-negotiable ones (no public data exposure, spend caps).
Workloads OU — split further into Prod and Non-Prod sub-OUs, since a production database account and a dev account for the same team have very different guardrail needs despite sharing a parent.
Suspended OU — where accounts land the moment they're being decommissioned; an SCP here denies essentially everything, so a suspended account can't quietly keep incurring cost or exposure while offboarding paperwork catches up.

Notice what this does: the log-archive account and the forensics account from Module 11 both live inside the Security OU, under the tightest guardrails in the organization — because the accounts holding your evidence and your detection data are exactly the accounts you can least afford to have loosely governed.

It's worth being explicit about why these are separate accounts rather than a folder inside an existing workload account. Account boundaries in AWS are the strongest isolation boundary available short of a separate cloud provider entirely — a compromised workload account cannot reach into a separate log-archive account's data unless a cross-account role was deliberately granted, and even then only with the specific permissions that role was scoped to. Putting logs in a dedicated account means an attacker who fully compromises a workload account still can't tamper with the evidence of what they did, because that evidence was never reachable from where they landed. This is the same blast-radius-minimization thread Module 3 introduced and Module 11 reinforced, now expressed as an account-topology decision rather than an IAM-policy decision.

The Suspended OU deserves one more note: it isn't only for planned decommissioning. It's equally the right first move for an account you suspect may be compromised and want to contain immediately while investigation proceeds — moving it into an OU with a near-total-deny SCP is a fast, structural way to stop further damage without needing to individually revoke every credential and permission inside that account first.

Trap

Treating OU structure as an org-chart exercise — mirroring reporting lines instead of security boundaries. Two teams that report to different VPs but have identical compliance needs should often share an OU; two teams under the same VP with different data sensitivity often shouldn't.

PREVENTIVE STOPS IT.

DETECTIVE JUST NOTICES IT HAPPENED.

The single most tested distinction in this domain. Get comfortable with it before you go further.

CONCEPT 4 OF 6

Preventive vs. detective guardrails

A preventive guardrail — mechanically, almost always an SCP or RCP — makes an action structurally impossible. There is no window of time where the violation could occur, because the API call itself is denied at evaluation time, before anything happens. If an SCP denies s3:PutBucketPolicy with a public-access condition, that bucket is never public, not even for a second.

A detective guardrail — an AWS Config rule, a Security Hub standard — allows the action to occur, then evaluates afterward and flags that it violated policy. That's genuinely valuable when a preventive block isn't feasible (some actions are too broad to safely deny outright) or would be overly restrictive for legitimate work. But it inherently leaves a window — however brief — where non-compliance existed before anyone or anything caught it.

This is not a stylistic choice between two roughly-equivalent tools. It's a decision about whether a window of exposure is acceptable for the specific risk in front of you. For a control like "no S3 bucket in this org is ever public," the risk of even a brief window is unacceptable — that's an RCP, preventive, no exceptions. For a control like "resources should be tagged with a cost center," a brief window of non-compliance is annoying, not dangerous — Config rule, detective, remediate on a schedule.

There's a practical reason detective guardrails exist at all rather than everything simply being made preventive: some legitimate actions are too broad, too situational, or too operationally necessary to safely deny outright. Blocking every IAM policy change organization-wide would also block the emergency access grant a team needs during an actual incident. In cases like that, a detective control — flag any IAM policy change that grants broad administrative access, then have a human or automated process review it quickly — accepts a short window of risk in exchange for not breaking legitimate operations. The skill this domain tests isn't "always prefer preventive" — it's correctly judging, for a specific described requirement, which side of that trade-off the situation actually falls on.

PREVENTIVE — SCP
DETECTIVE — Config rule

Decision walkthrough

Click: "Can any account ever make a data store publicly readable?" ▾

Step 1 — Is the consequence of even one instance unacceptable (data exposure, credential leak, disabled logging)? Yes here.
Step 2 — Is there a legitimate business case where this action is needed at all? No — no workload legitimately needs a public data store in this org.
Step 3 — Since the answer to "ever acceptable" is no, block the API call itself. Preventive: RCP or SCP. Only when a legitimate use case exists but needs monitoring rather than blocking do you move to detective.

Exam angle

Explicitly flagged as a recurring exam point: a question describes a requirement and two answer choices differ only in preventive vs. detective mechanism. Ask yourself first whether any window of non-compliance is tolerable. If not, the answer is preventive, full stop.

CONCEPT 5 OF 6

SCPs and RCPs, enforced at scale

Module 3 introduced SCPs and RCPs conceptually, likely attached close to individual accounts. At organizational scale, the practical mechanic that matters is applying them at the OU level instead — one attachment, every current and future account under that OU inherits it. This is exactly why OU design, as covered above, is a security decision and not just an org-chart exercise: get the tree wrong, and you either under-govern accounts that needed the guardrail or over-govern accounts that didn't.

Inheritance is strictly additive down the tree: an account is subject to every SCP attached to its own OU, every SCP attached to each OU above it, and every SCP attached to the root — there's no mechanism to "opt out" of a policy from a level above by attaching something more permissive lower down. SCPs and RCPs only restrict; they never grant. The most restrictive applicable policy always wins.

RCPs deserve their own moment at this scale specifically because of what they let you guarantee organization-wide, regardless of any individual account's own policy. An RCP attached at the Organization root stating that no S3 bucket resource policy anywhere in the org may grant public access holds even if some engineer, in some account, writes a resource policy that tries to make a bucket public. The RCP evaluates first and wins. That's Module 3's RCP content, but applied at the scope where it actually earns its keep.

Click: trace an SCP from root to a Prod account ▾

Root: RCP — no S3 bucket may ever be public, org-wide. Applies to this account.
→ Workloads OU: SCP — deny disabling of CloudTrail. Applies to this account.
→ Prod sub-OU: SCP — deny any IAM action outside a change-managed pipeline role. Applies to this account.
Effective policy on the account: all three, combined, most-restrictive-wins. The account cannot make an S3 bucket public, cannot disable CloudTrail, and cannot make IAM changes outside the pipeline — even though only one of those three was written specifically for Prod.

When someone asks "why is this Prod account blocked from an action that seems unrelated to Prod," the answer is almost always three or four OUs up, not in the account itself. Debugging SCP-related denials means walking the OU tree upward, not just reading the account's own policy.

Trap

An SCP meant only for a newly-created sandbox team gets attached at the Workloads OU instead of a scoped sub-OU beneath it — and silently restricts every Prod and Non-Prod account under Workloads too. This is an inheritance mistake, not a permissions mistake, and it's exactly the kind of scenario the exam likes to describe.

CONCEPT 6 OF 6

Tagging strategy as a governance tool

Tagging gets dismissed as a cost-allocation nicety, and that framing badly undersells it. A consistent, enforced tagging strategy — environment, owner, cost-center, data-classification — is a security and governance mechanism in its own right, because tags are what make automation scopeable. A Config rule scoped to data-classification=restricted can apply stricter checks only where they're needed. A remediation automation can target exactly the resources tagged environment=prod. And access control itself can key off tags directly, through condition keys like aws:ResourceTag — a policy that grants access only to resources tagged with a matching cost-center or team is enforcing authorization based on tags, not just organizing spend by them.

None of that works if tags are inconsistent — one team using Env, another using environment, a third leaving it blank. Tag policies, defined through Organizations, are the mechanism for enforcing tag key/value consistency across every account in the org — they don't just document a convention, they let you flag or prevent non-compliant tagging. Pair that with an SCP that requires specific tags be present at resource creation time (denying ec2:RunInstances without a cost-center tag, for instance), and you've made tagging discipline structural rather than aspirational.

Tag governance also has a lifecycle problem that's easy to overlook: a tag that was correctly required and enforced when a resource was created can still go stale. An account gets sold to a different cost center, a workload's data classification changes after a new dataset gets added to it, and the original tag never gets updated because nothing forces a re-check after creation, only at creation. This is exactly the kind of gap that pairs a preventive control (require the tag at creation) with a detective one (periodically check that tag values still reflect current reality) — the same preventive-plus-detective pairing pattern that shows up throughout this module, applied to a different problem.

Production scenario

A compliance audit needs a list of every resource that stores PII, org-wide, within an hour. A team with a rigorously enforced data-classification tag policy answers this with one scoped query. A team without one has to inspect resources individually — and inevitably misses some, because tagging that isn't enforced isn't reliable.

APPLY IT

Design this governance structure

Nine organizational requirements. For each, decide the OU placement and the guardrail type before revealing the reasoning.

Scenario 1

Requirement: A newly acquired business unit's accounts must be isolated from the rest of the org until a security review is complete.

Answer: A dedicated, temporary "Onboarding" or "Quarantine" OU with tight, mostly-preventive SCPs restricting cross-account access and outbound data movement. Move the accounts into their permanent OU (likely Workloads) only once the review clears.

Scenario 2

Requirement: No account, anywhere in the org, should ever be able to disable CloudTrail.

Answer: Preventive — an SCP denying the relevant CloudTrail-stopping actions, attached at the Organization root so every account, current and future, inherits it with zero exceptions.

Scenario 3

Requirement: Engineers need to freely experiment with new services without full production guardrails getting in the way.

Answer: Sandbox OU, with guardrails limited to true non-negotiables (no public data exposure, spend caps). Resist the urge to import Prod-level SCPs here — that defeats the purpose of the OU.

Scenario 4

Requirement: Guarantee that no S3 bucket in the entire org can ever be made publicly accessible, regardless of any individual account's own bucket policy.

Answer: An RCP at the Organization root. This is precisely the case an RCP is built for — a resource-control guarantee that holds no matter what any account's own policy grants.

Scenario 5

Requirement: Flag (but don't block) any EC2 instance launched without a required cost-center tag, since blocking would disrupt legitimate emergency work.

Answer: Detective — a Config rule checking for the required tag, paired with a tag policy for consistency. A window of non-compliance is acceptable here in exchange for not blocking urgent work.

Scenario 6

Requirement: An account is being decommissioned and must stop incurring cost or exposure immediately, before offboarding paperwork finishes.

Answer: Move the account into the Suspended OU immediately, where an SCP denies nearly all actions. Don't wait for offboarding process completion to restrict access.

Scenario 7

Requirement: Every new account created for any team should already have centralized logging and baseline guardrails in place before an engineer logs in for the first time.

Answer: Control Tower's Account Factory. This is the definition of "compliant from creation" rather than compliance retrofitted after the fact.

Scenario 8

Requirement: A production database account and a staging account for the same product team need meaningfully different guardrails despite sharing a parent team OU.

Answer: Split the team's Workloads sub-tree into Prod and Non-Prod sub-OUs so each inherits the shared team-level policy plus its own tier-specific SCPs — this is exactly why Workloads splits further down instead of staying flat.

Scenario 9

Requirement: The security team needs org-wide visibility into every account's compliance posture without being able to modify anything in those accounts.

Answer: The Audit account inside the Security OU — read-only cross-account roles into every other account, no write access. Pair with the Log Archive account for the underlying data.

WHERE THIS FAILS IN PRODUCTION

Production trap gallery

Six real patterns of governance going quietly wrong — not from malice, from structure.

Vignette 1 — wrong-level SCP

An SCP meant to restrict a single new sandbox team gets attached at the shared Workloads OU instead of a scoped sub-OU. Every Prod and Non-Prod account under Workloads inherits it, and several production deployments start failing for reasons that look nothing like the actual cause — an OU three levels away.

Vignette 2 — Control Tower assumption broke on account move

A team assumed an account's guardrails were tied to Control Tower's registration rather than its OU placement. When the account moved from Sandbox to Workloads for a promotion to production use, the guardrails it actually needed didn't automatically strengthen — because Control Tower manages the SCPs, but which SCPs apply is still a function of OU placement, not registration status.

Vignette 3 — tagging gap breaks compliance scoping

A cost-center tag was enforced by policy for six months, then a new resource-creation path (an infrastructure-as-code module) shipped without the tag-requiring SCP wired in. Six months later, an audit needing every PII-storing resource org-wide silently misses an entire category, because the automation trusted a tag that was never guaranteed present.

Vignette 4 — retrofitted compliance

Fifty existing accounts get the "compliant baseline" applied after the fact, account by account, over three months. Twelve of them have resource policies from before the retrofit that technically still violate the new baseline in edge cases the retrofit script didn't catch — because retrofitting inherently misses what account-factory-from-creation would have prevented entirely.

Vignette 5 — detective guardrail treated as sufficient for an unacceptable risk

A team relies on a Config rule to detect public S3 buckets rather than an RCP to prevent them, reasoning that "we'll get an alert." A bucket goes public for four hours over a weekend before anyone reviews the finding — a window that a preventive RCP would have made impossible from the start.

Vignette 6 — Suspended OU applied too late

An account marked for decommissioning stays in its original Workloads sub-OU for three weeks while offboarding paperwork is processed, still fully permissioned. It continues incurring cost and remains a live target the entire time — exactly the exposure the Suspended OU pattern exists to close immediately, not eventually.

QUESTIONS FROM THE FIELD

Discussion

🧑‍💻 You ask ›If a Config rule can catch the same violation an SCP would prevent, why does the exam care which one I use?
🛡️ Your MentorBecause "catching" and "preventing" have different consequences. A Config rule tells you a violation happened; an SCP guarantees it never happens. For low-stakes drift — a missing tag — that gap is fine. For something like a public S3 bucket holding customer data, the gap between "detected in four hours" and "never possible" is the whole ballgame, and the exam consistently tests whether you can tell which situation you're in.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Do RCPs and SCPs ever conflict with each other?
🛡️ Your MentorThey operate on different sides of the same interaction — SCPs restrict what the calling principal can do, RCPs restrict what a resource's own policy can grant to others. They don't override each other; both are evaluated, and either one denying is enough to deny the overall request.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Is it ever correct to attach an SCP directly to an account instead of an OU?
🛡️ Your MentorOccasionally, for a genuinely one-off requirement that applies to exactly one account and nothing else. But if you find yourself doing this repeatedly for a group of similar accounts, that's usually a sign you need a new sub-OU, not more per-account attachments.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Why does the Sandbox OU exist at all if it's loosely governed — isn't that a risk?
🛡️ Your MentorIt's a deliberate, bounded risk. Engineers need somewhere to experiment without production-grade friction, and isolating that need into its own OU with only the true non-negotiables enforced means the risk stays contained there instead of leaking into how you govern everything else.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›How is this different from what Module 9's centralized logging already covered?
🛡️ Your MentorModule 9 covered getting the logs to one place. This module covers the account structure that makes that pattern secure and sustainable at scale — the Security OU that houses the log-archive account, and the guardrails that keep that account from ever being loosely governed itself.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Does Account Factory eliminate the need to ever review an account's configuration manually?
🛡️ Your MentorNo — it eliminates the need to build the baseline manually every time. Accounts still drift after creation as teams request exceptions or new services get adopted, which is exactly why the detective guardrails and audits from Module 13 stay necessary even with a strong Account Factory baseline.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›If tag policies exist, do I still need an SCP requiring tags at creation?
🛡️ Your MentorYes, if you need the requirement to be structural rather than advisory. A tag policy alone can flag a non-compliant tag after the fact; it doesn't stop the resource from being created untagged in the first place. Pairing it with an SCP is what closes that gap.

WHAT'S NEXT

You've built the guardrails.

This module gave you the structure — OUs mapped to real security boundaries, SCPs and RCPs enforced at the right scope, preventive and detective guardrails chosen deliberately, tagging that makes automation trustworthy. That structure is the governance model, built once and inherited everywhere beneath it.

But a governance structure built today can still drift tomorrow — an SCP gets loosened for a one-off request and never reverted, a new account slips in through a path that bypasses Account Factory, a tag requirement quietly stops being enforced. Module 13 — Governance Frameworks, Auditing & Enforcement — is where you move from "how the governance structure is built" to "how you continuously verify it's actually being followed." You've built the guardrails; Module 13 is how you prove, on an ongoing basis, that they're holding.
