In Module 2, you mastered how IAM evaluates policies within a single account. That was the foundation. Now the scope expands: you are adding two more permission ceilings on top of everything you already know — organization-wide guardrails that govern entire fleets of accounts, per-identity caps that enable safe delegation, and a new resource-scoped defense that protects your data from principals that your organizational controls cannot even see.

This is the most conceptually dense module in the IAM sequence. The four mechanisms you will learn here — cross-account roles, Service Control Policies, Resource Control Policies, and permission boundaries — each answer a fundamentally different question about access control. The exam tests whether you truly understand the distinctions between them, not just whether you can recite their definitions. By the end, you will be able to predict the effective permission for any identity given any combination of these controls — and that predictive ability is exactly what the SCS-C03 scenario questions demand.

Core Mechanism 1

Cross-Account Roles

The safest, most operationally sound way to grant access across AWS account boundaries is through a role assumption — and understanding why requires unpacking two separate documents that serve two completely different purposes.

Trust policy vs. permission policy: two documents, two jobs

Every IAM role has exactly two types of attached policy, and the exam will test whether you can keep them straight. The trust policy answers the question: who is allowed to assume this role? It is a resource-based policy attached to the role itself, and its Effect is almost always Allow with the action sts:AssumeRole. The permission policy — which is just an ordinary identity-based policy — answers the question: what can the role do once it is assumed? These are completely independent documents. A trust policy that opens the door to Account A's identities does not automatically let them do anything useful; they step into an empty room until you attach permission policies that give the role real capabilities.

Here is the failure mode that shows up on the exam: an engineer creates a role in Account B, writes a trust policy allowing arn:aws:iam::111111111111:root (Account A), and calls it done. The assumption request from Account A still fails — not because the trust policy is wrong, but because the assuming principal in Account A does not have sts:AssumeRole permission in their own account's identity-based policy. Cross-account trust is bilateral: Account B says "I trust Account A," and Account A must say "this identity is allowed to assume that role." Both checks must pass independently.

The STS:AssumeRole flow, step by step

  • 1
    Request

    The principal in Account A calls sts:AssumeRole targeting the role ARN in Account B. This requires that the principal's own identity-based policy (or an attached permission boundary, if one exists) allows sts:AssumeRole on that specific role ARN.

  • 2
    Trust policy evaluation in Account B

    STS checks the role's trust policy. The Principal element must list the assuming entity — either the root of Account A (arn:aws:iam::111111111111:root, which allows any identity in A that has permission) or a specific user/role ARN. If the trust policy does not match, the assumption fails here regardless of what Account A says.

  • 3
    Temporary credential issuance

    If both checks pass, STS returns temporary security credentials: an access key ID, a secret access key, and a session token. These credentials are time-limited — the default session is one hour, maximum 12 hours for most roles — and they carry the role's own permission policies into the session.

  • 4
    Credential expiry and rotation

    The session token has a hard expiry. When it expires, the application must re-assume the role to get fresh credentials. This is one of the strongest security properties of the cross-account role pattern: there are no long-lived credentials to rotate, audit, or accidentally commit to a repository.

The single most common cross-account mistake I see in production is assuming the trust policy is all you need. People write a trust policy in Account B allowing Account A's root, then spend 20 minutes debugging why the CLI call is denied. Nine times out of ten, the developer in Account A does not have sts:AssumeRole in their own identity-based policy. The trust policy says "I trust you if you come knocking." The identity-based policy in Account A is the key that lets them knock in the first place. You need both.

Production scenario: data lake access pattern

Your organization has a dedicated data account (Account B) that hosts the production S3 data lake. Analysts work in a separate analytics account (Account A). The pattern: create a role DataLakeReadRole in Account B with permission policies allowing s3:GetObject on the data lake bucket. Set the trust policy to allow specific analyst roles in Account A. In Account A, attach an inline policy to the analyst roles permitting sts:AssumeRole on the exact DataLakeReadRole ARN. Analysts call aws sts assume-role, get temporary credentials scoped to the data they need, and never hold any persistent credentials in Account B. Audit trails in Account B CloudTrail show the assumed-role session principal, giving you full attribution.

Core Mechanism 2

Service Control Policies

Service Control Policies are the most misunderstood control in AWS Organizations. The misunderstanding is almost always the same: people treat them as a way to grant permissions. They are not. An SCP is a guardrail — a ceiling — that determines the maximum permissions an identity in a member account can ever exercise, regardless of what that account's own IAM policies say.

The mental model that everything else builds on

Imagine a room with a ceiling. The ceiling is the SCP. The identity-based policy inside that account is the person standing in the room, reaching upward. Even if the IAM policy says "Effect":"Allow","Action":"*","Resource":"*" — the broadest possible grant — the identity can only touch what the ceiling allows. If the SCP ceiling is at shoulder height, it does not matter that the IAM policy allows reaching the sky.

This analogy has one more critical layer: the SCP ceiling applies to every identity in the account simultaneously, except the management account itself (which is never governed by SCPs — one of the key architectural reasons to keep the management account's blast radius as small as possible). Even the account root user is subject to SCPs attached to the account's OU or to the root of the organization. This is one of the very few controls in AWS that can restrict the root user of a member account.

⚠️ The SCP cardinal rule — commit this to memory

An SCP with "Effect":"Allow" does not grant permissions. It merely does not deny them. An identity in an account needs both an SCP that does not deny the action AND its own identity-based policy that explicitly allows it. An SCP Allow with no matching identity-based Allow equals no access.

FullAWSAccess and what changes when you modify it

Every new account in an organization inherits a default SCP called FullAWSAccess. This SCP contains a single statement: Allow * on *. It does not grant anything — it simply means the ceiling is at the absolute maximum, and IAM policies within the account are the only governing layer. The moment you attach a custom SCP to an OU or account, AWS evaluates both the custom SCP and FullAWSAccess using the standard policy evaluation logic. If your custom SCP denies an action, that deny wins even if FullAWSAccess allows everything. Many organizations remove FullAWSAccess entirely when they start enforcing SCPs, replacing it with a more restrictive allow-list of approved actions.

How SCPs interact with identity-based policies — the intersection model

The effective permissions for any identity in a member account are the intersection of what the SCP permits AND what the identity-based policy allows. Think of it as two overlapping circles: the SCP circle defines the available space; the IAM policy circle defines what the identity requests. Only the overlapping region results in access. If an SCP has an explicit Deny for an action, that action is denied for every identity in the account through every mechanism — there is no override, no escalation path, no way to carve out an exception within the member account. Only the management account or a delegated administrator with org-level access can modify the SCP.

SCP: Region restriction — deny all actions outside approved regions
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "DenyNonApprovedRegions",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "NotAction": [
        "iam:*", "sts:*", "cloudfront:*",
        "route53:*", "support:*"
      ],
      "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": {
        "StringNotEquals": {
          "aws:RequestedRegion": ["us-east-1", "eu-west-1"]
        }
      }
    }
  ]
}

Note the use of NotAction here: global services like IAM, STS, CloudFront, and Route 53 are region-independent and would be broken if a region condition blocked them. This pattern — Deny + NotAction + region condition — is the correct way to implement region restriction in an SCP, and it appears regularly on the exam.

Common SCP patterns worth knowing for the exam

Governance

Prevent account from leaving the Organization

Deny organizations:LeaveOrganization across all member accounts. Without this, a compromised account root could remove the account from the org, immediately escaping all SCP guardrails.

Region control

Region restriction

Deny all service actions outside approved regions using NotAction + StringNotEquals on aws:RequestedRegion. Carve out global services with NotAction to avoid breaking IAM and STS.

MFA enforcement

Require MFA for sensitive actions

Deny high-risk actions like iam:DeleteUser or ec2:TerminateInstances unless aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent is true. Useful for break-glass scenarios without full lockout.

Blast radius

Protect the logging infrastructure

Deny cloudtrail:StopLogging, cloudtrail:DeleteTrail, and config:DeleteConfigurationRecorder in all accounts. An attacker who gains access cannot cover their tracks by disabling audit logging.

The most dangerous SCP mistake I have seen in production is a team that attached an SCP intending to restrict access, but wrote it as Allow-only statements thinking that equals a grant. Their member accounts looked like they had permissions, the IAM Console showed allows, yet every API call was denied because the SCP the team thought was granting access was actually just a ceiling — and the identities had no identity-based policy underneath it. An SCP Allow is invisible in the happy path: it just means the SCP is not blocking you. The IAM policy still has to do the actual allowing.

Production scenario: protecting the logging baseline

A security team attaches the following SCP to the root of their organization: deny cloudtrail:StopLogging, cloudtrail:DeleteTrail, config:DeleteConfigurationRecorder, and config:StopConfigurationRecorder for all principals. Even if an attacker compromises the root credentials of a member account, they cannot disable the audit trail. Even if a legitimate administrator with full IAM Admin permissions in that account tries, the SCP blocks them. The only entity that can modify this guardrail is someone with access to the management account — which should be protected by hardware MFA, break-glass processes, and monitored continuously.

Core Mechanism 3 · New in SCS-C03

Resource Control Policies

Resource Control Policies are new in the SCS-C03 exam, and they exist precisely because SCPs have a gap that cannot be patched by any combination of existing controls. Understanding that gap — and why RCPs close it — is the most likely new-content question on this version of the exam.

The gap SCPs cannot close

SCPs govern identities that belong to your organization. When an IAM user in your org tries to do something, the SCP is in the evaluation chain. But consider this scenario: your S3 bucket has a bucket policy that says "Principal":"*" — anyone can read objects from it. An external AWS account outside your organization accesses that bucket. The external account's identities are not governed by your SCPs. Your SCPs say nothing about external principals. The bucket policy has already opened the door.

Before RCPs, your options for preventing this were all bucket-level: keep the bucket policy locked down, enable S3 Block Public Access, use bucket policy conditions on aws:PrincipalOrgID. These work if applied correctly — but they require every bucket owner in every account across your entire organization to apply them consistently. One misconfigured bucket in one account breaks the security posture. You cannot enforce an org-wide guarantee about what happens to a resource at the organization level. That is the gap.

The RCP insight: resource-scoped, not identity-scoped

An SCP answers: "What can identities inside my organization do?" An RCP answers: "What can happen to this resource, regardless of who is trying to act on it — inside the organization, outside the organization, or even anonymous?" RCPs attach at the organization, OU, or account level in AWS Organizations and apply to every resource of a supported type within that scope, independently of the resource's own policy.

Why this distinction matters for the exam

The exam will present scenarios where an external principal — an identity from a different AWS account that is not part of your organization — is accessing (or attempting to access) a resource. SCPs do not apply to that external principal at all. If you try to use an SCP to block cross-account access from outside your org, you are applying the wrong tool. RCPs apply to the resource itself, so they govern all access — internal, external, cross-account, even service principals — regardless of origin.

Think of it this way: an SCP is a control on the person walking through the door. An RCP is a lock on the door itself. If someone climbs through the window (an external principal), the person-control does nothing. The door lock — the RCP — still applies regardless of entry point.

RCP: Enforce organization-boundary on all S3 buckets
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "EnforceOrgBoundary",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "Principal": "*",
      "Action": "s3:*",
      "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": {
        "StringNotEquals": {
          "aws:PrincipalOrgID": "o-exampleorgid"
        },
        "BoolIfExists": {
          "aws:PrincipalIsAWSService": "false"
        }
      }
    }
  ]
}

This RCP, attached at the organization root, means no S3 bucket anywhere in the organization can ever be accessed by a principal outside the organization — regardless of what any bucket policy says. The BoolIfExists condition on aws:PrincipalIsAWSService carves out AWS service principals (like CloudFront or Lambda in the same account) that do not carry org context. This is the canonical org-boundary pattern that will appear on the exam.

Supported resource types and practical scope

As of the SCS-C03 exam scope, RCPs support a targeted set of AWS resource types where cross-account exposure risk is highest: Amazon S3, AWS STS, AWS KMS, AWS Secrets Manager, and Amazon SQS. This list is worth knowing not just for completeness, but for what it reveals about design intent — these are the services most commonly misconfigured to allow broader access than intended, and the services where a per-resource misconfiguration in one account could expose sensitive data or credentials.

⚠️ Exam trap: Pre-2025 study resources will not cover RCPs

RCPs were introduced after many popular SCS-C02 study guides and video courses were published. If you are using older material, RCPs will be missing entirely. Flag any resource in your study plan and check whether it explicitly covers RCPs — if it does not, supplement with the AWS Organizations RCP documentation directly.

All three mechanisms share one property: they are ceilings, not grants. None of them can give an identity access it does not already have through an identity-based Allow. They can only restrict, never expand. This is the most important conceptual thread running through all three.

Core Mechanism 4

Permission Boundaries

A permission boundary is a managed policy that you attach to a specific IAM user or role, setting an absolute ceiling on what that identity's own identity-based policies can ever grant it. Unlike an SCP — which applies org-wide to all identities in an account — a permission boundary is per-identity and is the tool that makes safe delegated administration possible.

What a permission boundary actually does

Consider an IAM role for a Lambda function. That role has an identity-based policy saying Allow: iam:* (perhaps because the function needs to create execution roles). Normally, this would mean the function could create roles with any permissions — including AdministratorAccess. But if you attach a permission boundary to the role that allows only s3:* and lambda:*, then even though the identity-based policy says iam:*, the effective permissions are the intersection: nothing from iam:* makes it through, because the boundary does not include IAM actions. The function literally cannot create a role with IAM permissions, because the boundary ceiling is below IAM.

The critical nuance: a permission boundary with no matching identity-based Allow still results in no access. A boundary is a ceiling, and just like an SCP, it does not grant. You could set a permission boundary of Allow: * on a role, and the role still has no permissions if there are no attached policies. The boundary defines the outer limit of what policies can grant, not what is actually granted.

The delegated administration use case — walked through concretely

This is the canonical real-world use case for permission boundaries, and it is the scenario the exam almost certainly tests. Your platform team needs to let application teams self-service create IAM roles for their own Lambda functions. Without permission boundaries, giving a team permission to run iam:CreateRole means they can create a role with AdministratorAccess — effectively granting themselves full admin through a side door. This is privilege escalation, and it is a real attack vector.

The solution: allow the application team to create roles, but require that every role they create has a specific managed policy set as its permission boundary. The platform team defines a managed policy — call it AppTeamBoundary — that allows only the service actions their applications legitimately need: s3:GetObject, logs:PutLogEvents, xray:PutTraceSegments, and so on. The application team can run iam:CreateRole and iam:AttachRolePolicy only when they also set the AppTeamBoundary as the permission boundary of the new role, enforced by a condition in their own policy:

IAM Policy: Allow role creation only with required boundary
{
  "Effect": "Allow",
  "Action": [
    "iam:CreateRole",
    "iam:PutRolePolicy",
    "iam:AttachRolePolicy"
  ],
  "Resource": "*",
  "Condition": {
    "StringEquals": {
      "iam:PermissionsBoundary":
        "arn:aws:iam::123456789012:policy/AppTeamBoundary"
    }
  }
}

This pattern is powerful: the application team has genuine self-service IAM capabilities, but they cannot create any role that exceeds the boundary the platform team defined. The boundary is the constraint that keeps delegation safe without requiring the platform team to approve every individual role creation manually.

The permission boundary confusion I see most often is teams who think the boundary automatically limits what the identity can do. It does not — it limits what policies can grant. An identity with a very restrictive boundary but no attached identity-based policies still has zero permissions. And an identity with broad attached policies but an equally broad boundary still has exactly what those policies grant. The boundary only matters at the intersection point where policy grants something the boundary does not permit. Think of it as a safety valve, not a permission statement.

The three-ceiling evaluation model

This is the model you must be able to apply to any scenario question. An identity's effective permissions are the intersection of all applicable ceilings simultaneously:

The key rule: for any given action to be allowed, it must pass through all three layers simultaneously. If any layer denies it — or if any layer simply does not permit it — the action is denied. An explicit Deny at any layer overrides any Allow at any other layer. The SCP ceiling and the boundary ceiling are independent from each other; neither knows about the other. Both apply in parallel, and the identity-based policy must be consistent with both.

Quick evaluation rule for scenario questions

  • Does an explicit Deny exist anywhere (SCP, resource policy, any policy)? → DENY, stop here
  • Does the SCP permit the action? → If no, DENY
  • Does the permission boundary permit the action? → If no, DENY
  • Does the identity-based policy explicitly Allow the action? → If no, DENY (default deny)
  • All three pass? → ALLOW
Core Mechanism 5

Session Policies

Session policies are the fourth ceiling — passed inline at the moment of role assumption to further restrict, never expand, what the assumed role can do during that specific session. They are the mechanism that lets a single broad role serve many narrowly-scoped consumers without creating a separate role for each use case.

When a principal calls sts:AssumeRole, they can pass an inline session policy as a JSON document alongside the request. This policy adds an additional constraint to the resulting temporary credentials: the session can only perform actions that the role's permission policies allow AND that the session policy allows. The session policy cannot expand beyond what the role already has — it can only restrict further.

Use Case

Federation broker scoping sessions

A central identity broker issues temporary credentials to different teams from a single broad DataAnalyticsRole. For Team A, the session policy restricts access to the team-a-* S3 prefix. For Team B, a different session policy restricts to team-b-*. One role, multiple scope-limited sessions — no role proliferation.

Exam Trap

Session policies cannot expand permissions

If the role has permission to s3:GetObject only, and you pass a session policy allowing s3:*, the effective permission is still only s3:GetObject. The session policy Allow on s3:PutObject has nothing to expand — the role itself does not allow it.

The evaluation logic for session policies follows the same intersection model: effective session permissions = intersection of (role permission policies) AND (session policy), subject to any applicable SCP and permission boundary. A session policy is a fourth ceiling stacked below the role, the boundary, and the SCP. For exam purposes, the important rule is that session policies narrow — they never grant anything not already available through the role.

Field Experience

Production Trap Gallery

These are the real-world failure modes that cost teams hours of debugging — and the exact scenarios the exam uses to distinguish candidates who truly understand these mechanisms from those who have only memorized definitions.

Trap 01 · Cross-Account Trust

The one-sided trust policy

Team creates a role in Account B with a trust policy allowing Account A's root. They test from the CLI and get AccessDenied. The error message says "not authorized to perform sts:AssumeRole" — which they interpret as a trust policy problem. They spend 30 minutes rechecking the trust policy, which is perfectly correct.

Root cause: The IAM user in Account A does not have sts:AssumeRole in their own identity-based policy. The trust policy is necessary but not sufficient — the assuming principal needs permission on their own side too. Both sides of the trust relationship require explicit authorization.

Trap 02 · SCP Collateral Damage

The SCP that silently broke CI/CD

Security team attaches a well-intentioned SCP denying all IAM actions to workload accounts, intending to prevent privilege escalation. Two days later, the CI/CD pipeline that creates Lambda execution roles starts failing. The pipeline's error logs just show IAM permission denied — with no mention of SCPs.

Root cause: SCPs do not appear in CloudTrail as the denying entity — the denial looks like a standard IAM permission error. Always test SCPs in a sandbox OU first. Use IAM Access Analyzer and the policy simulator to model SCP impact before attaching to production OUs.

Trap 03 · Boundary Forgotten

The permission boundary that was never enforced

Platform team defines a delegated admin pattern: app teams can create IAM roles, but only with the AppTeamBoundary attached. The enforcement condition is in the platform team's policy. Six months later, a team member with direct IAM Admin rights (not going through the delegated pattern) creates a role without the boundary. The new role has AdministratorAccess.

Root cause: The enforcement condition only applies when creating roles through the delegated mechanism. Direct IAM Admin rights bypass the entire pattern. Permission boundaries must be enforced through SCPs (deny CreateRole unless boundary is set) to be universally applied — not just through the delegated policy.

Trap 04 · The RCP Gap

The S3 bucket SCP could not protect

Pre-SCS-C03 organization has SCPs blocking all external S3 access. A developer in the organization creates an S3 bucket, adds a bucket policy with Principal:"*" for a third-party vendor integration. The vendor accesses the bucket freely. The SCP did not fire.

Root cause: SCPs govern what identities inside the organization can do. The third-party vendor is an external principal — outside the organization — and SCPs do not apply to them at all. The bucket policy opened the door, and there was no RCP to close it regardless of who was knocking. This exact gap is why RCPs were introduced.

Trap 05 · SCP as Grant

The team that expected an SCP to give them access

A newly onboarded cloud team is told their OU has an SCP allowing s3:* actions. They create IAM users, assume a role, and cannot access any S3 buckets. They escalate to the cloud team: "The SCP allows S3 — why are we denied?" The SCP was correct; they were not.

Root cause: SCPs do not grant permissions. An SCP allowing s3:* simply means S3 is not blocked at the org level. The identities still need their own identity-based policies that explicitly Allow the S3 actions. The SCP is a ceiling; the IAM policies are what actually reach up toward it.

Trap 06 · Session Policy Expansion

The session policy that could not expand the role

A developer assumes a role that has permission to s3:GetObject on a specific bucket. They pass a session policy including s3:PutObject and s3:DeleteObject, expecting to unlock those capabilities for their session. Their writes are still denied.

Root cause: Session policies can only narrow — never expand — what a role already has. The effective session permissions are the intersection of the role's permissions AND the session policy. Since the role did not have s3:PutObject, there is nothing for the session policy to unlock. Session policies add a fourth ceiling; they cannot raise the role's existing ceiling.

What You Have Built

You now have the full mental model for multi-account access control in AWS — the most tested IAM domain on the SCS-C03 exam. You can reason from first principles about any combination of these mechanisms, and you can predict effective permissions without guessing.

You understand that cross-account access requires bilateral authorization: trust in the destination account, and permission to assume in the source account. You know that SCPs are ceilings, not grants, and that an SCP Allow with no matching IAM policy is silent — it does nothing. You understand why RCPs exist even though SCPs already did: SCPs govern identities inside your organization; RCPs govern what happens to a resource regardless of who is asking. And you can apply permission boundaries to enable safe delegation without opening privilege escalation paths.

The next module — Module 04: Federation, Identity Center & Temporary Credentials — takes everything you just learned about cross-account roles and temporary credentials and extends it to real human identity. How do your engineers, analysts, and contractors actually get federated access to AWS without ever holding a long-lived credential? How does AWS IAM Identity Center orchestrate that at scale across a multi-account organization? Module 4 is the human side of everything mechanical you just mastered here.

Cross-cutting threads you advanced in this module

  • Least privilege — reinforced at scale: SCPs, RCPs, and permission boundaries all express least privilege at different layers and scopes
  • Multi-account architecture — first introduced here: the OU/root/account hierarchy, where controls attach, and how they cascade
  • Blast radius minimization — first introduced here: SCPs on the management account, permission boundaries to contain delegation, RCPs to prevent data exfiltration regardless of misconfigured policies
