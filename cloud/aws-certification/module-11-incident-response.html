Incident Response: Planning, Containment & Automation

Module 10 gave you GuardDuty, Security Hub, and Detective — a pipeline that generates and correlates findings. But a finding sitting in a dashboard has done nothing yet. It has not isolated anything, preserved anything, or stopped anything from getting worse.

This module is what happens in the minutes and hours after that finding appears — under real pressure, with real consequences for getting the sequence wrong. Detection told you something is happening. This module is about what you do next, and just as importantly, what you must not do first.

Every incident response scenario on the exam is really testing one thing: do you reach for the fast, satisfying action, or the correct one? They are not always the same action, and the exam knows exactly where that gap is.

THE LIFECYCLE

The incident response lifecycle, in full depth

A fire department doesn't start deciding where the trucks are parked while the building is already burning. That decision, along with the hose lengths, the water pressure, and who's authorized to break down a door, was made long before any alarm sounded. Incident response works the same way — and the six phases you've probably seen before, Prepare, Detect, Contain, Eradicate, Recover, Lessons Learned, are really just naming when each of those decisions has to already be made. Naming them is not the skill the exam tests. What the exam actually tests is whether you understand what each phase concretely requires in an AWS environment — and Prepare is the phase almost everyone skips, which is exactly why it gets tested hardest.

Prepare — the phase that decides everything downstream.

Concretely, in AWS, preparation means: forensics tooling pre-provisioned in a dedicated account before it is needed, IAM roles for responders already created with exactly the permissions an investigation requires — no more — and runbooks already written and pre-approved for the incident types you expect. If any of these get built during the incident, you are not responding, you are improvising.

Detect — a handoff, not a re-teach.

This is Module 10's territory: GuardDuty findings, Security Hub aggregation, Detective's correlated timelines. This module picks up the moment a finding exists. The connection matters for the exam — a scenario question that opens with "GuardDuty generates a finding" is telling you the Detect phase is over and it wants to know what you do in Contain.

Contain — stop the bleeding without destroying the evidence.

Isolate the affected resource so it cannot do further damage or communicate further, while preserving its state for later analysis. This is where isolate-before-terminate lives, and it is the single most tested sequencing decision in this domain — its own scene is coming up.

Eradicate — remove the root cause, not just the symptom.

Rotate the compromised credential, patch the exploited vulnerability, remove the malicious IAM policy or backdoor user. Eradication only happens after evidence is preserved — doing it earlier destroys your ability to confirm you actually found the root cause.

Recover — restore service deliberately, not by reflex.

Rebuild from known-good AMIs or infrastructure-as-code rather than trusting a "cleaned" compromised resource, restore from backups taken before the compromise window, and monitor closely for re-compromise before declaring the incident over.

Lessons Learned — feeds back into Prepare.

A blameless post-incident review that updates runbooks, closes the gap that allowed the incident, and — critically — feeds directly back into Prepare for the next cycle. This phase is why the lifecycle is drawn as a loop, not a line.

Production scenario

A mid-sized fintech company had excellent detection — GuardDuty findings routed cleanly into Security Hub, with Slack alerts within seconds. But when an IAM access key was actually leaked in a public GitHub commit, the responding engineer had no pre-written runbook for "leaked access key" and no pre-approved role to act with. They spent forty minutes in a Slack thread debating who had permission to disable the key, while the leaked credential remained active the entire time. Detection was never the gap. Preparation was.

I've never seen a team get burned by too much preparation. I've seen plenty get burned by treating it as the phase they'll "get to later" — later is the middle of the incident, and by then it's not preparation anymore, it's improvisation with a deadline.

Exam angle

When a scenario question describes a slow or chaotic response, look for what was missing in Prepare — a pre-written runbook, a pre-provisioned role, pre-approved automation — before assuming the fix is "better detection." The exam frequently plants the gap in Prepare and dresses it up as a Detect or Contain problem.

Takeaway: the lifecycle is a loop, not a line — and the phase everyone under-invests in is the one at both ends of it.

🧠 Quick check — before you scroll on

A GuardDuty finding fires, gets triaged, and a response is executed — but no one updates the runbook or the automation afterward. Which phase got skipped, and what does that put at risk?

Lessons Learned was skipped. Without it, the gap that allowed this incident never feeds back into Prepare — so the same class of incident can recur with the exact same slow, improvised response next time. The loop only closes if this phase actually happens.

IR RUNBOOKS

A runbook is not a checklist

A checklist says "investigate the instance." A runbook says exactly which security group to attach, exactly which snapshot to take first, and exactly who is authorized to execute step four without waiting for anyone's approval. The difference is the entire point: a genuinely useful runbook contains specific, pre-approved steps for a specific incident type — compromised credential, compromised EC2 instance, publicly exposed S3 bucket — written so an on-call responder can execute it under pressure without inventing novel judgment calls in the moment.

Pre-approval is the part people underestimate. A runbook's containment steps should already be authorized by security leadership and legal before an incident happens. If a responder has to get emergency sign-off mid-incident to isolate a resource, that delay is pure cost — cost measured in the attacker's continued access, not in caution.

✗ Checklist

"Assess the situation." "Contain if necessary." "Notify stakeholders." Every word requires a judgment call, made for the first time, under pressure, by whoever happens to be on call.

✓ Runbook

"Attach security group sg-quarantine-0091 (no inbound, outbound to forensics VPC only). On-call engineer is pre-authorized to execute without further approval." Zero judgment calls left to make.

Production scenario

An engineering team had a well-intentioned but vague "security incident checklist": "assess the situation," "contain if necessary," "notify stakeholders." When a compromised EC2 instance was flagged, the on-call engineer spent the first critical minutes deciding what "contain if necessary" actually meant for this instance, second-guessing whether they had authority to quarantine a production resource. A specific runbook — "attach security group sg-quarantine-0091, which permits only outbound to the forensics VPC" — would have taken thirty seconds to execute with zero ambiguity.

If you cannot hand a runbook to an on-call engineer at 3 a.m. and expect them to execute it correctly without calling you, it is not a runbook yet. It is a description of a runbook.

Exam angle

Watch for answer choices that describe a runbook as a general checklist or guideline. The exam consistently rewards answers where the runbook specifies exact, pre-approved actions tied to a specific incident type.

Takeaway: a runbook only counts as a runbook once every decision in it has already been made and pre-approved — nothing left to improvise mid-incident.

AWS-NATIVE TOOLING

Systems Manager Incident Manager and OpsCenter

A hospital's crash cart protocol and the room the code team actually works in are two different things — one is the pre-agreed plan for who does what, the other is the physical space with the patient's chart, monitors, and equipment already at hand. AWS splits the same two concerns. Runbooks need a place to live and a system to execute them consistently: that is what Systems Manager Incident Manager provides — response plans that tie a specific incident type to a specific automation document, plus engagement and escalation configuration so the right on-call responder is paged automatically instead of relying on someone remembering to check Slack.

OpsCenter is the workspace next to it — the room the responder actually works in. It centralizes operational issues, including security-relevant ones, and attaches the related resources, the relevant runbook, and contextual CloudWatch and Config data directly to the issue. Instead of a responder tab-switching between six consoles trying to reconstruct context, OpsCenter puts that context in one place, next to the action they need to take.

Production scenario

A platform team configured an Incident Manager response plan for "public S3 bucket" findings: the plan automatically opens an OpsItem in OpsCenter, attaches the bucket's Config history and recent CloudTrail events, runs a pre-approved automation document that re-applies Block Public Access, and pages the data-security on-call rotation — all before a human has read the first Slack message. The human's job becomes verification and follow-up, not first response.

When you get confused between these two, ask which noun the question is describing — a plan, or a workspace. Incident Manager is always the plan. OpsCenter is always the room.

Exam angle

Distinguish the two services by role: Incident Manager structures the response workflow (plans, automation, escalation); OpsCenter is the operational workspace where a responder actually works the issue with context attached. A question describing "centralizing an operational issue with related resources" is pointing at OpsCenter, not Incident Manager.

Takeaway: Incident Manager decides the workflow in advance; OpsCenter is where a human executes it with context already attached.

ISOLATE FIRST.

TERMINATE DESTROYS THE EVIDENCE.

The module's single most tested concept
THE CENTRAL CONCEPT

Isolate before you terminate

When you find a compromised EC2 instance, terminating it feels decisive. It feels like you have solved the problem. It has not solved the problem — it has destroyed the evidence you needed to understand what actually happened, confirm the attacker's full reach, prevent recurrence, and in many industries, meet legal or compliance obligations around incident documentation.

The correct sequence is: isolate, preserve evidence, then eradicate. Isolation means attaching a locked-down quarantine security group — no inbound, no outbound except to your forensics tooling — so the instance can no longer communicate with the attacker or spread further, while its memory and disk remain intact. Preservation means taking an EBS snapshot, and capturing memory if your tooling supports it, before anything else touches the instance. Only after both of those steps is it safe to eradicate.

🔒1. Isolate
→
💾2. Preserve Evidence
→
🧹3. Eradicate
💥WRONG: Terminate first
Swap the security group, do not touch the data.

Attach a quarantine security group with no inbound rules and outbound restricted to your forensics tooling only. The instance stays running — its memory, running processes, and disk state remain exactly as they were at detection time.

Snapshot before you do anything else.

Take an EBS snapshot of every attached volume, and capture memory if your forensics tooling supports live memory acquisition. This snapshot is your permanent record of the instance's state at the moment of compromise, independent of anything that happens to the instance afterward.

Now it is safe to remove the root cause.

With isolation and evidence preservation complete, you can terminate the instance, rotate credentials, patch the vulnerability — whatever eradication requires — without losing anything you might need later.

Why terminate-first fails.

Terminating an EC2 instance destroys its instance store data immediately and makes memory forensics impossible. You lose the ability to determine how the attacker got in, what they accessed, and whether they left a persistence mechanism elsewhere in the account. The incident "looks" resolved. It is not understood.

Production scenario

An on-call engineer discovered cryptomining processes on a production EC2 instance and, wanting to stop the resource drain immediately, terminated it within two minutes of discovery. The mining stopped. But the team could never determine the initial access vector, could not confirm whether the attacker had pivoted to other instances via the same exploited web application, and could not produce the evidence their compliance team needed for a customer-facing incident report. The instance was gone in under two minutes. The uncertainty about the attacker's full reach lasted for months.

The urge to terminate is emotional, not technical. It feels like closure. But closure without understanding is just a compromised account waiting to be compromised the same way again. Isolate, snapshot, then act — the ninety extra seconds that takes will never cost you what a lost forensic trail costs.

Exam angle

Any scenario question offering "terminate the instance immediately" as the first step of a response, for a compromised-resource situation, is very likely the trap answer. Look for the option that isolates and preserves evidence before any destructive action.

Takeaway: isolate to stop the harm, preserve to keep the evidence, eradicate only once both are done — in that order, every time.

🧠 Quick check — before you scroll on

Your team's runbook says to snapshot a compromised instance's volumes before quarantining its security group. What's wrong with that order?

The instance is still fully networked while the snapshot is being taken, giving the attacker more time to do damage or notice the response and react. Isolate first — the quarantine security group can be attached in seconds — then snapshot. The order matters even between two "safe" steps.

AUTOMATED CONTAINMENT

Letting the response move at machine speed

An airbag doesn't wait for a committee to review the crash before deploying — the decision to deploy was engineered in long before any collision, so the response happens in milliseconds instead of minutes. Automated containment works the same way for well-understood, pre-approved incidents: automatically swapping a compromised EC2 instance's security group to quarantine, automatically revoking a compromised IAM credential's active sessions, automatically disabling an access key the moment it is flagged. Human responders are the bottleneck in the first minutes of an incident, and the first minutes are when an attacker does the most damage — automation closes exactly that gap.

The connective tissue between Module 10's detection and this module's response is EventBridge. A GuardDuty finding publishes an event, an EventBridge rule matches it, and that rule invokes a Lambda function that executes a specific, pre-approved containment action. No human decision is made in the loop — the decision was already made when the runbook and the automation were approved.

🛰️GuardDuty Finding
→
🔀EventBridge Rule
→
λLambda Function
→
🔒Containment Action
The trigger.

A GuardDuty finding such as UnauthorizedAccess:EC2/SSHBruteForce or CredentialAccess:IAMUser/AnomalousBehavior fires and is published as an event, either directly or via Security Hub's aggregated findings.

The match.

An EventBridge rule with an event pattern matching the specific finding type routes it to a target — in this case, a Lambda function scoped to handle exactly this class of incident.

The execution.

The Lambda function, running under an IAM role scoped to only the containment actions it needs — nothing broader — executes the pre-approved response: swap a security group, revoke a session, disable a key.

The outcome.

The resource is contained within seconds of the finding, not minutes or hours, and the action taken is logged for the post-incident review that follows in the Lessons Learned phase.

Production scenario

A company configured EventBridge to route any GuardDuty UnauthorizedAccess:IAMUser/InstanceCredentialExfiltration finding directly to a Lambda function that immediately attaches a deny-all IAM policy to the affected role and revokes all active sessions. When an EC2 instance's credentials were exfiltrated and used from an unfamiliar geography, the automation contained the credential in under thirty seconds — long before any human had opened the alert.

The scariest automation isn't the one that acts too fast — it's the one nobody scoped narrowly enough. Match on the most specific identifier you have, not the broadest tag that happens to be convenient.

Exam angle

Automated containment executes pre-approved actions, not novel decisions made in the moment. If an answer choice implies the automation is deciding what to do on the fly, that is a trap — the judgment call happened earlier, when the runbook and automation document were written and approved.

Takeaway: automation should execute a decision already made, at machine speed — never make a new decision on the fly.

MULTI-ACCOUNT ARCHITECTURE

Why forensics happens in a dedicated account

A crime scene investigator doesn't run the fingerprint analysis in the same room where the break-in happened, using the suspect's own equipment — they bag the evidence and take it somewhere the suspect never had access to. Cloud forensics needs the same separation. If the account that was compromised is also the account where you perform forensic analysis, you are trusting the integrity of an environment you already know was breached. An attacker with sufficient access could have altered logs, tampered with snapshots, or left IAM changes that interfere with — or actively destroy — the very evidence you are trying to analyze.

The fix is a direct application of both blast-radius minimization and the multi-account architecture thread running through this course: copy the evidence — EBS snapshots, memory captures, relevant logs — into a dedicated, isolated forensics account, where the potentially-compromised account's own permissions have no reach. Analysis happens there, on a copy, with clean IAM that was never exposed to the incident.

⚠️ Compromised Account

  • Affected EC2 instance
  • Original EBS volumes
  • Potentially-tampered IAM
➜snapshot copy

✅ Forensics Account

  • Copied EBS snapshots
  • Memory captures
  • Clean, unexposed IAM roles
Production scenario

A security team initially performed all forensic analysis inside the compromised account itself, using the account's existing IAM roles. During a later, more sophisticated incident, they discovered the attacker had escalated privileges broadly enough to potentially have altered CloudTrail log delivery. They could no longer fully trust their own analysis. After that incident, the team moved to a dedicated forensics account within their Organizations structure, with cross-account roles that only allow read access to copied evidence — never write access back into the source account.

Ask yourself: if the attacker had root in this account, could they have touched the thing I'm about to trust? If the answer is yes, that thing needs to live somewhere else before you trust it.

Exam angle

Any answer choice describing forensic analysis performed directly in the compromised account, using that account's own tooling or IAM, is a trap. The correct pattern always copies evidence into a separate, dedicated forensics account first.

Takeaway: never trust an investigation performed with the tools of the environment under investigation — move the evidence, not the analyst's assumptions.

DDOS RESPONSE

Shield Advanced from the response side

A home alarm system that just makes noise is prevention. A monitored alarm system with a response team already dispatched the moment it triggers is something else entirely — and that's the shift this scene covers. Module 5 covered Shield Advanced as prevention: always-on detection and mitigation for network and transport layer DDoS attacks. This module covers what changes once an attack is actually underway. Shield Advanced subscribers get direct engagement with the DDoS Response Team, AWS's specialized team that helps triage and mitigate sophisticated or high-impact attacks in real time — not a generic support ticket, but a team that works your specific attack with you.

During an active event, Shield Advanced can also create WAF rules automatically, tuned to the specific attack pattern it observes, without waiting for a human to hand-write a rule under pressure. And Shield Advanced's cost protection covers scaling charges incurred during a DDoS event — so an attack that forces you to scale out horizontally does not also produce a punishing bill on top of the operational disruption.

Production scenario

An e-commerce platform on Shield Advanced experienced a sophisticated application-layer DDoS attack during a product launch. Rather than manually tuning WAF rules against a shifting attack pattern, they engaged the DRT directly, who helped refine automated WAF rule creation in real time as the attack evolved. The auto-scaling costs incurred defending against the traffic surge were covered under Shield Advanced's cost protection, rather than becoming an unplanned infrastructure bill layered on top of the incident.

Prevention and response are both valuable, but they answer different questions. Prevention asks "how do we stop this from starting?" Response asks "we're already under attack — now what?" Shield Advanced answers both, just with different tools for each.

Exam angle

DRT engagement, automatic WAF rule creation, and cost protection are response capabilities, not prevention capabilities. A question framing these as ways to prevent an attack from starting is misreading Shield Advanced's role during an active event.

Takeaway: once an attack is live, Shield Advanced shifts from silent automatic defense to active, expert-assisted response — engage the DRT rather than hand-tuning rules alone.

ASK YOUR MENTOR

Questions learners actually ask

🧑‍💻 You ask ›Isn't isolating slower than just shutting it down? Why does the exam care about evidence if the threat is contained either way?
🛡️ Your MentorBoth actions stop the immediate threat, so that part is true. But shutting down or terminating throws away the answer to "how did this happen, and is it still happening somewhere else?" Without evidence, you cannot confirm the attacker did not pivot to another resource, and you cannot prevent the same compromise from recurring next month. Isolation costs you almost nothing in speed — a security group swap takes seconds — while giving you everything termination throws away.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›If automation already contains the resource, why do we still need a human-written runbook at all?
🛡️ Your MentorBecause the automation is executing the runbook. Someone has to write the specific, pre-approved steps before a Lambda function can execute them. The runbook is the decision; the automation is the execution speed. Skip the runbook and you either have no automation to build, or automation making decisions nobody approved.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›What's the actual difference between Incident Manager and OpsCenter? They feel like the same thing.
🛡️ Your MentorIncident Manager is the structure — response plans, automation documents, escalation chains, who gets paged. OpsCenter is the workspace — where a human responder actually sits and works the issue, with the relevant resources, runbook, and CloudWatch or Config data already attached. One defines the workflow; the other is where the workflow gets executed.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Do I really need a whole separate AWS account just for forensics? Can't I just use a separate IAM role in the same account?
🛡️ Your MentorNo — a separate role in the same account is still governed by that account's control plane, which may itself be compromised. Account-level isolation means the attacker's reach, however deep, stops at the account boundary. A separate role gives you organizational tidiness. A separate account gives you an actual trust boundary.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›For a compromised IAM access key, is disabling the key enough, or do I need to do more?
🛡️ Your MentorDisabling the key stops new requests, but any sessions or tokens already issued from that key may still be valid depending on the service. A thorough response revokes active sessions, disables or deletes the key, and reviews CloudTrail for what the key was actually used for during the compromise window — because disabling the key alone does not tell you what already happened.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›How does this module connect to blast radius minimization from Module 3?
🛡️ Your MentorModule 3 introduced blast radius minimization as a side effect of tight permission boundaries and SCPs — limiting what a resource could do if compromised. This module makes it operational and active: isolating a resource so it cannot spread, and running forensics in a separate account so a compromised account's own permissions cannot reach the evidence. Same principle, now applied under live incident pressure.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›What's the exam-level difference between Shield Advanced prevention and Shield Advanced response?
🛡️ Your MentorPrevention is the always-on detection and mitigation that runs before and during an attack automatically. Response is what activates once you are actively under attack and need more: DRT engagement for hands-on help, automatic WAF rule creation tuned to the live attack pattern, and cost protection for the scaling charges the attack forces on you. If a question mentions engaging AWS's response team or refining WAF rules mid-attack, that is the response side.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Is it ever correct to terminate an instance first?
🛡️ Your MentorOn the exam, essentially never as the first step for a compromised resource — the tested pattern is always isolate and preserve evidence first. In the real world there are edge cases involving active, catastrophic data exfiltration where every second counts, but even then, most teams find a quarantine security group is just as fast as termination and does not sacrifice the evidence.
