SCS-C03 · Module 13 — Governance, Auditing & Enforcement
Continuing from Module 12

A structure you can't verify is just a diagram

Module 12 built the real thing: organizational units, service control policies, resource control policies, guardrails wired across every account in the org. On paper, that structure says "nothing bad can happen here." But a guardrail nobody checks is a belief, not a control.

This module is about the harder, less glamorous question that comes right after you finish building governance: how do you prove — continuously, not once — that the guardrails are actually holding? Not "did we configure this correctly in January," but "is it still true today, and can we show that to an auditor without three weeks of manual evidence-gathering?"

That's the thread running through everything below: conformance packs, Audit Manager, Artifact, and the frameworks they support all exist to answer one question — is your governance real, or just documented?

Notice too that this module reuses the multi-account architecture from Module 3 and 12 rather than introducing new mechanics. Nothing here changes how an OU or an SCP works. What changes is the lens: instead of asking "how do I design guardrails across accounts," this module asks "how do I continuously verify those guardrails, at the same multi-account scale, without doing it by hand."

Topic 1 — Continuous Checking

AWS Config Conformance Packs

A conformance pack is a collection of AWS Config rules and remediation actions, packaged together and deployed as a single unit. Combined with Module 12's Organizations content, one deployment can push the same set of rules across every account in an OU, or the entire org, at once.

Think of it as a governance policy that actually runs. Module 12's SCPs and RCPs stop certain actions from happening at all — they're preventive. Conformance packs don't stop anything; they watch. Every rule in the pack evaluates continuously against live resource configuration, and every resource that drifts out of compliance shows up immediately, not at the next audit cycle.

AWS-provided vs. custom. AWS publishes sample conformance packs loosely mapped to common frameworks — operational best practices packs referencing NIST, PCI DSS, and similar. These are a starting point, not a certified mapping; nothing in AWS's sample packs constitutes a compliance attestation on their own. Teams commonly start from a sample pack and layer in custom Config rules for internal requirements the sample pack doesn't cover.

The exam angle worth internalizing: conformance packs operationalize continuous checking, not a one-time audit. A pack deployed today keeps evaluating every resource, every configuration change, indefinitely — that's the entire value proposition over a manual spreadsheet-based review.

Under the hood, a conformance pack is really a template — an AWS CloudFormation-based bundle that references one or more managed or custom Config rules, each with its own parameters, plus optional remediation configurations describing what should happen automatically when a resource is found non-compliant. Deploying the pack once provisions every rule inside it in a single operation, rather than clicking through each rule's setup individually. That matters operationally: a security team maintaining forty Config rules by hand across a growing account fleet is exactly the kind of manual toil conformance packs exist to eliminate.

Remediation actions deserve a second look, because they're where conformance packs stop being purely observational. A rule can be paired with an SSM Automation document that runs automatically the moment a resource is flagged non-compliant — reverting an overly permissive security group rule, or re-attaching an encryption policy that was removed. Whether remediation runs automatically or requires manual approval is itself a configuration choice, and it's a reasonable one to make deliberately: automatic remediation is powerful, but it can also silently undo a change an engineer made intentionally and forgot to account for.

The question I'd ask a candidate in an interview: "if a conformance pack shows 100% compliant right now, what does that tell you about six months from now?" Nothing — unless it's still running. That's the whole point of "continuous."

Production scenario. A platform team deploys a conformance pack built from the AWS Operational Best Practices for PCI DSS sample, targeting an OU that holds every account touching cardholder data. Six S3 buckets across three accounts fail the "s3-bucket-public-read-prohibited" rule within the first hour — not because anyone intentionally exposed them, but because a legacy Terraform module set ACLs before the org adopted stricter defaults. The pack doesn't just flag it; a paired remediation action can automatically apply the corrected bucket policy, or route the finding to Security Hub for manual triage, depending on how the pack was configured.

How compliance rolls up. Each Config rule inside the pack evaluates independently, per resource. Those per-rule results aggregate into an overall compliance status for the pack in that account. Layer in an Organization-level Config aggregator, and those per-account pack statuses roll up further — giving a security or compliance lead a single view of "is this control holding org-wide," not forty-two separate per-account dashboards.

It's worth being precise about what "aggregated" means here, because it's easy to picture a single number that flattens everything. In practice, the aggregator preserves the ability to drill back down — a compliance lead sees the org-wide rollup first, but can click into any account or any individual rule to see exactly which resource is causing that account's status to read non-compliant. The rollup is a starting point for triage, not a replacement for the underlying detail.

Common mistake: deploying a conformance pack to individual accounts as they're created, by hand, instead of attaching it at the OU level through Organizations. The pack works fine in every account someone remembered — and silently doesn't exist in the one they didn't.

Topic 2 — Evidence Collection

AWS Audit Manager

Audit Manager automates continuous evidence collection mapped to the controls of a specific compliance framework — reducing the manual, screenshot-and-spreadsheet burden that a real audit engagement traditionally imposes on the teams being audited.

It supports both AWS-provided standard frameworks and fully custom frameworks you define for internal or industry-specific requirements. As it runs, it continuously gathers evidence — configuration snapshots, API call records, resource compliance state — and organizes that evidence against each control in the framework, ready to hand to an assessor.

The distinction the exam actually tests: Audit Manager and Config/Security Hub are complementary tools doing different jobs, not redundant ones doing the same job twice. Config and Security Hub evaluate — they tell you, right now, whether a resource is compliant with a rule. Audit Manager collects and organizes evidence over time, packaging that evaluation history (and other supporting artifacts) into a structure an external or internal auditor can actually review against named controls.

Put another way: Config answers "is this true right now?" Audit Manager answers "can I prove this was true, continuously, across the assessment period, in a form an auditor will accept?"

An Audit Manager assessment is built from a framework — a structured set of controls, each mapped to one or more data sources it can pull evidence from automatically. Those sources include AWS Config rule evaluations, Security Hub findings, and CloudTrail events, but also manual evidence an assessor uploads directly, for controls that don't have an automatable AWS-native signal. That mix matters: not every control in a real compliance framework maps cleanly to a technical check, and Audit Manager is built around the assumption that some evidence will always be manually attached rather than automatically collected.

Custom frameworks are worth understanding on their own terms, separate from the AWS-provided standard ones. A company with an internal security standard that doesn't map to any named external framework — say, an internal "vendor security baseline" required before any new SaaS integration goes live — can define that as a custom framework in Audit Manager, then run assessments against it exactly the way they would against a standard one. This is a common real-world pattern: internal governance requirements get the same continuous-evidence treatment as external regulatory ones.

Production scenario. A healthcare SaaS company needs to demonstrate HIPAA-aligned controls to a new enterprise customer's security team. Instead of a compliance analyst manually pulling IAM policy exports, encryption configuration screenshots, and access logs every quarter, the team stands up an Audit Manager assessment using a HIPAA-aligned framework. Audit Manager continuously pulls evidence from AWS Config, CloudTrail, and Security Hub findings, organizing it by control. When the customer's security team asks for evidence of encryption-at-rest enforcement, the analyst exports an assessment report instead of reconstructing the answer from scratch.

A trap I've seen more than once: a team stands up an Audit Manager assessment and then manually re-checks the same S3 encryption settings Config was already continuously evaluating — because nobody connected the two. Audit Manager is meant to consume Config's and Security Hub's findings as evidence, not duplicate the checking work they already do.

Common mistake: treating an Audit Manager assessment as a substitute for continuous Config monitoring. An assessment is only as good as the evidence feeding it — if nothing is continuously evaluating compliance, Audit Manager just organizes a gap.

NOT ABOUT YOU.

Topic 3 — AWS's Own Compliance

AWS Artifact

AWS Artifact is an on-demand portal for accessing AWS's own compliance reports and certifications: SOC 1/2/3 reports, ISO certifications, PCI attestations, and similar documents describing AWS's compliance posture as a cloud provider.

This is the module's sharpest distinction, and the one most commonly missed: Artifact is not a tool for managing your compliance posture. It doesn't check your resource configurations, it doesn't collect evidence about your controls, and it has no relationship to the SCPs, RCPs, conformance packs, or Audit Manager assessments covered everywhere else in this module. Artifact answers a completely different question: "has AWS itself been independently audited against this framework, and can I get the paperwork?"

It's easy to lump Artifact in with conformance packs and Audit Manager because all three sit under the umbrella of "compliance tooling." But conformance packs and Audit Manager are about proving your controls work. Artifact is about proving AWS's controls work — the shared responsibility model's "security of the cloud" half, made retrievable as a PDF.

Agreements, not just reports. Artifact also covers agreements you accept when your own compliance program requires them — the Business Associate Addendum (BAA) for HIPAA workloads is the standard exam example. Accepting the BAA through Artifact is a step in enabling your own HIPAA-aligned use of AWS services, but accepting it doesn't make your workload compliant on its own; that still depends on how you configure and operate the services you're using.

Artifact organizes reports into two broad categories worth keeping straight. Artifact Reports gives on-demand access to the actual compliance documents — SOC 1/2/3, various ISO certifications, PCI DSS Attestation of Compliance, and region- or service-specific certifications, refreshed on AWS's own audit cadence rather than yours. Artifact Agreements is where an account owner reviews and accepts legal agreements with AWS tied to specific compliance needs, the BAA being the most exam-relevant example, alongside things like the Nondisclosure Agreement some regulated customers require before certain reports can even be viewed.

Access to some of the more sensitive reports — certain SOC 2 details, for instance — may require the requesting IAM identity to have specific Artifact permissions and, in some cases, an NDA acceptance on file before the report becomes downloadable. That's a minor operational detail, but it explains why "just go download it from Artifact" occasionally turns into a short access-provisioning task rather than an instant download for whoever first goes looking.

Production scenario. A fintech company's enterprise sales team is closing a large bank as a customer. The bank's procurement process requires proof of AWS's SOC 2 Type II report before the deal can close. A well-meaning engineer, new to the account, spends a day trying to generate a "compliance report" out of Security Hub and Config, assuming that's what's being asked for — when the actual answer is a five-minute download from Artifact of a report AWS already produced.

If a question describes needing proof of AWS's own certifications — SOC reports, ISO 27001, PCI attestation of compliance for the AWS infrastructure itself — that's Artifact, full stop. The moment the question is about proving something about your resources or your controls, Artifact is off the table and you're looking at conformance packs or Audit Manager instead.

Common mistake: a team tries to use Artifact to manage or demonstrate their own compliance posture — pointing an auditor at Artifact reports when the auditor is actually asking about the customer's controls, not AWS's. Artifact proves AWS did its part; it says nothing about whether you configured your S3 buckets, IAM policies, or encryption correctly.

Topic 4 — Frameworks, Kept Proportionate

Compliance Frameworks Mapped to AWS Tools

The exam does not require memorizing framework control numbers or clauses. It tests whether you know which AWS tool maps to which type of compliance activity. That's the whole scope of this section — deliberately kept short.

Three frameworks show up repeatedly on the exam, each governing a different kind of data or environment: NIST (general federal security control baselines), PCI DSS (payment card data), and HIPAA (healthcare data). None of them require you to know clause numbers — they require you to recognize which AWS tool operationalizes which part of a compliance program against them.

Notice the pattern: conformance packs handle the "is it configured right, continuously" question for any framework. Audit Manager handles "can I produce organized evidence for an assessment" for any framework. Artifact only enters the picture when the question is specifically about AWS's own attestation (PCI Attestation of Compliance) or an agreement you need AWS to countersign (the HIPAA BAA). The framework name changes; which tool answers which kind of question doesn't.

That consistency is the actual exam-tested skill here — not framework trivia, but recognizing that "continuous checking," "evidence for an assessment," and "AWS's own attestation" are three distinct needs that map to three distinct tools, regardless of which named framework is in the question stem.

It also explains why real compliance programs rarely pick just one tool. A company operating under PCI DSS typically runs conformance packs to keep cardholder-data-adjacent accounts continuously checked, an Audit Manager assessment to organize evidence ahead of the annual Report on Compliance, and separately references Artifact's PCI DSS Attestation of Compliance when a payment processor partner asks for proof of AWS's own certification. Three tools, three different questions, one framework name tying them together in conversation.

I'd rather see a candidate spend their prep time drilling the tool-selection instinct than memorizing PCI DSS requirement numbers. The exam consistently rewards the former and doesn't ask for the latter — don't over-invest study time in framework trivia.

Topic 5 — The Module's Core Distinction

Continuous vs. Point-in-Time Auditing

Everything above collapses into one distinction: continuous compliance tooling runs constantly and reflects status in near-real-time. Point-in-time auditing is a bounded, dated assessment — an external audit engagement, or a specific Audit Manager assessment snapshot pulled together for a deadline.

Conformance packs and Security Hub standards are the continuous side — always evaluating, always current. A traditional audit engagement, or an Audit Manager assessment frozen and exported for a specific auditor's request, is the point-in-time side — a snapshot, dated and bounded, that says "as of this date, here's the evidence."

Why point-in-time audits don't disappear. Continuous compliance tooling doesn't eliminate the need for point-in-time audits — external auditors, regulators, and enterprise customers still often require a formal, bounded assessment with a defined scope and a signed report. What continuous tooling changes is the effort required to produce that assessment: instead of weeks of manual evidence gathering, the point-in-time audit becomes largely a matter of exporting what was already being collected continuously.

It helps to think about who each mode actually serves. Continuous tooling serves the people running the environment day to day — the platform team that wants to know the moment a guardrail drifts, not three months later during an audit debrief. Point-in-time auditing serves a different audience: a third party who wasn't watching continuously and needs a bounded, attestable statement covering a defined period, with clear start and end dates and a named scope. Both audiences are legitimate, and neither replaces the other's need.

This is also where the earlier distinction between Config/Security Hub and Audit Manager clicks into place structurally. Config and Security Hub are what make Stage 1 possible — the continuous evaluation layer. Audit Manager sits at Stage 2, translating that continuous signal into an assessment structure. The point-in-time audit at Stages 3 and 4 is the output the continuous layers were building toward all along, not a separate, disconnected process bolted on afterward.

Discussion — FAQ

Questions Worth Asking Out Loud

🧑‍💻 You ask ›If Config already checks compliance continuously, why do we still need external audits at all?
🛡️ Your MentorBecause "continuous checking exists" and "an independent third party has verified and attested to it" are different claims. Regulators, banks, and enterprise customers generally require the latter — a signed, bounded report from someone with no stake in the outcome. Config gives you the ongoing truth; the external audit gives everyone else confidence in that truth.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Can I just use Audit Manager and skip conformance packs entirely?
🛡️ Your MentorTechnically you can point Audit Manager at other evidence sources, but you'd be giving up the continuous, automated checking conformance packs provide. Most real environments run both — conformance packs doing the ongoing evaluation, Audit Manager organizing that evaluation history into assessment-ready form.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Is downloading a report from Artifact the same as being compliant?
🛡️ Your MentorNo — it proves AWS's side of the shared responsibility model, not yours. You still have to configure and operate your own resources correctly; Artifact has no visibility into that at all.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›How much time should I actually spend memorizing NIST or PCI clause numbers for the exam?
🛡️ Your MentorClose to none. Know what each framework generally governs and which AWS tool operationalizes which activity against it. That's the tested skill — not clause-level recall.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Do conformance packs work the same way at the account level and the Organization level?
🛡️ Your MentorThe rules and remediation logic are the same either way — what changes is scope. Deploy at a single account and only that account is checked. Deploy through Organizations at an OU and every account in that OU gets the same checking automatically, including accounts created later.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›What's the fastest way to spot an Artifact question on the exam?
🛡️ Your MentorLook for the word "AWS's" — AWS's SOC report, AWS's ISO certification, AWS's PCI attestation. If the question is about proving something belongs to AWS, it's Artifact. If it's about proving something about your resources, it isn't.
