MODULE 08 · DOMAIN 5 — DATA PROTECTION

You know how encryption works. Now let's find what needs it.

Module 7 gave you the mechanics — envelope encryption, key policies, grants, the whole KMS machinery. That's the "how." This module is the "what" and the "with what credentials." Knowing how to encrypt a bucket doesn't help if you don't know sensitive data is sitting in it, and encrypting a database is a hollow win if the master password protecting it is sitting in a Lambda environment variable in plaintext. This is the operational, day-to-day side of data protection — the part that shows up in real incident postmortems far more often than a misconfigured KMS grant does.

Four services carry this module: Macie for finding sensitive data, Inspector for finding vulnerable infrastructure, Secrets Manager and Parameter Store for storing what needs storing, and ACM / ACM Private CA for the certificates that keep transport encrypted. We'll close with how rotation actually works under the hood, because "rotate your secrets" is advice everyone gives and almost nobody can explain mechanically.

CONCEPT 1 OF 5

Amazon Macie: automated sensitive data discovery

Macie is a fully managed service that discovers and classifies sensitive data stored in Amazon S3. It uses machine learning and pattern matching against a library of managed data identifiers — Social Security numbers, credit card numbers, AWS access keys, database connection strings — and you can layer in custom data identifiers for anything specific to your organization, like an internal account-number format or a proprietary record ID pattern.

Scope: a Macie job targets specific S3 buckets — all buckets in the account, a defined list, or buckets matching tags/criteria. This is a deliberate boundary decision: scanning everything is expensive and slow, so most real deployments scope jobs to buckets known or suspected to hold regulated or customer data, then expand from there.
Scan (sampling vs. full): you choose between a sampling scan (a percentage of objects, cheaper, faster, good for a first-pass discovery sweep across a large estate) and a full scan (every object in scope, more expensive, used when you need certainty — e.g. before a compliance attestation). This tradeoff between cost/speed and completeness is a realistic exam scenario.
Finding: Macie produces a finding per sensitive-data discovery. Each finding names the specific S3 object, the category and type of sensitive data detected (e.g. "financial information — credit card number"), and a severity/confidence indication so you can triage a backlog of findings by what actually matters first.
Remediate: findings publish to EventBridge and Security Hub, so the discovery step feeds directly into an automated response — tagging the object, restricting the bucket policy, notifying the data owner, or kicking off a deeper investigation. Macie finds; it does not remediate on its own.
PRODUCTION SCENARIO

A healthcare analytics company ingests third-party CSV exports into a "raw-landing" S3 bucket before any transformation. Nobody validates what's actually in those files before they land. A Macie discovery job scoped to that bucket, running on a scheduled interval, is the safety net that catches a vendor accidentally including patient names and diagnosis codes in a file that was supposed to be de-identified — well before that file gets copied downstream into a dozen other systems.

The exam loves testing the boundary of what Macie touches. It is an S3 tool, full stop. It does not reach into RDS, DynamoDB, or the storage behind an EC2 instance. If a question describes sensitive data sitting in a database and asks how Macie would find it — the trick is that it wouldn't, directly. You'd need to export or replicate that data into S3 first, or reach for a different discovery approach entirely.

COMMON TRAP

Assuming a Macie job covering "all S3 buckets" also covers sensitive data anywhere else in the account. It doesn't extend to RDS snapshots, DynamoDB tables, or EBS volumes — those need their own discovery or export strategy. The exam angle: a question describing sensitive data in a non-S3 store is testing whether you know Macie's boundary, not whether you know Macie's features.

CONCEPT 2 OF 5

Amazon Inspector: vulnerability management, not data discovery

Inspector automatically assesses EC2 instances, container images pushed to ECR, and Lambda functions — looking for software vulnerabilities (unpatched CVEs in installed packages or base images) and for network reachability issues, like an instance that's unintentionally exposed to the internet based on its security group and route configuration.

MACIE

Finds sensitive data

Scans S3 object contents for PII, financial data, credentials. Answers: "is there something sensitive sitting here?"

INSPECTOR

Finds vulnerable infrastructure

Scans compute (EC2, ECR images, Lambda) for known vulnerabilities and exposure. Answers: "is this workload exploitable?"

Continuous, not point-in-time

Inspector runs continuous scanning — it re-evaluates automatically when a new CVE is published or when an image is pushed, rather than waiting for you to trigger a scan. That's a meaningful shift from older point-in-time vulnerability scanning tools where a finding could be silently stale for weeks. Findings flow into Security Hub and EventBridge, which is where they connect to the broader detection ecosystem — Modules 9 and 10 go deep on how that detection layer consumes and correlates findings like these.

COMMON TRAP

Exam writers like to describe a scanning requirement in a scenario and let you assume "scanning" means one tool. If the scenario is about exposed credentials or PII sitting in storage, that's Macie's job. If it's about an unpatched package or an internet-facing instance, that's Inspector's job. Two different tools, solving two genuinely different problems, that get lumped together in casual conversation as "just security scanning" — the exam is testing whether you actually know the difference.

CONCEPT 3 OF 5 · THE MODULE'S CORE COMPARISON

Secrets Manager vs. Parameter Store

This is the single most tested comparison in this module, and it isn't really a feature checklist — it's a decision you make by answering three questions in sequence: does this value need to rotate, is there budget pressure, and is it genuinely a credential or just configuration data?

SECRETS MANAGER

Built for credentials

PARAMETER STORE

Built for configuration

Free or low-cost, no built-in rotation. Click to open the full breakdown →

The real decision framework

Start with rotation: if the value must rotate on a schedule and you want AWS to manage that lifecycle, Secrets Manager's native rotation integrations (RDS, Redshift, DocumentDB) or its Lambda-based rotation for anything else make this close to a solved problem. If rotation is a hard requirement, cost is rarely the deciding factor — the operational cost of a stale, unrotated credential dwarfs the per-secret price. Then check whether it's genuinely a secret: a database password, an API key, a third-party credential — versus general configuration like a feature flag, an environment name, or a non-sensitive endpoint URL. Configuration data with no rotation need is Parameter Store's home turf, especially at scale, where per-parameter cost differences add up.

Cost isn't the only factor, and it usually isn't even the right first factor. I've seen teams choose Parameter Store purely because it's free, store an actual rotatable database credential in it, and then build a fragile home-grown rotation script because Secrets Manager "costs money." That home-grown script is now a liability nobody owns. The few cents per month Secrets Manager charges is cheap insurance against exactly that failure mode.

PRODUCTION SCENARIO

A platform team stores their RDS master credential in Secrets Manager with native rotation enabled — zero custom code, AWS handles the rotation lifecycle against the database directly. The same application also needs a non-sensitive "current API version" string read by dozens of Lambda functions at cold start; that lives in Parameter Store Standard tier, at no cost, with no rotation need at all. Same application, two services, because the two values are fundamentally different kinds of data.

COMMON TRAP

Treating "which one is cheaper" as the whole decision. Both are encrypted at rest via KMS by default — so encryption isn't the differentiator either. The differentiator is rotation need and whether the value is genuinely a credential. Remember Module 7's key policy mechanics too: whichever service you pick, controlling who can actually call the decrypt/retrieve API is what determines who can really read that secret's value, not just who can see that it exists.

SECRETS MANAGER

Full breakdown

Rotation: built-in automatic rotation with native integrations for RDS, Redshift, and DocumentDB — AWS handles the credential change and the database side together. For anything else (a third-party API key, a custom application), you supply a Lambda function that implements the rotation contract.

Cost: charged per secret, per month, plus API call charges. Not free, but the cost of the rotation automation you're buying alongside storage.

Cross-account sharing: supports resource-based policies directly on the secret, so another account's role can be granted access to retrieve it without you having to build a custom sharing mechanism.

Typical use case: database credentials, third-party API keys, anything that is a genuine credential and benefits from scheduled rotation.

PARAMETER STORE

Full breakdown

Tiers: Standard tier is free, with parameter size and throughput limits; Advanced tier costs more per parameter but raises those limits and adds parameter policies (like automatic expiration).

Rotation: no built-in rotation mechanism. If you need rotation, you script it yourself — typically with a scheduled Lambda function you own end to end.

Cost: Standard tier is free; Advanced tier is a small per-parameter monthly cost. Both are dramatically cheaper than Secrets Manager at scale for non-rotating values.

Typical use case: configuration data that isn't a credential — feature flags, environment identifiers, non-sensitive endpoint values, application settings.

CONCEPT 4 OF 5

ACM and ACM Private CA

AWS Certificate Manager (ACM) issues free public TLS certificates for use with AWS-integrated services — CloudFront, Application Load Balancer, API Gateway — and handles renewal automatically as long as the certificate stays attached to a validated, integrated resource. The design constraint that trips people up: the private key never leaves ACM or the service it's attached to. You cannot export it to install on some other, non-integrated server.

"Public-facing or internal?" — the decision diagram

Click through the decision path below.

Is the certificate for a public-facing endpoint on an AWS-integrated service (CloudFront / ALB / API Gateway)?
→ YES: Public ACM certificate
Free, auto-renewing, zero certificate-management overhead — as long as it stays attached to a supported, validated AWS resource. This is the default answer for "we need TLS on our public website or API," and the exam rewards recognizing when a scenario is this simple.
Do you need your own internal certificate authority — internal TLS, mutual TLS between services, or IoT device certificates at scale?
→ YES: ACM Private CA
A managed private certificate authority you control, used to issue certificates that aren't meant to be publicly trusted — internal service-to-service TLS, mutual TLS (mTLS) authentication between microservices, or certificates provisioned to thousands of IoT devices. This is a paid service and a genuinely different tool solving a different problem than public ACM: you're establishing your own trust hierarchy, not borrowing a public one.
PRODUCTION SCENARIO

A manufacturing company deploys 40,000 IoT sensors across factory floors, each needing a unique certificate for authenticated, encrypted communication back to AWS IoT Core. Public ACM certificates aren't the right shape for this at all — there's no public-facing website involved and no path to attach 40,000 individual public certs at that scale. ACM Private CA, integrated with IoT Core's certificate provisioning, is the tool actually built for issuing certificates at that volume under an authority the company controls.

COMMON TRAP

An expired certificate is one of the most common, entirely preventable production incidents there is — an outage caused not by a security failure but by a calendar failure. ACM's automatic renewal on integrated services exists specifically to take that failure mode off the table. The trap is assuming that protection extends everywhere: a certificate exported or used outside ACM's supported integrations, or one issued by ACM Private CA without your own renewal automation, is back to being your responsibility to track.

COMMON TRAP

Assuming a public ACM certificate's private key can be exported for use on a non-integrated service, like a self-managed EC2 web server outside the supported integration list. It cannot, by design — that's a deliberate security boundary, not a missing feature. If a scenario needs a portable certificate for a server ACM doesn't directly manage, you're looking at a different certificate source entirely, or ACM Private CA issuing to that resource instead.

CONCEPT 5 OF 5

Secret rotation patterns

"Just rotate your secrets" is easy advice to give and hard to implement correctly, because a naive rotation — change the password, done — creates a window where the credential is invalid before every consuming application has actually picked up the new value. Secrets Manager solves this by staging rotation into four distinct steps.

The four-step rotation Lambda contract

Click each step to see what it does and why the order matters.

1

createSecret

2

setSecret

3

testSecret

4

finishSecret

createSecret: generates a brand-new secret value and stores it as a new, pending version — the current, working credential is untouched and still active. Nothing that depends on the old value breaks yet.
setSecret: takes that new pending value and actually applies it to the target system — for example, setting the new password on the database itself. At this point both the old and new credentials may work briefly, depending on the target.
testSecret: validates that the new pending value actually works against the target system before committing to it — attempting a real connection or authentication check. If this fails, rotation can be aborted before anything consuming the secret is affected.
finishSecret: promotes the pending version to be the current version, which is what applications retrieve going forward. Only after testing succeeds does the "official" secret value change from an application's point of view.
PRODUCTION SCENARIO

An application fleet reads a database credential from Secrets Manager at connection time rather than caching it indefinitely. When rotation runs, createSecret and setSecret prepare and apply the new password, testSecret confirms a real connection succeeds with it, and only then does finishSecret make it the version new connections retrieve — existing connections aren't forcibly killed, and no application ever sees a moment where the "current" secret is a password that doesn't actually work.

Tie this back to blast-radius thinking from Module 3: a leaked credential is only as dangerous as the window it stays valid for. Rotating a credential every 24 hours instead of every 90 days doesn't stop a leak from happening, but it shrinks the value of that leak enormously — an attacker with a credential that expires in hours has a much narrower opportunity than one with a credential that's valid for months. Rotation frequency is a blast-radius control, not just a compliance checkbox.

COMMON TRAP

Writing a rotation Lambda that fails silently — say, setSecret succeeds but testSecret is never properly implemented, so a broken credential gets promoted to "current" anyway. The four-step contract exists precisely to prevent this, but only if you actually implement testSecret meaningfully rather than as a stub that always returns success.

Discover, Store, or Certify?

Each scenario below describes a real operational need. Click a scenario to reveal the correct service and the reasoning behind it.

1. You need to find exposed customer PII that may have been accidentally uploaded into a data lake bucket.

MACIE

This is sensitive-data discovery inside S3 — exactly Macie's job. A scoped or full scan over the data lake bucket surfaces findings for any PII patterns detected.

2. You need to store an RDS database master password that must rotate automatically every 30 days.

SECRETS MANAGER

A genuine credential with a hard rotation requirement, and RDS is one of Secrets Manager's native rotation integrations — no custom Lambda needed.

3. You need to store a non-secret configuration value — the current API version string — read by many Lambda functions at cold start.

PARAMETER STORE

Not a credential, no rotation need, high read volume — Standard tier's free pricing makes this the obvious choice.

4. You need to issue an internal mutual TLS certificate between two microservices that never face the public internet.

ACM PRIVATE CA

Internal trust, not public trust — you need your own certificate authority. Public ACM certificates aren't designed for this use case.

5. You need a free, auto-renewing TLS certificate for a public-facing CloudFront distribution.

PUBLIC ACM CERTIFICATE

Textbook public ACM use case — free, auto-renewing, and CloudFront is a supported integration.

6. You need to assess a fleet of EC2 instances and their container images for known software vulnerabilities.

INSPECTOR

Vulnerability management on compute and container images — this is Inspector's exact scope, not Macie's.

7. You need to provision unique certificates to 15,000 IoT devices for authenticated device-to-cloud communication.

ACM PRIVATE CA

Certificate issuance at IoT scale under your own authority — precisely what ACM Private CA is built for.

8. You need to store a third-party SaaS API key that has no native AWS rotation integration, but you still want scheduled rotation.

SECRETS MANAGER (CUSTOM LAMBDA ROTATION)

Still a genuine credential needing rotation — you write the Lambda implementing the four-step contract against the third party's own API.

9. You need to confirm whether an S3 bucket holding years of archived customer support tickets contains any accidentally-embedded credit card numbers.

MACIE (FULL SCAN)

Given the compliance stakes of credit card data specifically, a full scan rather than a sampling scan is the right choice here — completeness matters more than cost.

PRODUCTION TRAP GALLERY

Six ways this goes wrong in production

Every one of these is a real incident pattern, not a hypothetical.

TRAP 01

The expired certificate outage

A certificate was manually issued outside ACM's supported integrations and never wired into any renewal automation. Nobody owned tracking its expiry date. It expired on a Saturday, and the outage wasn't discovered until customer complaints started Monday morning. ACM's auto-renewal only protects what stays attached to a validated, integrated resource.

TRAP 02

The credential hiding in Parameter Store

A database password was stored in Parameter Store Standard tier to avoid Secrets Manager's per-secret cost. It never rotated, because nobody scripted rotation for it, because "it's just Parameter Store, that's not really what it's for." Two years later it was still the original password, unrotated, from initial provisioning.

TRAP 03

The Macie scope that missed a bucket

A Macie job was scoped to a hand-maintained list of "known sensitive" buckets. A new bucket, created for a one-off data export, was never added to that list. It sat unscanned for months with customer records in it before anyone noticed the gap during an audit.

TRAP 04

The rotation Lambda that failed silently

A custom rotation Lambda implemented createSecret and setSecret correctly but had a testSecret function that always returned success without actually attempting a connection. A bad password got promoted to "current" during a routine rotation, and every application relying on that secret started failing to connect at the same moment.

TRAP 05

Confusing Macie findings with Inspector findings

A security team triaged an Inspector finding about an unpatched package as if it were a data-exposure event, spending hours looking for leaked customer data that was never the issue — the finding was about a vulnerable library, not exposed PII. Knowing which tool produced a finding tells you what kind of problem you're actually looking at.

TRAP 06

The exported private key that wasn't

A team assumed they could export their ACM public certificate's private key to install on a self-managed, non-integrated server. They couldn't, by design, and the misunderstanding cost a full day of scrambling to source and install a certificate through a different path before a launch deadline.

Q1. A company needs to find any credit card numbers accidentally stored across dozens of S3 buckets. Which service should they use?

Macie is purpose-built for sensitive-data discovery in S3, including credit card number patterns via managed data identifiers.

Q2. Which AWS service does Macie's sensitive-data discovery directly cover?

Macie is an S3-focused tool. It does not scan RDS, DynamoDB, or EC2-attached storage directly.

Q3. A team needs to assess Lambda functions and ECR container images for known vulnerabilities. Which service fits?

Inspector assesses EC2, ECR images, and Lambda functions for vulnerabilities and network exposure.

Q4. A database credential must rotate automatically every 30 days with AWS managing the rotation lifecycle. What's the best fit?

Secrets Manager's native rotation integrations (RDS, Redshift, DocumentDB) handle this without custom code.

Q5. A non-sensitive feature-flag value is read by hundreds of Lambda functions and needs no rotation. What's the most cost-effective storage choice?

Non-secret configuration data with no rotation need is exactly what free Parameter Store Standard tier is for.

Q6. A company wants a free, auto-renewing TLS certificate for a public CloudFront distribution. What should they use?

Public ACM certificates are free, auto-renewing, and CloudFront is a supported integration.

Q7. A team needs to issue mutual TLS certificates between internal microservices that never face the public internet. What's the correct tool?

Internal trust between services calls for your own certificate authority — ACM Private CA, not a publicly-trusted ACM certificate.

Q8. Which step of the Secrets Manager rotation Lambda contract validates the new credential actually works before it's promoted?

testSecret confirms the new value actually works against the target system before finishSecret promotes it to current.

Q9. Why is Secrets Manager rotation staged into four steps rather than changing the credential in one action?

The staged contract prevents a gap where the secret has changed but consuming applications haven't yet retrieved the new value.

Q10. A scenario describes an internet-facing EC2 instance that shouldn't be reachable from outside the VPC, discovered via automated network-exposure analysis. Which service most likely surfaced this?

Inspector's network reachability analysis identifies unintended internet exposure on EC2 instances, alongside its vulnerability scanning.

Q11. Shortening a credential's rotation window from 90 days to 24 hours primarily reduces what?

Faster rotation doesn't prevent a leak, but it shrinks the window an attacker can actually use a leaked credential — classic blast-radius minimization.
DISCUSSION-STYLE FAQ

Questions engineers actually ask

🧑‍💻 You ask ›Secrets Manager costs money per secret — when is Parameter Store genuinely the better choice, not just the cheaper one?
🛡️ Your MentorWhen the value truly isn't a credential and doesn't need rotation — a feature flag, an environment name, a non-sensitive configuration value. In that case Secrets Manager's rotation machinery is pure overhead you'd never use. Parameter Store genuinely is the better tool there, not just the cheaper one, because it matches what the data actually is.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Can Macie scan an RDS database if I just point it at the right ARN?
🛡️ Your MentorNo — Macie only reaches into S3. If you need to discover sensitive data sitting in RDS, you'd need to export a snapshot or extract into S3 first, then let Macie scan that export. There's no direct RDS integration.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Is Inspector basically the same thing as GuardDuty?
🛡️ Your MentorNo, and this distinction matters for the exam too. Inspector assesses known vulnerabilities and exposure in your own resources (EC2, ECR, Lambda) — it's proactive vulnerability management. GuardDuty is threat detection, looking for signs of active malicious behavior in account activity and network traffic. Different job entirely; we cover GuardDuty in Modules 9-10.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›If ACM certificates are free, why would anyone pay for ACM Private CA?
🛡️ Your MentorBecause they're solving different problems. Public ACM certificates are trusted by browsers and public clients by default. ACM Private CA issues certificates trusted only within a hierarchy you define — for internal service-to-service TLS, mTLS, or device fleets where public trust isn't the goal. You'd never use a public certificate for those, regardless of cost.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Do I have to write the rotation Lambda myself for every secret?
🛡️ Your MentorOnly for secrets outside Secrets Manager's native integrations. RDS, Redshift, and DocumentDB get managed rotation with no custom code. Anything else — a third-party API key, a custom application credential — needs your own Lambda implementing the four-step contract.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Are Secrets Manager and Parameter Store both encrypted the same way?
🛡️ Your MentorBoth encrypt at rest via KMS by default, yes. That's not the differentiator between them — rotation and whether the value is genuinely a credential are. But it does mean whichever you pick, Module 7's key policy rules govern who can actually decrypt and retrieve the value.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Does a sampling Macie scan ever miss something a full scan would catch?
🛡️ Your MentorYes, by design — sampling checks a percentage of objects, not all of them, trading completeness for speed and cost. That's fine for a broad first-pass sweep across a large estate, but for anything with real compliance stakes, like confirming no credit card data exists in an archive, a full scan is the right call.
