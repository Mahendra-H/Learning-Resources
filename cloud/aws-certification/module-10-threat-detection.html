SCS-C03 / Module 10: Threat Detection Services
Continuing from Module 9

Logs are not the same thing as detection.

Module 9 gave you the full logging foundation — CloudTrail capturing every API call, VPC Flow Logs recording every packet decision, Config tracking every configuration drift, all centralized into buckets and log groups you control. That was necessary. It is nowhere close to sufficient.

Here's the uncomfortable truth every security team eventually confronts: a perfectly configured, beautifully centralized logging pipeline that nobody — and nothing — is actively watching is functionally identical to no logging pipeline at all. The attacker doesn't care that you have 90 days of VPC Flow Logs sitting in an S3 bucket if nobody notices the flow log entry showing your bastion host suddenly talking to an EC2 instance in a country your company has no business presence in.

This module is where raw data becomes automated detection. You're moving from Domain 1's logging foundation into Domain 1's detection services — the managed AWS services that continuously read the data Module 9 taught you to generate, apply threat intelligence and machine learning against it, and surface findings a human (or an automated workflow) can act on. Four services, one core mental model, and a set of capability boundaries the exam tests aggressively.

Service 1 of 4 — Amazon GuardDuty

GuardDuty: continuous, managed threat detection.

Amazon GuardDuty is a managed threat detection service. That single word — managed — is doing a lot of work, and it's the first thing to internalize: you do not feed GuardDuty its own copy of your logs, you do not configure trails or flow log destinations for it, and you do not manage retention for what it analyzes. GuardDuty reaches into AWS's own internal copies of VPC Flow Logs, DNS query logs, CloudTrail management events, S3 data events, and (when EKS is in play) EKS audit logs — and it does this analysis independently of whatever CloudTrail trail or Flow Log setup you built for your own purposes back in Module 9.

This is a nuance the exam loves to test in a specific way: if a question asks "what do you need to enable before GuardDuty can analyze VPC Flow Logs," the correct answer is nothing — you just enable GuardDuty itself. Your own Flow Logs configuration from Module 9 exists for your own audit and troubleshooting needs; GuardDuty's internal consumption of the same underlying network metadata is separate and requires no setup on your part beyond turning the service on.

Once enabled, GuardDuty continuously correlates what it observes against threat intelligence feeds (AWS's own, plus curated third-party feeds like Proofpoint and CrowdStrike) and behavioral models trained on what "normal" looks like for your environment. When something crosses a threshold, it produces a finding — a structured, typed record describing what was observed, why it matters, and how confident GuardDuty is.

Finding types and severity

Findings follow a naming convention that tells you the threat purpose and resource type at a glance — for example UnauthorizedAccess:IAMUser/InstanceCredentialExfiltration or Recon:EC2/PortProbeUnprotectedPort. Broadly, findings cluster around a few recognizable categories the exam expects you to recognize instantly:

  • Reconnaissance — port scanning, unusual API call patterns consistent with an attacker mapping your environment.
  • Instance compromise — an EC2 instance communicating with a known command-and-control domain, mining cryptocurrency, or exhibiting backdoor behavior.
  • Account compromise — API calls from an unusual geographic location, credentials being used from a Tor exit node, or a pattern consistent with credential exfiltration.
  • S3-specific findings — a bucket policy change that suddenly opens data to the internet, or access patterns consistent with data being scraped by a compromised identity.

Each finding also carries a severity score (Low, Medium, High) so triage can be prioritized — a High-severity account-compromise finding earns immediate attention in a way a Low-severity recon finding usually doesn't.

Production Scenario

A fintech company's GuardDuty console lights up with an UnauthorizedAccess:IAMUser/ConsoleLoginSuccess.B finding — a successful console login from an IP address GuardDuty has never seen associated with that user, geolocated to a country the company has no employees in. Because CloudTrail management events were already flowing (thanks to the Module 9 setup) and GuardDuty was already consuming them internally, the finding appears within minutes of the login, not during a quarterly log review three months later.

Extended threat detection — correlation over time

GuardDuty's newer extended threat detection capability is where the exam's questions get more sophisticated. A single suspicious event — one odd API call, one unusual login — is often just noise; real attacks unfold as a sequence across multiple stages: initial access, followed by reconnaissance, followed by privilege escalation, followed by data access or lateral movement. Extended threat detection is GuardDuty's ability to correlate signals across multiple data sources and across time to recognize that sequence as a single, higher-confidence, multi-stage attack — rather than reporting four disconnected low-confidence findings that a human analyst has to mentally stitch together.

Concretely: instead of surfacing "unusual API call" on Monday and "unusual data access" on Wednesday as two separate low-priority tickets, extended threat detection recognizes the pattern connecting them and raises a single critical finding describing the likely attack sequence. This is what "extended" adds over single-signal detection — it's not just more alerts, it's recognizing that isolated signals belong to one coordinated event.

Capability boundary

What GuardDuty explicitly does not do.

This is the single most commonly tested distinction attached to GuardDuty, and it's tested precisely because it's the most common real-world misconception among teams new to the service. GuardDuty is a detection and alerting service. Full stop. It does not close the loop on its own:

CapabilityDoes GuardDuty do this?
Detect malicious or anomalous activityYes — this is its entire purpose
Generate a structured, typed finding with severityYes
Automatically remediate anythingNo
Block malicious network trafficNo
Modify, revoke, or quarantine IAM permissionsNo
Terminate or isolate a compromised instanceNo

If any of that response needs to happen automatically, you build it — typically as an EventBridge rule that reacts to a GuardDuty finding event and triggers a Lambda function, a Systems Manager Automation document, or a Step Functions workflow that does the actual remediation (isolating the instance, attaching a restrictive security group, disabling the credential). GuardDuty hands you the signal; the response pipeline is separate infrastructure you own — and Module 11's Incident Response content builds directly on this exact seam.

I've watched more than one team assume GuardDuty was "handling" a compromised instance because a finding showed up in the console, while the instance kept mining cryptocurrency for another six hours. GuardDuty told them, clearly, in the finding detail. Nothing was listening for the event to act on it. Detection without a wired response is just a very expensive notification system.

Service 2 of 4 — AWS Security Hub

Security Hub: one dashboard, many sources.

Where GuardDuty is a generator of findings, Security Hub is primarily an aggregation and normalization layer. This is the contrast the exam wants you to hold in your head simultaneously: GuardDuty analyzes raw log data and produces new findings from scratch; Security Hub, for the most part, does not generate findings from raw logs the way GuardDuty does. Instead, it collects findings that other services already produced — GuardDuty, Inspector, Macie, Config, and supported third-party security tools — and normalizes all of them into one consistent format (the AWS Security Finding Format) inside a single dashboard.

Think of it less as a detective and more as a very well-organized inbox: GuardDuty, Inspector, and Macie each speak their own dialect of "here's something wrong"; Security Hub translates all of it into one shared vocabulary so your security team isn't context-switching between five different consoles with five different finding schemas during an investigation.

Security standards — Security Hub's own findings

There is one place Security Hub does generate its own findings: automated compliance checks. When you enable a security standard — the AWS Foundational Security Best Practices (FSBP) standard or the CIS AWS Foundations Benchmark — Security Hub continuously evaluates your account's configuration against each control in that standard (things like "root account MFA is enabled" or "S3 buckets should block public access") and produces its own findings when a control fails. This is a preview of the compliance-automation theme that Modules 12 and 13 own in full depth; for this module, just recognize that standards checks are the one category of Security Hub finding that isn't simply relayed from another service.

Production Scenario

A retail company runs GuardDuty, Inspector, and Macie independently across 40 AWS accounts. Their SOC team used to check three consoles every morning, cross-referencing account IDs by hand to figure out which findings related to the same incident. After enabling Security Hub with cross-account aggregation, all three services' findings — plus FSBP compliance findings — land in one normalized dashboard in the designated administrator account, searchable and filterable the same way regardless of which service originally generated them.

Cross-account, cross-region aggregation

Security Hub supports a designated Security Hub administrator account that can aggregate findings from member accounts across an AWS Organization, and can further aggregate across regions using a designated home region. This is a direct, concrete payoff of the multi-account architecture thread introduced back in Module 3 — the entire point of that account structure is that security visibility shouldn't require logging into 40 separate accounts, and Security Hub's aggregation model is exactly how that promise gets delivered for detection findings specifically.

Service 3 of 4 — Amazon Detective

Detective: the investigation layer, not a detection source.

Here's a question worth sitting with before reading further: once Security Hub shows you a GuardDuty finding that a specific IAM user made an unusual API call from an unfamiliar IP address, what's your very next question as an investigator? Almost certainly something like — what else did that IAM user do around that time? What other resources did that IP address touch? Is this connected to anything else we've seen? Security Hub's dashboard doesn't answer that. Amazon Detective exists specifically to answer it.

Detective automatically builds and maintains behavior graphs — visualized, navigable maps of the relationships between findings, IAM entities (users and roles), IP addresses, and resources, plotted across time. Instead of manually cross-referencing CloudTrail events, VPC Flow Log entries, and GuardDuty findings by hand across dozens of log files, you get an interactive graph you can pivot through: click the flagged IAM user, see every API call it made in the surrounding time window, click a suspicious IP address, see every resource that IP touched across your entire account — or across every linked account, if you've enabled cross-account behavior graphs.

The position to hold firmly for the exam: Detective is explicitly the investigation tool that picks up after a finding already exists. It is not a detection source in its own right — it doesn't generate the initial "something is wrong" signal the way GuardDuty does.

Why Detective needs a data source already flowing

This is the direct callback to Module 9 the brief wants explicit: Detective's behavior graphs are built from data that has to already be flowing into it — GuardDuty findings, VPC Flow Logs, and CloudTrail management events. If those sources aren't enabled and populated before you need to investigate, Detective has nothing to visualize. It doesn't retroactively reconstruct history from nothing; it's only as useful as the underlying telemetry Module 9's logging foundation was responsible for establishing. A team that skipped VPC Flow Logs entirely will open Detective during an incident and find a graph with conspicuous gaps exactly where the network-level story should be.

Production Scenario

An analyst receives a High-severity GuardDuty finding for a compromised EC2 instance. Rather than manually querying CloudTrail and Flow Log data across three linked accounts, they pivot directly from the finding into Detective's behavior graph, immediately see that the instance's role was assumed from an unusual source, and trace that role's activity backward to a misconfigured trust policy — an investigation that would have taken hours of manual log correlation compressed into minutes of graph navigation.

Service 4 of 4 — Amazon Security Lake

Security Lake: centralizing everything, on an open schema.

GuardDuty detects, Security Hub aggregates findings, Detective investigates relationships behind a specific finding — but none of those three services are built to be your organization's long-term, queryable, bulk repository of raw security telemetry across every source you have. That's the gap Amazon Security Lake fills.

Security Lake is a purpose-built, managed data lake specifically for security data. It automatically collects and normalizes logs from AWS sources (CloudTrail, VPC Flow Logs, Route 53 resolver logs, Security Hub findings) and from supported third-party sources, storing everything centrally in a common format so it can be queried consistently and integrated with external SIEM tools — Splunk, or a custom analytics pipeline built on Athena.

OCSF — the schema that makes it work

The detail the exam most wants you to recognize by name: Security Lake normalizes data into the Open Cybersecurity Schema Framework (OCSF) — an open, vendor-neutral standard, not an AWS-proprietary format. This matters for a very practical reason. Without a common schema, every source's logs arrive shaped differently: CloudTrail's JSON structure looks nothing like a third-party firewall's log format, which looks nothing like Route 53 resolver query logs. Analysts and SIEM tools would need custom parsing logic for every single source, forever.

OCSF solves that by giving every event type — network activity, authentication, file activity, and dozens more — a standardized set of fields regardless of which product generated the original log. A query written against "authentication events" works the same way whether the underlying event originated from CloudTrail or from a supported third-party identity provider, because both get normalized into the same OCSF authentication event class before they land in the lake.

Because OCSF is an open, cross-industry standard (not something AWS invented and controls exclusively), it's the answer whenever an exam question is fishing for whether you know Security Lake's normalization is built on an open framework rather than a proprietary one — a trap explicitly called out in Section 2's exam-angle list.

Production Scenario

A company runs AWS workloads alongside an on-premises SIEM that also ingests firewall and endpoint logs from non-AWS sources. Rather than building custom ETL pipelines to reshape CloudTrail and VPC Flow Log data into whatever format their SIEM expects, they enable Security Lake, which normalizes everything into OCSF — and because their SIEM already understands OCSF-formatted data from other vendors, integration is a subscription, not a months-long parsing project.

The core mental model

How the four services actually relate.

Every service you've just learned solves a different problem, and the exam's scenario questions test whether you can place a described operational need at the correct point in this chain. It is not a strict, mandatory pipeline — each service has real independent value — but the typical relationship, in order, is the mental model to hold:

GuardDuty · Macie · Inspector · Config
generate findings from raw signals
These four services each analyze a different slice of your environment and produce their own findings independently: GuardDuty (threats, covered fully above), Macie (sensitive data discovery), Inspector (vulnerability scanning), and Config (configuration drift and compliance rules). None of them wait for another service — each generates findings on its own.
Security Hub
aggregates + normalizes findings, runs compliance standards
Security Hub pulls findings from all four upstream services into one normalized format and dashboard, and additionally generates its own findings from FSBP / CIS standard checks. This is the layer your SOC actually watches day to day, rather than switching between four consoles.
Amazon Detective
investigates the relationships behind a specific finding
When a Security Hub finding needs deeper investigation, Detective's behavior graph shows what else the flagged entity or IP touched, across time and across accounts — reconstructing the story a raw finding alone can't tell.
Amazon Security Lake
centralizes underlying raw data at scale, OCSF-normalized, for SIEM + broad querying
Security Lake isn't strictly "downstream" of Detective — it independently ingests the same underlying raw log sources (plus Security Hub findings) for long-term, broad, OCSF-normalized querying and SIEM integration, supporting analysis at a scale and time horizon beyond what any single finding investigation needs.

Don't memorize this as a rigid pipeline where step 2 can't happen without step 1 finishing. Macie can run standalone with nobody touching Security Hub. Detective needs data sources flowing but doesn't require Security Hub as an intermediary. What the exam actually tests is whether you know each service's role well enough to place it correctly in a described scenario — not whether you can recite an assembly line.

Exercise

What does this service actually do?

Pick an answer for each scenario. The reasoning reveals immediately either way — read it even when you're right.

1. Does GuardDuty block the malicious IP address it detects in a finding?
GuardDuty never modifies network traffic, security groups, or NACLs on its own. If you want the IP blocked automatically, you build that as a separate automation reacting to the finding — this is the single most tested capability boundary in this module.
2. Does Security Hub generate its own findings directly from raw CloudTrail logs, the way GuardDuty does?
Security Hub is primarily an aggregation and normalization layer. The one exception is standards checks (FSBP, CIS) — those it evaluates and generates itself. Everything else arrives from GuardDuty, Inspector, Macie, or third-party tools.
3. A team wants to visualize everything a compromised IAM role touched across three linked accounts over the past week. Which service?
This is exactly Detective's behavior-graph use case — relationships between an entity, resources, IPs, and time, across accounts. Neither GuardDuty nor Security Hub visualizes relationships this way.
4. Which service centralizes raw security logs from AWS and supported third-party tools, normalized for long-term SIEM querying?
Security Hub aggregates findings (already-processed alerts), not raw logs at scale. Security Lake is purpose-built for centralizing the underlying raw telemetry itself.
5. True or false: GuardDuty requires you to configure your own dedicated CloudTrail trail before it can analyze management events.
GuardDuty is a managed service that reaches into AWS's own internal event stream. Your Module 9 CloudTrail setup serves your own audit needs — it isn't a prerequisite for GuardDuty's analysis.
6. A team wants a compromised EC2 instance automatically isolated the instant GuardDuty detects it, with zero manual steps. What do they need to build?
GuardDuty never isolates anything on its own. The standard pattern — and the one Module 11 builds on — is an EventBridge rule watching for the finding event type, triggering the actual remediation action.
7. Amazon Detective returns an almost-empty behavior graph during an active investigation. What's the most likely cause?
Detective only visualizes data that was actually flowing. It doesn't reconstruct history retroactively — this is the direct dependency on Module 9's logging foundation.
8. What schema does Amazon Security Lake normalize ingested data into?
OCSF is open and vendor-neutral, not AWS-proprietary. That's exactly why third-party SIEM tools can integrate with Security Lake data consistently.
9. What does GuardDuty's extended threat detection add over standard, single-event findings?
Extended threat detection connects isolated-looking events into one higher-confidence, multi-stage finding — it's about correlation and recognition, never automated response.
10. A Security Hub administrator account aggregates findings from 40 member accounts across an Organization. Which earlier cross-cutting thread does this directly build on?
Cross-account aggregation is a direct, concrete payoff of the multi-account architecture thread first introduced in Module 3 — visibility without needing to log into every account individually.
Production Trap Gallery

Where real teams get this wrong.

The "GuardDuty will handle it" assumption

A startup's on-call engineer saw a High-severity CryptoCurrency:EC2/BitcoinTool.B finding, assumed GuardDuty's console alert meant the service was actively containing the threat, and moved on to other tickets. The compromised instance kept mining for another six hours before someone manually checked back.
Wire every High-severity finding type to an EventBridge rule with a real remediation action — don't rely on the console alert alone.

Security Hub without cross-account aggregation

A company enabled Security Hub in their management account only, assuming it would automatically surface findings from all 30 accounts in their Organization. It didn't — member accounts never delegated a Security Hub administrator, so findings in those accounts sat invisible until an audit caught the gap.
Designate a Security Hub administrator account explicitly and confirm every member account is actually associated — aggregation isn't automatic just because Security Hub is "on" somewhere.

Detective with nothing to show

During an active incident, an analyst opened Amazon Detective expecting a rich behavior graph and got a nearly blank canvas. VPC Flow Logs had never been enabled in that account — Detective had no network-activity data to visualize, right when it mattered most.
Confirm CloudTrail, VPC Flow Logs, and GuardDuty are enabled and flowing account-wide before an incident — Detective can't backfill history that was never captured.

Closing findings without checking correlation

A junior analyst closed two separate GuardDuty findings for the same IAM user as unrelated "false positive-looking" noise, without pivoting into Security Hub to see they were 36 hours apart on the same entity — missing what extended threat detection was trying to flag as a multi-stage sequence.
Treat repeated findings on the same entity as a signal to correlate, not a signal to bulk-dismiss — check Security Hub context before closing anything.

Building custom parsers Security Lake already solved

A platform team spent two months writing custom transformation scripts to reshape CloudTrail and Flow Log data into their SIEM's expected format, unaware that Security Lake already normalizes everything into OCSF — a schema their SIEM could ingest natively with a fraction of the engineering effort.
Check whether Security Lake's OCSF-normalized output already matches what your downstream tooling expects before building custom ETL from scratch.

Treating Detective as a detection source

A team configured Detective and then wondered why they weren't getting proactive alerts the way GuardDuty produces them — Detective was never going to generate that initial signal on its own; it has no alerting mechanism because investigation, not detection, is its entire purpose.
Keep GuardDuty as your alert source and Detective strictly as the tool you open after a finding exists — don't expect Detective to tell you something is wrong in the first place.

Discussion

Questions worth asking out loud.

🧑‍💻 You ask ›If Security Hub already shows me findings, why do I need Detective at all?
🛡️ Your MentorBecause a finding tells you what happened, not the full story around it. Security Hub shows you "this IAM user made an unusual API call." Detective shows you everything else that IAM user did in the surrounding window, every IP it touched, every resource it accessed, and how those connect to other findings across accounts. Security Hub is the notification; Detective is the investigation that follows it.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Do I need all four services, or can I get away with just GuardDuty?
🛡️ Your MentorGuardDuty alone gives you detection, which is genuinely valuable on its own. But without Security Hub you're manually checking multiple consoles as your services multiply, without Detective you're manually correlating logs by hand during every investigation, and without Security Lake you have no centralized, queryable long-term store for SIEM integration or broad historical analysis. Each service solves a problem the others don't.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Does enabling Security Hub turn off GuardDuty or replace it?
🛡️ Your MentorNo — they're complementary, not competing. GuardDuty keeps running and generating its own findings exactly as before; Security Hub simply pulls those findings into its normalized dashboard alongside findings from other services. Disabling GuardDuty would mean Security Hub has nothing to aggregate from that source.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Is OCSF something only AWS uses, or is it broader?
🛡️ Your MentorIt's genuinely open and cross-industry — that's the entire point. Multiple vendors and security tools align to OCSF specifically so data from different sources can be queried and correlated consistently. AWS adopting it for Security Lake is AWS participating in an existing open standard, not inventing a proprietary one.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›If GuardDuty doesn't remediate anything, what's the point of severity scoring?
🛡️ Your MentorSeverity scoring drives triage priority for whatever response process you've built — automated or human. A High-severity account-compromise finding should page someone or trigger automation immediately; a Low-severity recon finding might just get logged for weekly review. Severity doesn't cause action by itself, but it's the input your response pipeline uses to decide urgency.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›Can Security Lake ingest data from outside AWS entirely?
🛡️ Your MentorYes, for supported third-party sources — that's a meaningful part of its value. Because everything normalizes into OCSF regardless of origin, Security Lake can genuinely act as a central point for security telemetry that spans your AWS footprint and non-AWS tooling like firewalls or endpoint protection, not just AWS-native logs.
🧑‍💻 You ask ›How is extended threat detection different from just enabling more GuardDuty finding types?
🛡️ Your MentorEnabling more finding types gives you more individual signals — still one-at-a-time. Extended threat detection is about connecting signals that already exist across sources and time into a single, higher-confidence multi-stage finding. It's a correlation capability, not a volume increase.

What's next

Detection tells you. Response is what you do.

You now have the full detection layer: GuardDuty watching continuously and alerting, Security Hub aggregating and normalizing that noise into one dashboard, Detective giving you the investigation graph behind any single finding, and Security Lake centralizing the raw telemetry underneath all of it on an open schema. That's Domain 1 — Detection — in full depth.

But detection, on its own, only ever tells you something is wrong. It doesn't decide what to do about it, how fast, or how carefully. Module 11 is the course's next domain shift — from Domain 1 into Domain 2, Incident Response — and it's where you'll actually build the response side of everything this module deliberately left as "not GuardDuty's job."
